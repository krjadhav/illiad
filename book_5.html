The Iliad, retold

Book 5

Book Five, after Samuel Butler’s translation — Diomedes gets one permission slip, and two Olympians go home bleeding.

Every long war produces one day that belongs to a single soldier. Book Five is that day, and it belongs to Diomedes. The Greeks had a word for it — aristeia, the stretch where one man outfights the world — and Homer opens it like a product launch: Athena picks him, sets fire streaming from his shield and helmet “like the star that shines brightest in late summer,” and shoves him into the thickest of the fighting. Worth noticing what being chosen by a god looks like from the outside: indistinguishable from being on fire.

He starts at mortal scale — kills one of two brothers who charge him; the other flees, whisked to safety by a god for their old father’s sake, because in this poem even the mercies come with a reason attached. Meanwhile Athena pulls the day’s first trick: she takes Ares by the hand — “Ares, Ares, bane of men, blood-stained stormer of cities” — and sweetly suggests they both step out and let the mortals settle it themselves. Then she parks the war god on a riverbank like a coat. With Ares benched, the Greek line wins everywhere at once, and Homer’s invoices keep coming: a huntsman Artemis herself had taught (she didn’t save him); a priest of the river; Phereclus the shipwright — the man who built the ships Paris sailed to Sparta, “the beginning of all mischief,” killed for carpentry done ten years ago.

Then Pandarus — the archer who broke the truce in Book Four — puts an arrow through Diomedes’s shoulder and starts celebrating early. Diomedes has Sthenelus pull the arrow out, blood spouting through his shirt, and prays: Athena, if you ever loved my father, put that man in range of my spear. She gives him far more than he asked. Strength in his limbs. The mist lifted from his eyes, so he can tell gods from men on the field. And one precise rule for the day: fight no immortal — except, if Aphrodite enters the battle, stab her.

Pause — check yourself

Athena’s rule: touch no god — except Aphrodite, her you may wound. Why that one exception?

All three sound plausible, and Homer plants evidence for the wrong two: Diomedes does note Aphrodite is “feeble,” and Zeus himself will later say war isn’t her department. But watch whose interests the exception serves. Athena and Aphrodite are rivals from the judgment of Paris; the “tactical advice” happens to arrange, in front of both armies, the beating of Athena’s competitor by Athena’s protégé. When a mentor’s rule contains exactly one exception, ask who benefits from the exception.

He goes back in three times as fierce — Homer’s simile is a lion wounded just badly enough to become a much worse problem — and starts killing in pairs. Two sons of an old dream-interpreter, “who never came back for him to read them any more dreams.” Two sons of an aged man with no other heirs, whose wealth will be carved up by distant kin. Two sons of Priam in one chariot. Whole family trees, pruned in an afternoon, each with its line of accounting.

The Trojan response produces the strangest small scene of the war: Aeneas finds Pandarus, and the two of them stand in the middle of a rout discussing chariot logistics. Pandarus unburdens himself: I’ve hit two of their best — Menelaus, and now this one — drew real blood both times, and only made them angrier. And here’s the thing: I have eleven beautiful chariots at home, fresh from the builder, horses standing by each pair. My old father begged me to bring one. But I worried that in a besieged city the horses wouldn’t get their proper feed — they were used to eating their fill — so I left them and walked here with my bow. The bow. If I ever get home, someone can cut my head off if I don’t snap this thing over my knee and throw it in the fire.

Pause — check yourself

Minutes before his death, Homer has Pandarus explain why he has no chariot: he worried his horses wouldn’t be fed properly in a siege, so he came on foot with the bow. Why give the truce-breaker this speech?

Option b is true as plot — the spear that kills him is literally Athena-guided — and c is a real Homer pattern. But neither explains why the speech is about horse rations. That detail does specific work: the man who broke the world’s truce is dying downstream of a modest, prudent, utterly reasonable logistics call. No hubris, no omen — a barn decision. Homer keeps insisting on this: catastrophe’s paper trail runs through choices too small to notice at the time.

Sthenelus sees the two of them coming — the best archer alive and the son of a goddess — and suggests, reasonably, backing up. Diomedes’s reply defines him: my people don’t retreat, I’m not tired, and — mark this — if I kill them both, jump for Aeneas’s horses. They’re the bloodline Zeus paid Tros for Ganymede, the finest under the sun; Anchises stole the strain in secret. In the middle of an oncoming chariot charge, he’s doing bloodstock arithmetic. Pandarus throws first, punches through the shield, and boasts — “clean through the belly! the glory is mine!” — wrong for the last time. The return spear, Athena guiding, goes in under his eye, through the teeth, out under the chin. He dies mid-boast, killed by the answer to it.

Aeneas does the correct heroic thing — stands over his comrade’s body like a lion — and it nearly ends him. Diomedes picks up a boulder “that two men of our day could not lift,” and crushes his hip socket with it. Darkness. And then his mother comes for him. Aphrodite — on a battlefield — wraps her white arms around her son, folds her shining robe over him against the spears, and carries. Diomedes, permission slip in hand, chases the goddess of love across the field and puts his spear through her wrist. What comes out is ichor — the clear fluid gods bleed instead of blood, Homer explains, because they eat no bread and drink no wine. She screams, drops her son — Apollo catches him — and Diomedes shouts her off the field: isn’t it enough to beguile weak women? If you keep meddling with war, you’ll learn to shudder at the word.

The wounded goddess borrows Ares’s chariot and flies home to cry in her mother’s lap. Dione’s consolation is extraordinary: a casualty list of gods hurt by mortals. Ares once spent thirteen months chained inside a bronze jar. Hera took a three-barbed arrow in the breast. Even Hades took an arrow, at the very gates of hell. Bear it, child; we who dwell on Olympus have to put up with much from men — and inflict much on each other. Then, softly, the threat: men who fight gods don’t live long; no children will prattle at that man’s knee. Upstairs, Athena is openly gloating — the Cyprian must have scratched her hand on a brooch-pin, caressing some Greek girl — and Zeus smiles and hands down the verdict: my child, war is not your department. Attend to your delightful matrimonial duties and leave the fighting to Ares and Athena.

Pause — check yourself

A goddess takes a spear through the wrist, cries in her mother’s lap, gets teased at court, and is healed by a hand-wipe. What is this scene doing in the middle of a battle book?

Dione’s casualty list makes option a tempting, but notice how every item on it ends: healed, freed, fine. That’s the tell. Gods experience the sensation of war without its consequence — ichor instead of blood, Paeeon instead of a pyre, teasing instead of a tomb. Homer runs the same scene at two altitudes all book: upstairs a family comedy, downstairs Pandarus with a spear through his jaw. It’s Book One’s ending again, sharpened: nothing is at stake for immortals, and mortality is what makes anything matter.

Down on the plain, Diomedes is still coming — at Apollo now, who is holding the unconscious Aeneas in his arms. He charges three times; three times the god flicks his shield aside. On the fourth, Apollo drops the pretense and uses the voice: Take heed, son of Tydeus, and draw off. Think not to match yourself against gods; men that walk the earth cannot hold their own with immortals. Diomedes gives ground — “a little space.” He stops exactly at the line, exactly when the line is announced, and not one step before. Meanwhile Apollo spirits the real Aeneas to his own temple in the citadel, where Leto and Artemis heal him — and drops onto the field a wraith, an exact copy of the man, armor and all. For the rest of the afternoon, Trojans and Greeks hack at each other around a fake body.

Pause — check yourself

Apollo swaps a wraith for Aeneas, and both armies spend hours killing each other around a body that isn’t there. What’s Homer showing?

Option b is true — Aeneas has a destiny no spear is allowed to cancel, and ancient readers knew where his line ends up. But that explains the rescue, not the wraith. Apollo could simply have carried him off; instead the poem builds a scene where the fighting’s object is a hollow copy and the fighting proceeds at full intensity anyway. It’s the war in miniature: Book Three already showed every human reason settled and the armies fighting on. Men die around the shape of a cause. Whether anything is inside it turns out not to affect the effort.

Apollo also files a complaint. He fetches Ares off his riverbank — that man would fight Zeus himself now; go deal with him — and the war god enters the Trojan lines wearing a Thracian captain’s face. And here Sarpedon — Zeus’s own mortal son, captain of the Lycians — delivers Hector the harshest performance review in the poem: you used to boast you could hold this city with just your brothers and brothers-in-law. I don’t see one of them. They cower like dogs around a lion while we, your allies, carry your battle. I left a wife and an infant son on the Xanthus and wealth enough to tempt any raider; I have nothing here to lose to the Greeks — and I stand my ground, while you stand there not even exhorting your men. Hector, stung, leaps down and rallies the line — with Ares himself striding beside him, now in front, now behind. Even Diomedes backs up at that sight, and says so out loud: keep your faces to them and give ground; there’s a god on that man; we don’t fight gods.

The middle of the book is the war’s metronome, trading priced lives. Aeneas comes back healed — his comrades overjoyed and too busy to ask a single question, which Homer notes — and kills twin brothers from Phere, raised like two young lions, felled like two pines. Ares baits Menelaus toward him, hoping to see him killed; Antilochus quietly steps up shoulder-to-shoulder and the trap dissolves. A charioteer takes a stone to the elbow, falls headfirst into deep sand, and sticks there — planted, upside down — until his own horses knock him flat. Homer notices the grotesque exactly once, and moves on.

Then the duel of cousins: Tlepolemus, son of Heracles, against Sarpedon, son of Zeus — grandson against son, and they fight the way heroes do, genealogy first: my father sacked this town with six ships; they lie who call you Zeus’s child. Both spears fly at once. Tlepolemus takes Sarpedon’s through the throat. Sarpedon takes his through the thigh, to the bone — “his father as yet warding off destruction.” What follows is the poem at its most unsentimental: his men carry him out in such haste that nobody thinks to pull the spear from his thigh, so it drags. He begs Hector: if I can’t go home to my wife and my infant son, at least let me die inside your city. Hector doesn’t answer. He’s busy. A friend pulls the spear under an oak tree; Sarpedon faints; the north wind revives him. The war’s most honorable ally, and his reward is triage. (Odysseus, denied Sarpedon by fate — Athena turns him aside — settles for seven Lycians of the rank and file.)

By now Ares and Hector are grinding the Greeks backward, and Hera has seen enough. She and Athena mobilize, and Homer lavishes a full paragraph on the hardware: the eight-spoked bronze wheels on the golden chariot, the aegis fringed with Rout — Strife and Strength and Panic on it, and the Gorgon’s head — the golden helmet “decked with the emblems of a hundred cities.” Heaven’s gates groan open on their own. And the permission scene is Zeus in one line. Hera: may I hit Ares and chase him off the field? Zeus: set Athena on him — she punishes him more often than anyone else does. The father of gods, outsourcing the discipline of his war god to his war goddess, on grounds of prior experience.

Hera takes the voice of brazen Stentor — loud as fifty men — and shames the Greek line: while Achilles fought, Troy never dared come outside its gates; now they’re at your ships. Athena finds Diomedes by his chariot, cooling the arrow wound under his shield-strap, and needles him with his father one more time: Tydeus was a small man, but he fought. You must be no son of his. Diomedes answers like a lawyer with a perfect file: I’m not afraid and I’m not tired. I am following your rule — no gods, Aphrodite only. Ares is lording it out there, so I pulled my people back. And Athena, delighted, tears up her own rule on the spot: fear neither Ares nor any other immortal — I am with you. Drive straight at him. She tips Sthenelus out of the chariot, boards it herself — the oak axle groans under goddess and hero together — takes whip and reins, and puts on the helmet of Hades, which makes the wearer invisible even to gods. The morning’s rule was never a law. It was a leash, and she has just moved it.

Ares sees only Diomedes coming and throws first; Athena’s unseen hand flicks the spear over the chariot. Then Diomedes thrusts, and the goddess leans on the shaft and drives it into the pit of the war god’s stomach. Ares roars “as loudly as nine or ten thousand men in the thick of a fight” — both armies shudder — and streaks up to Olympus like a thundercloud, to sit beside his father and complain: your daughter is a menace. All of us gods owe you a grudge for begetting her. She wounded Aphrodite’s wrist, then set that man on me. And Zeus delivers the coldest parenting in literature: don’t come whining, Sir Facing-both-ways. I hate you worst of all the gods in Olympus — you have your mother’s intolerable temper. But you are my own offspring; I can’t leave you in pain. If you’d been any other god’s son, you’d have been lying lower than the Titans long ago. Paeeon spreads herbs on the wound and it closes as fast as fig juice curdles milk; Hebe bathes him; he takes his seat beside his father “all glorious to behold.” One mortal put two Olympians in the infirmary in a single afternoon, and by dinner, upstairs, it is all smoothed over. Downstairs, the bodies keep their names.

Last one

Zeus, to his bleeding son: “I hate you worst of all the gods — but you are mine, and I can’t leave you in pain.” Why does the book of wounded gods end on this line?

Option a flatters modern taste, and there’s real revulsion in Zeus’s speech — but hatred that ends in healing and a seat at the table isn’t rejection. That’s the point of running the whole book’s machinery into this one exchange: Ares is loathed by his own father, chained in jars, speared by mortals — and always patched up, always restored, because he’s kin. War doesn’t persist in this poem because anyone loves it. It persists because the powers that could end it keep tucking it back into bed. The comedy (option c) is real, but it’s the sugar on exactly this pill.

TLDR

Diomedes has his great day of slaughter under Athena's protection. He kills Pandarus, wounds Aeneas, drives Aphrodite and Ares bleeding from the field, and shows how thin the line is between heroic courage and divine permission.

Who’s who

Diomedes

Son of Tydeus, on fire

Wounded early, upgraded by Athena, and for one day the most dangerous being on the field — man or god. Two Olympians go home bleeding. Butler calls him Diomed.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

She grants Diomedes god-sight and one narrow permission — Aphrodite only — then widens it in person: she boards his chariot, dons Hades’ helmet of invisibility, and steers the spear into Ares.

Ares

God of war

He backs Troy wearing a mortal’s face, takes Diomedes’ spear in the belly, roars like ten thousand men, and flies home to complain. His father’s sympathy is limited.

Aphrodite

Goddess of love

She wades into a battlefield to carry her son out of it and takes a spear through the wrist — ichor, not blood. Her mother comforts her; Zeus tells her to stick to weddings.

Apollo

God of archery

He blocks Diomedes three times and warns him off on the fourth — men cannot hold their own with immortals — then swaps a wraith for Aeneas and calls in Ares.

Pandarus

The archer, out of luck

The truce-breaker of Book Four, now full of doubts about his bow. He left eleven good chariots at home to spare the horses’ rations, fights on foot, and dies mid-boast.

Aeneas

Prince of Troy, Aphrodite’s son

Troy’s second-best soldier. A boulder crushes his hip; his mother, then Apollo, spend the afternoon keeping him alive. Rome will thank them.

Hera

Queen of the gods

She watches Ares wreck the Greek line and goes to Zeus for leave to intervene. Granted — with the note that Athena punishes him best.

Zeus

King of the gods

He lets the goddesses off the leash, and delivers the family verdict to his bleeding son: I hate you worst of all gods — but you are mine.

Sarpedon

Captain of the Lycians, Zeus’s son

Zeus’s mortal son. He shames Hector into rallying, kills Tlepolemus, and takes a spear through the thigh — his father’s protection, for now, holds.

Hector

Commander of Troy

Stung by Sarpedon’s rebuke, he dismounts and rallies Troy with Ares himself at his shoulder — the one sight that makes even Diomedes back up.

Dione

Aphrodite’s mother

She heals the wounded wrist and consoles her daughter with the gods’ own casualty list: Ares chained in a jar, Hera and Hades shot. Mortals have always hurt gods who meddle.

Sthenelus

Diomedes’ charioteer

Diomedes’ friend and driver. He pulls the arrow from the wound, steals Aeneas’ famous horses on order, and gets tipped off the chariot when a goddess wants his seat.

Tlepolemus

Son of Heracles

He trades genealogical insults and simultaneous spears with Sarpedon — grandson of Zeus against son of Zeus — and loses by a throat.

Odysseus

King of Ithaca

Denied Sarpedon by fate — Athena turns him aside — he settles for seven Lycians of the rank and file. Butler calls him Ulysses.

Menelaus

King of Sparta

Ares baits him toward Aeneas hoping to see him killed; Antilochus quietly steps up beside him, and the trap dissolves.

Achilles

The absent measure

Still out of the war, still the yardstick: Hera’s rallying cry is that Troy never dared leave its gates while he fought.

Priam

King of Troy

King of Troy. Two more of his sons fall from one chariot to Diomedes’ afternoon — the family ledger keeps thinning.
