The Iliad, retold

Book 20

Book Twenty, after Samuel Butler’s translation — Zeus opens the gates for every god in heaven, an enemy rescues Aeneas, and Achilles stops being a man and becomes the weather.

Book Twenty opens with the largest meeting in the poem. Zeus sends Themis out to summon the gods, and Homer tells you who came by telling you who didn’t: not a river was absent except Oceanus, not one of the nymphs that haunt the groves and the springs and the meadows of green grass. Everyone with power over anything, down to the naiad of the smallest creek, files into the marble arcades Hephaestus built and takes a seat. When the boss calls a meeting of literally everyone — the interns included — you know the announcement is structural. Poseidon comes up out of the sea and asks the question on the agenda: why are we here?

The answer is a policy reversal. Since Book Eight the gods have been banned from the field on pain of Zeus’s lightning. Now: “go about among Trojans and Achaeans, and help either side as you may be severally disposed.” He himself will stay on Olympus and “look on in peace.” And then, in the same breath, the actual reason, and it is not generosity: if Achilles fights the Trojans without hindrance, they will not stand against him for an hour — and roused as he is about his dead friend, “he will override fate itself and storm their city.” Read that twice. Zeus is not freeing the gods; he is deploying them. Troy is scheduled to fall, but not today, and the one man on the field is about to beat the schedule. So the regulator floods the market. When you cannot restrain your best actor directly, you let everyone else in and let friction do it for you.

Pause — check yourself

Zeus has kept the gods off the field since Book Eight. Now he summons all of them — rivers and nymphs included — and tells them to intervene freely, on either side. What is the lifted ban actually for?

The spectacle reading quotes him accurately — he really does plan to watch, and plainly enjoys it. But he states his mechanism in the same sentence: fighting “without hindrance,” Achilles “will override fate itself and storm their city.” The ban isn’t lifted to free the gods; it’s lifted to add friction around one actor. Zeus doesn’t deregulate because he trusts the market — he deregulates to flood it, so that no single participant, even the best one, can move the price. Fate has a delivery date for Troy, and Zeus’s whole job in this poem is schedule protection.

The descent is staged like a natural catastrophe. Hera, Athena, Poseidon, Hermes, and limping Hephaestus go to the ships; Ares, Apollo, Artemis, Leto, the river Xanthus, and Aphrodite go to Troy. Strife rises. Athena screams from the trench and then from the shore; Ares bellows back “dark as some black thunder-cloud,” now from the acropolis, now from the hill Callicolone. Zeus thunders overhead while Poseidon shakes the earth from below until the spurs of Ida quake — and down in the underworld Hades leaps from his throne and cries out, terrified the earth-shaker will crack the ground open and expose his mouldy mansions, “so ghastly grim that even the gods shudder.” That detail is the whole escalation in one image: the panic has reached the one stakeholder with nothing left to lose.

Then Homer publishes the bracket. Apollo takes his stand against Poseidon; Athena against Ares; Artemis of the golden arrows against Hera; Hermes against Leto; the river men call Scamander and gods call Xanthus against Hephaestus. A full card of divine single combats, announced pair by pair like a tournament sheet. Hold on to that page, because what happens next is: nothing.

First, though, Apollo goes recruiting. Wearing the voice of Lycaon, one of Priam’s sons, he needles Aeneas: where are the brave words you vaunted over your wine, about meeting Achilles in single combat? The answer he gets is the most clear-eyed risk assessment in the poem. I have met this man before, says Aeneas — he chased me off Ida when he raided our cattle, he sacked Lyrnessus; I survived by running. No man may fight Achilles, because a god always walks in front of him, and even without that, his spear flies straight until it finds flesh. This is not cowardice; it is underwriting. So Apollo switches to the one argument that works on an aristocrat: rank. You are the son of Aphrodite, a daughter of Zeus; Achilles comes of Thetis, a mere daughter of the old man of the sea. Your mother outranks his. And on that — a protocol point about mothers — Aeneas puts on his armor and walks into the front ranks to die.

Hera spots him coming and convenes an emergency session: shall we turn him back, or shall one of us stand at Achilles’ shoulder so his heart does not fail? It is Poseidon who talks her down, and his reasoning deserves a plaque: “restrain your fury; it is not well … the advantage is too greatly on our own side.” Let the mortals fight it out; we move only if Ares or Apollo moves first. So the Greek-side gods seat themselves on the old barrow of Heracles, wrapped in a cloud, the Trojan-side gods settle on the brow of Callicolone, and “neither side was willing to begin battle,” while Zeus sits above them all, in command. The great war of the gods, announced pair by pair, becomes two hills of spectators. Homer got great-power conflict exactly right three thousand years early: the matchups are declared, the high ground is held, deterrence holds — and the bleeding is outsourced to the people on the plain.

Pause — check yourself

The gods pair off by name for war — Apollo against Poseidon, Athena against Ares, Hera against Artemis — and then both sides sit down on hills to watch. Why does Homer stage a battle of gods that doesn’t happen?

The setup reading has the facts on its side — these matchups do come due in Book 21 — but it treats the sitting-down as filler, and Homer gives the sitting-down a speech. Poseidon declines to fight for a stated reason: “the advantage is too greatly on our own side”; move only if the other side moves first. That’s not suspense management, it’s doctrine — the stronger power gains nothing from an open exchange and everything from visible readiness. So both alliances occupy high ground, wrapped in cloud, watching mortals do the actual dying. Every great-power standoff since has looked like this: the pairings announced, the hills held, the fighting outsourced.

Down on that plain, the two champions meet in the open ground between the armies. Aeneas comes on first, helmet tossing defiance. Achilles springs to meet him like Homer’s lion — the one a whole countryside has turned out to hunt, who ignores the crowd until a spear stings him, then crouches with foaming jaws, lashes his own ribs with his tail, and comes straight on to kill or be killed. Note whose simile that is. The cornered animal, wounded and past caring, is the Greek.

Then, being Homeric heroes, they talk. Achilles’ taunt is worth slowing down for, because of what he chooses to attack — not Aeneas’ strength, his prospects. Why stand out before the host against me? Do you hope to reign over the Trojans in Priam’s seat? Even if you kill me, Priam will not hand you the kingdom; he is a man of sound judgment, and he has sons of his own. Or have they promised you a rich estate, orchards and corn land, if you bring me down? I have hunted you once already, helter-skelter down the slopes of Ida, and you never once looked back. Go back into the crowd before you rue it: “even a fool may be wise after the event.”

Aeneas’ reply is one of the strangest set pieces in the Iliad. Do not talk to me as though I were a child, he says — we both know each other’s parentage by common fame. And then he recites it anyway, all of it: Dardanus, son of Zeus, who founded Dardania before Ilius ever stood on the plain; Erichthonius, richest of living men, whose three thousand mares so entranced the north wind that Boreas covered them in the shape of a dark-maned stallion, siring foals that could run across a field of ripe corn without breaking an ear; Tros; Ilus; Assaracus; Ganymede, so beautiful the gods carried him up to pour Zeus’s wine; and so on down both branches to “Anchises, who was my father, while Hector is son to Priam.” Sixty lines of begats, delivered at spear’s length from the most dangerous man alive — and then Aeneas dismisses the whole genre in the next breath: enough prating in mid-battle like children; we could fling taunts a hundred-oared galley would not hold; the tongue can run all whithers. “Let us make trial of one another with our spears.”

Pause — check yourself

Aeneas, facing the most lethal man alive, spends his last calm minute reciting his family tree back to Zeus — then dismisses the whole exchange as childish prating. What was the genealogy for?

Menace is what Apollo just modeled — he talked Aeneas into this duel with a rank argument about mothers — so it’s the natural guess. But watch Achilles’ opening taunt: it isn’t about strength, it’s about succession — do you hope to reign in Priam’s seat? Priam has sons. The duel is framed as inheritance law from the first word, and Aeneas answers in kind: here is the line, Dardanus down to Anchises, that my death would cut. He can wave it off as prating precisely because the recital wasn’t persuasion; it was the record. In this world you are your lineage, so before a lineage is put at risk it gets stated in full — the way a man reads his will aloud.

The exchange itself takes about four seconds. Aeneas’ spear crashes into the god-forged shield — and Achilles is frightened, Homer says so plainly, sure the point will come through, “not reflecting that the god’s glorious gifts were little likely to yield before the blows of mortal men.” Even Achilles does not entirely believe in his own equipment. The spear beats two layers and dies in the gold. His answering throw tears clean through the rim of Aeneas’ shield and passes over his crouching back, close enough that he stands afterward “blinded with fear and grief.” Then everything happens at once: Achilles comes on with drawn sword and a cry “as of death,” Aeneas heaves up a boulder that two men of our day could not lift, and the duel is one heartbeat from its verdict.

And a Greek god stops the clock. Poseidon — nine years of earthquakes on the Achaean side — looks down and pities the Trojan. Listen to the order of his reasons. First the sympathy: the fool listened to Apollo, who will never lift a finger to save him; he is guiltless, suffering “in another’s quarrel”; he has always paid his sacrifices. Then the clause that actually moves heaven: it is fated that he should escape — that the race of Dardanus, whom Zeus loved above all his mortal sons, not perish without seed or sign. Zeus has come to hate Priam’s blood, but “Aeneas shall reign over the Trojans, he and his children’s children that shall be born hereafter.” Hera’s answer is pure compliance office: do as you like, but I and Athena have sworn, many times, before all the immortals, never to shield a Trojan from destruction — not even when all Troy is burning. She will not veto the rescue. She just wants the record to show her hands stayed clean.

So Poseidon does it himself, and the mechanics are wonderful. He walks into the spear-clash, pours darkness over Achilles’ eyes, plucks the ash spear out of Aeneas’ shield and lays it politely at its owner’s feet, then lifts Aeneas off the earth and throws him — over whole squadrons of horses and men — to the far fringe of the battle. The advice he leaves him with is a schedule: give way before this man while he lives; once Achilles is dead, fight among the foremost, “for none other of the Achaeans shall slay you.” This is the only moment the Iliad ever looks past the fall of Troy, and what it sees is a dynasty held in escrow — preserved against the wishes of both armies, by a god from the wrong side, because the institution’s continuity outranks everybody’s team. A thousand years on, Rome would build its founding epic on those two lines. Homer has no idea, and doesn’t need one: he has already written down the principle, which is that fate keeps accounts no loyalty can touch.

Pause — check yourself

The boulder is up; Achilles is closing with drawn sword. And the god who saves Aeneas is Poseidon — nine years a Greek partisan. Why him, and why at all?

The piety line really is in his speech, which is what makes it the trap — but it’s the sympathy, not the trigger. The operative clauses come after: “it is fated that he should escape,” that the race of Dardanus “shall not perish utterly,” that Aeneas and his children’s children “shall reign over the Trojans.” Troy’s account is closed — Zeus now hates the blood of Priam — but one Trojan line is held in escrow for a future the poem never shows, and the trustee is whichever god stands closest, allegiance irrelevant. Hera, who has sworn oaths against Troy, simply recuses herself. Team loyalty runs deep in this poem; institutional continuity runs deeper.

The mist lifts. Achilles blinks at his spear lying on the ground with no corpse attached, and files the incident with a shrug that is almost funny: the man must really be under heaven’s protection — “let him go hang.” Then he turns to the army and says the honest thing: I cannot chase all of them and fight them all; even Ares or Athena would shrink from the jaws of a fight this size; but whatever hand and foot can do, I will do. Across the field Hector is giving the mirror speech — I will go against him “though his hands be fire and his strength iron” — when Apollo appears at his elbow with instructions that unmake it: on no account meet Achilles in single combat. Stay in the crowd. Keep a lookout. And Hector, afraid, obeys. Both champions have now been throttled back by their own patrons. The gods lifted the ban on themselves and immediately imposed one on the men.

What follows is the reason the ban mattered. Achilles goes into the Trojans “clothed in valour as with a garment,” and Homer runs the ledger: Iphition, whose head is split clean in two — and who still gets his one-line obituary, the naiad mother, the estate by the fish-rich lake; Demoleon, through the temple, the helmet no help at all; Hippodamas, speared as he leaps from his chariot, bellowing his life out like a bull dragged to sacrifice. And then the entry the book has been building to. Polydorus, Priam’s youngest — the son his father loved best and had forbidden to fight, the fastest runner in the family — is out in the front ranks anyway, showing off his speed, when Achilles’ spear takes him in the back at the golden clasp of his belt and comes out at the navel. He goes down on his knees holding his own entrails. The boy wasn’t fighting. He was running past.

That is what breaks Hector’s discipline. He sees his little brother sinking with his entrails in his hands, a mist comes over his eyes, and the god’s instructions burn off like fog: he goes at Achilles “like a flame of fire.” Achilles sees the one target he actually wants and exults — here is the man who slew my beloved comrade; “draw near, that you may meet your doom the sooner.” Hector’s reply borrows Aeneas’ opening word for word — do not think your words can scare me like a child — and adds the only theology available to the weaker man: the issue lies in the lap of heaven, and even a worse man’s spear has been found keen before now. Then he throws, true — and Athena breathes on the shaft, breathes “but very lightly,” and it drifts back through the air and lies at Hector’s own feet. Achilles springs with a cry; Apollo snatches Hector up and hides him in thick darkness; three times Achilles lunges into the fog and hits air, and on the fourth he screams the sentence the whole poem is waiting on: hound, this time too you have escaped death — but if I have any friend among the gods, “I will surely make an end of you when I come across you at some other time.” That is not a threat. It is an appointment, and Book Twenty-Two will keep it.

Denied the one death he wants, Achilles takes every other death on offer, and the poem’s camera pulls back until he stops being a character at all. Dryops, through the neck. Demouchus, brought down at the knee, then finished. Laogonus and Dardanus, swept off one chariot. Tros, son of Alastor, who tries the thing that has worked in this poem before — clasps Achilles’ knees, same age, surely worth sparing — and takes the sword in his liver mid-plea, “for the man was in no mood for pity or forbearance, but was in grim earnest.” Mulius, in one ear and out the other. Echeclus. Deucalion, beheaded so cleanly the marrow wells from his spine. Rhigmus of Thrace, and his squire for turning the horses too slowly. Then Homer gives up on human comparison altogether: as a fire rages through the glens of a parched mountain forest, the wind carrying great tongues of flame in every direction, “even so furiously did Achilles rage, wielding his spear as though he were a god … till the dark earth ran with blood.” His horses trample the dead the way oxen tread barley on a threshing floor; the axle of his chariot is sprayed with clots of blood; “his hands were bedrabbled with gore.” Fire, then harvest. In Book Nine this man made the noblest argument in the poem — that all the wealth of Troy is not worth one life. The argument is over. The gods spent the whole book saving people from him one at a time, which is how you fight a fire. Nobody argues with one.

Last one

The book ends in similes: Achilles as fire in a drought-parched forest, his horses as oxen treading barley, the dark earth running with blood. What have the comparisons stopped doing?

The always-this reading tempts because Book One promised wrath from its first word — but the man of Book Nine argued, at length and brilliantly, that no pile of gifts equals a single life. He had positions; he took inputs. Watch what fails now: Tros clasps his knees — the gesture that opens every ransom negotiation in the poem — and the sword goes into his liver mid-plea, because Achilles “was in no mood for pity or forbearance.” A man can be argued with. Fire in a dry forest cannot; you don’t negotiate with it, you count what it burns. That is the terrible precision of the closing similes — not that Achilles fights like a force of nature, but that a force of nature is what grief has left where the arguer used to be.

TLDR

Zeus releases the gods back onto the battlefield because Achilles is too dangerous unchecked. Poseidon rescues Aeneas, Apollo protects Hector, and Achilles turns the Trojan rout into a killing storm.

Who’s who

Achilles

Grief, weaponized

He wants Hector and keeps being handed substitutes. Robbed of Aeneas by one god and of Hector by another, he ends the book as the similes say: fire in a parched forest, and the dark earth running with blood.

Aeneas

Prince of the Dardanian line

Talked into the duel by a god quoting his pedigree, he answers Achilles with a family tree sixty lines deep — and survives because fate needs him: his children’s children are to reign over the Trojans.

Hector

Commander of Troy

Twice pulled off course: Apollo orders him back into the crowd, and when his brother’s death drags him out anyway, his spear floats back to his feet and the mist swallows him. The appointment is only postponed.

Zeus

King of the gods

He gathers every god, river, and nymph, lifts his own ban, and stays home to watch. His stated reason: left unchecked, Achilles will override fate itself and take Troy today.

Poseidon

Earth-shaker, Greek partisan

He counsels the gods to sit and watch — then breaks his own rule to rescue a Trojan. Aeneas’s line is fated to outlive Troy, and fate’s escrow outranks his rooting interest.

Apollo

God of the bow, Trojan partisan

The book’s busiest god: he goads Aeneas into the duel wearing a borrowed voice, abandons him there, and saves Hector twice — once with a warning, once with mist.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

One intervention, one breath: Hector’s spear, thrown true at Achilles, drifts back through the air and lies at its owner’s feet.

Hera

Queen of the gods

She wants a god posted at Achilles’ shoulder, but when a Trojan needs rescuing she recuses herself: she has sworn, many times, never to shield Troy from ruin. Let Poseidon decide.

Ares

God of war

He bellows from the acropolis dark as a thundercloud and draws Athena as his assigned opponent — then spends the battle of gods sitting on a hill.

Aphrodite

Goddess of love

Aeneas’s mother, and Apollo’s whole argument: she is a daughter of Zeus, while Achilles’ mother is only a daughter of the old man of the sea. Aeneas nearly dies of the comparison.

Artemis

Archer goddess

Apollo’s sister, assigned to face Hera when the gods pair off. Like almost everyone on the card, she never takes her shot in this book.

Polydorus

Priam’s youngest son

Forbidden to fight because his father loved him best and he ran fastest. He was out front showing off his speed when Achilles’ spear caught him at the golden clasp of his belt.

Priam

King of Troy

Offstage and already condemned: Zeus, says Poseidon, has come to hate the blood of Priam. His best-loved, fastest son does not survive the afternoon.
