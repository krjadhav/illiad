The Iliad, retold

Book 19

Book Nineteen, after Samuel Butler’s translation — a feud ends because its object stopped mattering, a king blames the goddess of delusion, and a horse tells Achilles how he dies.

Book Nineteen opens with a delivery. Dawn is coming up over the ships when Thetis arrives with the armor Hephaestus forged overnight, and finds her son where she left him: on the ground, wrapped around the body of Patroclus, weeping, his men weeping around him. She sets the armor down and it “rang out bravely” as she did so — divine metal announces itself. Then Homer does a precise thing with the crowd. The Myrmidons are struck with awe; none of them dares look straight at it; they are afraid. Achilles looks, and his eyes gleam “with a fierce light,” and handling the splendid present makes him glad. Same object, two readings. The armor is a test of what is inside the viewer, and what is inside Achilles now is something none of the others have: grief that has finished converting into fury.

His first words about the gift are practical, which tells you exactly where he is. Fine armor, he says — the handiwork of a god, obviously. I will arm now. But I am afraid flies will settle on Patroclus and breed worms in his wounds, and the flesh will rot. Grief at this stage is mostly logistics: the mourner’s mind snags on the body’s maintenance because that is the only part of the disaster still open to action. Thetis takes the one problem she can solve — she drops ambrosia and red nectar into the wounds, and promises the flesh will keep for a year, “or even sounder” — and then hands him his agenda in a single breath: call the assembly, unsay your anger against Agamemnon, arm, and fight. Unsay. As if the feud were a sentence that could be taken back.

The assembly is a census of how much this moment matters. Achilles walks the shore shouting, and everyone comes — not just the fighters but the pilots and helmsmen and the stewards who serve out rations, men who never leave the ships, because Achilles has shown himself after so long. Odysseus and the son of Tydeus limp in on their spear wounds and take seats in the front row. Agamemnon comes last, also wounded. The three most senior Greeks in the room are all damaged goods; the ninth year has been collecting from everybody.

Then Achilles stands up and ends the feud, and it takes him about three sentences. Was it better for either of us, he asks Agamemnon, that we raged over a girl? I wish Artemis’ arrow had killed her at the ships the day I took her — fewer Achaeans would have bitten the dust while I sulked. “If we have been angry, necessity has schooled our anger. I put it from me: I dare not nurse it for ever.” Now arm the men and let me at the Trojans. That is the whole speech, and its coldest line is the wish that Briseis — a person, sitting at that moment in Agamemnon’s tents — had died on schedule to spare everyone the inconvenience. Notice what did not happen: nobody conceded anything, nobody was persuaded, not one term of the dispute got settled. The quarrel was not resolved. It was obsoleted. His anger has not shrunk; it has moved — and honor measured in prizes, the currency the whole fight was denominated in, is worthless to a man who now wants exactly one thing, and it is not payable in tripods.

Pause — check yourself

The feud that consumed eighteen books ends in three sentences, and the terms of the dispute are never even mentioned. What actually ended it?

Option a is the redemption arc readers want, and it’s the best trap. But listen to the speech: he wishes Briseis had died so the fight would never have happened, and he demands battle in the same breath. His anger hasn’t been renounced; it has been re-aimed at Hector, at a higher setting. Option c quotes him, but “necessity” here isn’t war-weariness — it’s one specific corpse. Nobody concedes, nobody persuades, no term is settled. The feud ends because the thing it was denominated in — honor paid in prizes — stopped being worth anything to one of the parties. Quarrels rarely get resolved. They get obsoleted.

Agamemnon’s reply is everything that speech was not: long, careful, and delivered sitting down — Homer notes that he speaks from his place and does not walk to the middle of the assembly. He opens with a complaint about heckling — it is hard even for a practised speaker when there is an uproar — which is what a man says when he knows how his next sentence is going to land. Then the defense: “it was not I that did it: Zeus, and Fate, and the Erinys that walks in darkness struck me mad” on the day he took the prize. What could I do? All things are in the hand of heaven, and Delusion — Ate, the eldest daughter of Zeus — shuts men’s eyes to their destruction. She walks delicately, he explains, not on the solid earth, but hovers over the heads of men to make them stumble. It is a genuinely beautiful piece of theology, deployed as an alibi.

And because a defense needs precedent, he tells a story. Delusion once fooled Zeus himself. On the day Heracles was to be born, Zeus boasted that a man of his lineage born that day would rule all who lived around him — and Hera, “all crafty and full of guile,” extracted an oath making the boast binding, then held back Alcmena’s labor and rushed a lesser child, Eurystheus, into the world two months early. The oath held. The greatest of gods watched his greatest son bound over to serve a mediocrity, and in his rage he seized Delusion by the hair, swore she would never set foot in heaven again, and flung her down among mortals. Even so was I blinded, says the king. Watch what the story buys: if Ate could blind Zeus, then being blinded proves nothing against the blinded — the defendant stands in the best possible company. It is the second non-apology of the poem, and this one comes with divine precedent entered as an exhibit.

The comparison that matters is with the first one. Back in Book Two, in a small council where nothing was at stake, Agamemnon said plainly, I was first to offend. Confession was free that day, so he spent it. Today, in front of the whole army, with the cost of the feud lying mangled on the plain, it is not I that did it — Zeus did, and Fate, and the Erinys — followed, in the same breath, by an offer to pay the entire bill: everything Odysseus listed in the failed embassy, brought out for inspection. He denies the agency and pays the damages, which tells you the payment is not about guilt; it is about closing the account. Achilles barely lets him finish. Give the gifts if you like, he says, or keep them: “it is in your own hands.” The most insulting acceptance possible — indifference — and then: enough talk, there is a deed yet to do.

Pause — check yourself

Agamemnon’s apology: “it was not I that did it” — Zeus, Fate, and the Erinys struck him mad, and Delusion once fooled Zeus himself. What is the Heracles story doing in the middle of it?

Option b is the strong distractor, because the theology is real: gods in this poem do reach into heads, and even Achilles will say so before the morning is out. But watch the accounting, not the metaphysics. Agamemnon denies agency and pays full damages in the same breath — “not I,” followed by treasure — and compare Book Two, where he said “I was first to offend” freely, in a small council, when confession cost nothing. What varies isn’t his belief about Ate; it’s the size of the audience and the bill. That’s what the Heracles story is for: an exhibit entered into the record, proving the defendant was deceived in the best possible company.

Odysseus — wounded, limping, and the only man present thinking past noon — takes over the meeting. Two rulings. First, the army eats: you cannot send men against Ilius fasting, because the battle will be long, and courage runs on bread and wine; however much a man’s spirit wants to fight, “his strength will fail him before he knows it.” Second, and stranger: the settlement must be performed. Let Agamemnon bring the gifts “in presence of the assembly, that all may see them,” and let him stand and swear publicly that he never touched Briseis. And then the only sentence of genuine accountability anyone offers all morning — aimed at the king: treat people more righteously in future; “it is no disgrace even to a king that he should make amends if he was wrong in the first instance.”

Achilles fights the first ruling. How can men sit down to food while the dead lie mangled on the plain? Let them fight fasting and eat at sundown with the ships avenged; as for him, he can think of nothing “save only slaughter and blood and the rattle in the throat of the dying.” Odysseus overrules him with the veteran’s arithmetic: men fall “thick and threefold” every single day — if the living fasted for the dead, “when should we have respite from our sorrow?” Mourn for a day, bury them, and then those left must eat, so they can fight harder. It may not be, he says, that the Achaeans mourn the dead with their bellies. It sounds heartless and is the opposite: it is what care for the living looks like when the dying is not going to stop.

So the ceremony happens, over the objection of one principal and the indifference of the other. Seven tripods, twenty cauldrons, twelve horses, seven women skilled in useful arts and Briseis the eighth, ten talents of gold weighed out by Odysseus himself — all of it carried through the camp and laid down in the middle of the assembly, where every man can count it. Talthybius holds the boar; Agamemnon cuts bristles from it, lifts his hands, and swears by Zeus, Earth, Sun, and the Furies below that he never laid a hand on the girl — then cuts its throat, and the carcass is whirled around and flung into the sea for the fish. And Achilles rises and closes the proceedings by adopting, wholesale, the other man’s theory: “Father Zeus, of a truth you blind men’s eyes and bane them,” else Agamemnon could never have stirred me so. It is the only frame that lets both men keep standing, so both men stand in it. The feud began as public theater in Book One; Odysseus understood it could only end the same way.

Pause — check yourself

Achilles says keep the gifts or give them, he doesn’t care; Agamemnon would happily settle quietly. Odysseus insists the treasure be paraded through the assembly and the oath sworn over a boar. Why force a ceremony neither principal wants?

Option b is the tempting one because Odysseus really does make the meal argument — but that explains the delay, not the display. He specifies the point himself: “in presence of the assembly, that all may see them.” The quarrel was a public event in Book One — the army watched their best weapon walk off the job — so a private thaw between two moods can’t close it; moods reopen. Institutions can’t read hearts, only ceremonies, which is why the goods get counted and the oath gets a carcass thrown in the sea. Briseis’ restoration (option a) is a real side effect, but no one in the scene treats her as its audience — the oath is sworn to the army, not to her.

The gifts are carried to Achilles’ ships, and with them walks the woman the war stopped for. Briseis sees the mangled body of Patroclus, throws herself on it, and tears her breast and neck and face with both hands — and then she speaks, for the first and only time in the poem. The prize has a voice, and this is what it says: I left you living, I come back to find you dead; so sorrow follows sorrow for me. She counts her losses like a woman who has had practice: the husband her father and mother gave her, cut down before their city; her three brothers, all on the same day; the city itself sacked — by Achilles, the man she was then handed to. And through all of it, Patroclus told her not to weep. He promised to make her Achilles’ wife, to carry her to Phthia, to give her a wedding feast among the Myrmidons. “You were always kind to me and I shall never cease to grieve for you.” Kindness so scarce that one man’s ration of it fills a eulogy.

Then Homer appends one of his acid codas: the other women take up the lament, weeping “as though their tears were for Patroclus, but in truth each was weeping for her own sorrows.” That line is usually read as deflation, and it is the opposite. Every captive in that camp carries a ledger like Briseis’ — a husband somewhere, brothers, a burned city — and a funeral is the only licensed occasion for opening it. The poem has spent eighteen books watching kings price this woman in tripods and in oaths about her bed; it never once showed anyone asking her anything. Now it lets her talk, and the single humane entry in her whole account belongs to the man in the shroud. Nobody is accused by name. Everybody is implicated.

Pause — check yourself

Briseis has been the object of the poem’s central quarrel since Book One. Her only speech turns out to be a lament for Patroclus — not a word about the kings who traded her. What is the poem doing?

Option c quotes the text, which makes it the best trap — but the coda widens the charge instead of dissolving it: a camp where every captive needs a funeral as cover to mourn her own losses is a camp full of Briseis-sized ledgers, which is the indictment restated at scale. And the speech can’t be aimed at Achilles (option a): he isn’t addressed, isn’t present in it, and never reacts. What Homer does is colder than reproach. After eighteen books of men trading this woman, he lets her speak once, about her actual life — a husband, three brothers, a city, all dead — and lets you notice that the only person who ever treated her as a future wife rather than a prize is the corpse she’s holding.

The camp goes to its dinner. Achilles will not. The elders stay behind — the sons of Atreus, Odysseus, Nestor, Idomeneus, old Phoenix — and beg him to eat, and he refuses: grief greater than this he could not know, he says, not even at word of his father’s death, or his son’s. You were the one, he tells the body, who would have a good dinner ready when the army hurried to fight; now the tents are full of meat and drink and I want none of it. Zeus watches from above and takes a kind of executive pity: he sends Athena darting down like a falcon to drop nectar and ambrosia into Achilles’ chest so hunger will not buckle his knees — secretly, without his knowledge or consent. He wins the argument about fasting, and heaven quietly falsifies the result. The gods feed the hero the way you medicate a child: his reasons are real, and they cannot be allowed to matter.

Then the arming, and Homer opens the throttle. Armor pours from the ships thick as snowflakes on a north wind, and the sheen of it “pierced the sky.” In the middle of it all Achilles puts on the god’s gift, gnashing his teeth, eyes glowing like fire: the greaves, the breastplate, the silver-studded sword, the great shield throwing light like the moon. For that shield Homer reaches for the loneliest image of light he has: a fire burning in a homestead high in the hills, seen by sailors as a storm carries them out to sea, away from the haven and the people they wanted. That is what the most beautiful object in the world looks like from where its owner stands. The helmet shines like a star; the armor, tested, lifts him “as though it had been wings”; and out of the spear-stand comes the Pelian ash spear no other Achaean can even wield, Chiron’s gift to his father, “fraught with the death of heroes.” Automedon harnesses the horses, and Achilles mounts behind them, resplendent as the sun.

Then he does something strange: he scolds his horses. Xanthus and Balius, famed offspring of Podarge — this time, when the fighting is done, bring your driver back alive; do not leave him dead on the plain the way you left Patroclus. And Xanthus answers him. Hera gives the horse a human voice, and he bows his head until his mane touches the ground: we will save you today, dread Achilles, but the day of your death is near, and the fault will not be ours — heaven and stern fate will destroy you. It was no slackness of ours that killed Patroclus; a god struck him down and handed Hector the glory. We can run with the west wind, fleetest of all winds — and it will not matter: “it is your doom to fall by the hand of a man and of a god.” Then the Erinyes stop his voice. Fate borrowed a mouth, said exactly as much as it meant to say, and hung up.

Achilles’ answer is the reason the scene exists. “Why, O Xanthus, do you thus foretell my death? You need not do so, for I well know that I am to fall here, far from my dear father and mother; none the more, however, shall I stay my hand till I have given the Trojans their fill of fighting.” Every prophecy in this poem so far has been dodged, bargained with, or shouted down. This is the first time a man hears his own death read out and simply agrees to the terms — corrects the messenger, in fact, for telling him something he already knew. Then, with a loud cry, he drives his horses to the front. The book ends on a man accelerating toward the thing he has just co-signed.

Last one

Hera gives Xanthus a voice, the horse announces his master’s coming death, and Achilles answers that he knows it well — and drives forward. What does the scene actually deliver?

Option c is the sly one, because the speech genuinely is half self-defense — “the blame will not be ours,” it was no slackness of ours that lost Patroclus. But a defense brief doesn’t need the Erinyes to cut it off mid-sentence; fate polices the speech because the speech is fate’s, delivered through a borrowed mouth. And option b fails on Achilles’ reply: notice is only owed to someone who hasn’t been told, and his first words are that the warning was unnecessary. No grief, no bargaining — a correction. Every hero before him treated prophecy as something to negotiate. Achilles hears his death announced, agrees with it, and accelerates — which is exactly why the book can end there.

TLDR

Achilles and Agamemnon formally reconcile, but neither quite owns the original damage. Odysseus forces the public ritual and the meal, Briseis mourns Patroclus, and Achilles arms while his horse foretells death.

Who’s who

Achilles

Returned to the war

He renounces the feud in three sentences and asks for battle before breakfast. The new armor fits him like wings, his eyes flash when he handles it — and when his own horse foretells his death, he answers that he already knew.

Agamemnon

Commander-in-chief of the Greek army

His apology, delivered from his seat: it was not I that did it — Zeus, Fate, and the Erinys struck me mad, and Delusion once fooled Zeus himself. He pays the full bill anyway, while denying he ever ran it up.

Odysseus

The procedural mind

Wounded and limping, he still runs the meeting: the men must eat, the gifts must be paraded before the whole assembly, the oath must be sworn. Neither principal wants the ceremony. He insists, because the ceremony is the point.

Briseis

The prize, finally a person

Her first and only speech in the poem is a lament for Patroclus — the man who was kind to her after Achilles killed her husband, and who promised to make her a wife instead of a prize.

Patroclus

The mourned

Dead in the tent with his feet to the door. Thetis keeps his flesh from rotting with ambrosia, Briseis tears her face over him, and Achilles will not touch food while he lies unavenged.

Thetis

Achilles’ mother, a goddess

She arrives at dawn carrying armor no man has ever worn, sets it down ringing, and solves the one problem grief can still act on: she drops ambrosia into Patroclus’ wounds so the body will keep.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

Zeus notices that Achilles is starving himself and sends her down like a falcon to slip nectar into his chest without his knowledge. Achilles wins the argument about fasting; the gods quietly falsify the result.

Xanthus

Achilles’ immortal horse

Hera lends him a human voice for one speech: we will save you today, but your death is near, and the fault will not be ours. The Erinyes stop his mouth. He told his master nothing his master didn’t know.

Hera

Queen of the gods

Two cameos, both in character: in Agamemnon’s story she is the schemer who cheated Zeus over Heracles’ birth, and on the plain she is the one who gives Xanthus a voice.

Zeus

King of the gods

Present at one remove: as the precedent Agamemnon pleads — even Zeus was once fooled by Delusion — and as the quiet pity that sends Athena down with nectar.

Nestor

Old king of Pylos

One of the elders who stay behind after the assembly to coax Achilles into eating. The comfort does not take; the nectar has to be smuggled in.
