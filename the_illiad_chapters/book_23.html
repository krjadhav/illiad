The Iliad, retold

Book 23

Book Twenty-Three, after Samuel Butler’s translation: a ghost asks for one urn, twelve prisoners feed a pyre, and the army remembers how to play.

Hector is dead, and the army clocks out. When they reach the ships every man goes back to his own tent: except the Myrmidons, whom Achilles will not release. Not yet, he says; first we mourn. Three times they drive their chariots weeping around the body of Patroclus, and Achilles lays his bloodstained hand on his friend’s chest and renews the ugliest vow in the poem: I will drag Hector here and let dogs devour him raw, and twelve noble sons of Trojans will I slay before your pyre. Then a funeral feast, which he attends but will not wash for. He swears it as an oath: no water touches my body until Patroclus is on the flames, the barrow built, my head shaved. Grief, for Achilles, is a project plan with a hygiene freeze.

That night, worn out from chasing Hector around windy Ilius, he finally sleeps: and Patroclus comes to him. Not a symbol, not a voice: the man himself, “like what he had been in stature, voice, and the light of his beaming eyes,” wearing his own clothes. And the first thing the ghost does is complain. “You sleep, Achilles, and have forgotten me; you loved me living, but now that I am dead you think for me no further.” Bury me with all speed, he says, so I may pass the gates of Hades: the other ghosts drive me off; they will not let me across the river; I wander desolate outside the walls. It turns out the underworld has a lobby, and admission is bureaucratic: no rites, no entry. The dead themselves enforce it. Burial in this world isn’t sentiment. It’s paperwork the deceased is actively waiting on.

Then the second request, and it’s the reason this scene has outlived every empire since. Let not my bones be laid apart from yours, Achilles, but with them: “even as we were brought up together in your own home.” And he tells the story: how as a boy in Opoeis he killed another child in a stupid quarrel over dice: “not of set purpose”: and Menoetius brought him to Peleus’ house, and Peleus was kind to him and named him squire to his son. So: one urn for both sets of bones, the two-handled golden vase your mother gave you. The poem’s whole definition of friendship, stated from the far side of death: two lives that were raised as one should be filed as one. Achilles answers that of course he’ll do all of it, then asks for the one thing not on the list: come closer, let us hold each other one more time. He reaches out: “and there was nothing, and the spirit vanished as a vapour, gibbering and whining into the earth.” He springs up and smacks his hands together, and his cry is precise: there really are ghosts in the house of Hades, but there is no life in them. All night the thing hovered over me, looking wondrously like himself. The likeness was perfect. The person was gone. That gap is what mourning is.

Pause: check yourself

The ghost asks for two things: burn me quickly, and let our bones lie in a single urn. What is the second request actually saying?

The ghost does announce Achilles’ death: “you too are doomed to die beneath the wall”: which makes option a tempting. But notice he states it flatly, as fact, not leverage; the urn request never mentions it. The reason he actually gives runs entirely backward: the dice quarrel, Menoetius, Peleus’ kindness, “we were brought up together in your own home.” And it isn’t duty either: nothing in the culture requires a squire’s bones to share his master’s urn; that’s why the request needs making at all. What was one life should be one grave. The Iliad defines love by its storage requirements.

Morning brings logistics. Agamemnon sends men and mules from every part of the camp under Meriones, squire of Idomeneus; they fell tall oaks on Ida and haul the timber to the shore, to the spot where Achilles has planned “a mighty monument for Patroclus and for himself.” Read that clause twice. Then the procession: chariots first, a cloud of infantry behind, the body carried in the middle covered with locks of hair the men cut off and threw on it. At the pyre Achilles cuts one more lock: the one his father vowed to the river Spercheius against his homecoming, with fifty she-goats promised at the springs. Standing there he cancels the contract out loud: you have not granted the prayer, and I shall see my home no more: so I give this lock to Patroclus instead. He liquidates his own homecoming in a sentence and transfers the collateral to a dead man’s hands.

He also manages the crowd like a professional. There is a time to mourn, he tells Agamemnon, and a time to cease from mourning: dismiss the people to their dinners; the chief mourners will finish the work. Then the pyre goes up, a hundred feet on a side, and what goes onto it is an inventory of escalating darkness. Sheep and oxen, their fat wrapped around the body. Jars of honey and unguents. Four horses, thrown on as their owner groans. Two of the dead man’s own house-dogs. And then: “he also put twelve brave sons of noble Trojans to the sword and laid them with the rest, for he was full of bitterness and fury.” That’s the whole account. Butler gives the massacre a clause; the Greek gives it one flat editorial phrase: evil were the deeds he devised: and moves on to the fire. No names, no speeches, no scene. Twelve bound prisoners butchered between the dogs and the kindling, marked as evil in passing by the same poem that’s conducting the funeral. Meanwhile the other half of the vow is quietly failing: the dogs never touch Hector. Aphrodite keeps them off day and night and anoints him with rose oil; Apollo shades the ground with a cloud so the sun can’t parch him. The gods have started tending the body Achilles is still cursing.

Pause: check yourself

Twelve Trojan prisoners go onto the pyre with the horses and the dogs. How does the poem handle it?

Option a is Achilles’ own framing: he announced it as a vow: but nothing in the rite requires it: horses, dogs, honey, and fat are the furniture of a grand funeral, and no other funeral in Homer burns men. Option b imports a justice the text never promises: no god objects, no punishment follows, and the divine care for Hector’s body predates the massacre. What the poem actually does is spend one clause: the Greek flatly calls it evil work: and turn to the problem of getting the wood lit. That’s the horror: the atrocity is neither hidden nor dramatized. It’s itemized, in line, between the dogs and the honey.

Then the pyre won’t light. There is something almost comic in it: the greatest warrior alive, standing before his masterpiece of grief, and the wood just sits there. So Achilles does what you do in this world: he files a request with the relevant department. He prays to the north and west winds, Boreas and Zephyrus, and vows them offerings; Iris carries the message and finds the winds at a feast in Zephyrus’ house. They all jump up and offer her a seat; she can’t stay: the Ethiopians are sacrificing hecatombs and she wants her share: but delivers the request. The winds rise with a cry, whip the sea into waves, and fall on the pyre all night while Achilles paces the fire drawing wine from a golden bowl and pouring it into the ground, calling the name of the dead. Homer reaches for his measure of the grief: as a father mourns while burning the bones of a son who died on his wedding day. The oldest audience in the world knew exactly what that weighed.

At dawn the flames fall and the winds go home. They quench the embers with wine and gather the bones with tears: easy to find, Achilles notes with a mourner’s terrible practicality, “for they lie in the middle of the pyre,” everything else burned at the edges. Into a golden urn they go, in two layers of fat, “against the time when I shall myself go down into the house of Hades.” The urn stays open. He specs the barrow too: modest for now; afterwards, let those Achaeans who are left at the ships when I am gone build it broad and high. He has priced his own death down to the earthworks and the phasing of construction. And then, with the past buried and his own grave half-planned, he does the strangest and sanest thing in the poem. He stops the people from leaving, sits them down in a wide circle, and sends to the ships for prizes.

What follows is a full day of games, and it’s worth saying plainly what you’re watching: civilization rebooting from a cold start. These are the same men who have spent twenty-two books killing people: and here they are competing under published rules, for listed prizes, with an umpire (Achilles stations old Phoenix at the turning post “to note the running, and report truly”). Every dispute that flares: and they flare constantly, these are still Homeric heroes: gets settled in minutes, with words, judges, oaths, and consolation prizes. The quarrel that opened the poem was exactly this shape: men, prizes, honor. It ran unmanaged and nearly destroyed the army. Book Twenty-Three is the same experiment run with the safety systems on, and the man operating them is the last person you’d have predicted.

First event, biggest prizes: the chariot race. A woman skilled in useful arts and a twenty-two-measure cauldron for first; a pregnant mare for second; on down to two talents of gold and a two-handled urn. Achilles opens by recusing himself, with the unembarrassed frankness of the genuinely best: you know my immortal horses would win: but they are in mourning for the driver who used to wash them and oil their manes; “see how they stand weeping here, with their manes trailing on the ground.” Five men enter: Eumelus, the best horseman alive; Diomedes with the Trojan stallions he took from Aeneas; Menelaus with Agamemnon’s mare Aethe under the yoke; young Antilochus; and Meriones. And before the start, Nestor takes his son aside for the poem’s great lecture on cunning. Your horses are the slowest in the field, he tells Antilochus, so the race must be won somewhere else: “the woodman does more by skill than by brute force; by skill the pilot guides his storm-tossed barque over the sea, and so by skill one driver can beat another.” Everything compresses to the doubling-post: the dead tree stump with the two white stones. Hug it so close the nave of your wheel almost grazes it; lean your body left; give the right-hand horse the lash and a loose rein. A man who knows the precise moment to pull the rein beats a man with faster horses every time. It is the old man’s whole theory of life: intelligence as the poor man’s horsepower: handed over at the rail of a racecourse. His son listens carefully. Perhaps a little too carefully.

Pause: check yourself

Nestor’s horses are the slowest entered, and he lectures Antilochus anyway. What is the lecture actually about?

Option b fails on a detail Homer plants deliberately: the post is public knowledge: Achilles showed it to every driver before the start. What Nestor adds isn’t the location but the technique: lean left, loose rein, wheel-nave grazing the stone, and above all the “precise moment.” Option c is emotionally true, but the poem gives that theme its own scene later, with the urn. The lecture itself is Homer’s ode to metis: skill as the thing that beats endowment at the point where everything converges. Note what Antilochus does with it: the lesson was about the turning post, and he runs it on Menelaus.

The race is glorious chaos, and the gods can’t stay out of it even at sporting scale. Apollo, still sulking, knocks the whip from Diomedes’ hands; Athena hands it back and, in a rage, breaks Eumelus’ yoke for him: the best horseman in the field goes down at full speed, “his elbows, mouth, and nostrils all torn,” his eyes filling with tears while Diomedes sweeps past to a lead no one will touch. Behind them, Antilochus finds his moment: a narrow place where winter rain has sunk the road. Menelaus is holding the line into it; Antilochus pulls alongside anyway. Menelaus shouts that the road is too narrow, that he’ll wreck them both: and Antilochus “plied his whip, and drove faster, as though he had not heard him.” The senior man blinks first, easing off rather than pile both chariots into the ditch: the junior driver created the risk, and the elder absorbed it. Even the spectators start fighting. Idomeneus, watching from high ground, calls the new leader correctly; the lesser of the two men who share a famous name: Oileus’ quick little son, we’ll meet him again shortly: abuses him for it; wagers are proposed, tempers rise. And Achilles puts it out in two sentences: cease your railing, both of you; sit down and watch; you’ll know the winner directly. The man who once let a dispute over prizes run to catastrophe now closes one before it finishes its second insult.

Diomedes comes in first and takes the woman and the cauldron. Antilochus is second: “by a trick and not by the fleetness of his horses,” Butler notes, auditing to the last: with Menelaus a wheel’s-breadth behind and closing. Meriones is fourth, and last of all comes Eumelus, dragging his wrecked chariot and driving his horses before him on foot. Now watch Achilles, because the poem certainly is. He’s moved by the sight: “the best man is coming in last”: and proposes giving Eumelus the second prize. The crowd applauds. And Antilochus stands up and refuses: that mare is mine; Eumelus should have prayed to the immortals; if you pity him, “you have much gold in your tents”: give him a better prize from your own store, “but I will not give up the mare, and he that will fight me for her, let him come on.” A commander proposing to reassign a subordinate’s prize, and the subordinate refusing: this is Book One’s geometry, atom for atom. And Achilles smiles. He is pleased with his friend’s nerve, sends Automedon to his tent for the bronze breastplate he stripped from Asteropaeus, and gives it to Eumelus, who receives it gladly. Look at the move itself: he doesn’t overrule the winner and he doesn’t raid the prize pool. He expands it, at his own cost. The entire first book of the Iliad happened because nobody in it would do this.

Pause: check yourself

Achilles proposes second prize for Eumelus, who crashed and came in last; Antilochus, who earned it, refuses to hand it over. In Book One this exact shape burned the army down. What keeps it from burning now?

Option a fails on the text: Antilochus offers to fight any man who touches the mare: game prizes carry full honor-weight here, which is exactly why the scene is a real test. Option b is half-true: Achilles does smile because he loves him: but affection existed in Book One too, and it stopped nothing. The difference is mechanical, and Antilochus names it: “you have much gold in your tents... give him a better prize.” Someone absorbs the cost instead of transferring it, and honor stops being zero-sum. That solution was available to Agamemnon in Book One, stated almost word for word. Nobody would take it.

One protest left, and it’s the book’s masterpiece. Menelaus rises in a rage over the foul at the narrow place: and even his rage is procedural: an attendant puts the speaker’s staff in his hands and calls for silence first. His complaint is a small marvel of self-awareness. Judge between us, princes: and show no favor, lest men say Menelaus won by weight and influence over a better driver. No: I’ll rule on it myself, and no man will blame me: Antilochus, come stand before your chariot as custom is, and swear by the earth-encircling god that you did not foul me on purpose. It’s a perfectly built trap: perjure yourself before heaven, or admit the trick. And Antilochus does neither. He folds, and folds beautifully: “Forgive me; I am much younger, King Menelaus, than you are; you stand higher than I do and are the better man of the two.” Young men’s tempers are hasty, he says, their judgment thin: the mare is yours, and anything more you ask of mine, rather than fall from your good graces and be wrong in the sight of heaven. And Menelaus melts. Homer gives him the book’s loveliest simile: his heart made glad “as when dew falls upon a field of ripening corn”: and then, warmed all through, he gives the mare back. You’ve never been headstrong before, he says; your youth got the better of your judgment; don’t outwit your betters next time: and the people will see “that I am neither harsh nor vindictive.” Run the comparison the poem is begging you to run. In Book One, two men each refused to yield first, and both lost everything. Here the junior yields completely: and gets the prize back, plus Menelaus’ regard. Yielding turns out to be the only move in the whole poem that returns more honor than it spends.

The fifth prize from the race, the two-handled urn, is left unawarded: so Achilles carries it across the assembly to Nestor. Take this, my good old friend, as an heirloom and memorial of the funeral: “for you shall see him no more among the Argives.” And then, with a tactlessness so total it comes out the other side as tenderness: I give you this prize though you cannot win one; “you can now neither wrestle nor fight,” nor box, nor run, “for the hand of age has been laid heavily upon you.” Read cold, it’s brutal. Delivered and received, it’s an honor: because Nestor understands exactly what’s being given. He accepts gladly and pays for it in his own currency, the reminiscence: the funeral games of King Amarynceus, where he won the boxing and the wrestling and the running and the spear, and lost only the chariot race, to twins who ganged the reins between them. My heart rejoices, he finishes, that you are ever mindful of the respect due to me. An unearned prize, gracefully given, gratefully taken: and it works because prizes in this world were never really about performance. They’re about being seen. Achilles, of all people, has learned to hand out the seeing.

The rest of the card runs fast, and every event lands the same joke from a different angle: the war’s deadly machinery, scaled down until it’s safe enough to laugh at. Boxing: Epeus stands up and delivers the eternal athlete’s press conference: I am the best here, no man can be good at everything, and whoever faces me should have his friends standing by to carry him out. He then proves every word on Euryalus’ jaw: and catches him as he falls, and sets him kindly on his feet. Wrestling: Ajax and Odysseus, strength against craft, gripping like the rafters of a roof, backbones cracking. Neither can throw the other: Odysseus tries the knee-trick, they go down twice in the dust: and before a third fall can settle anything, Achilles stops it: “the victory is with both alike”: equal prizes, next event. A draw, called by the judge before pride can force an ending someone would have to lose.

Then the foot race, the book’s best comedy. Oileus’ son takes the lead at once; Odysseus runs so close behind that his breath is on the leader’s neck, treading in his footprints before the dust can settle. Nearing the finish, Odysseus prays inwardly to Athena: and she does two things: makes his limbs light, and trips the leader into the slick of dung left from the funeral cattle. The man crosses the line second with his mouth and nostrils full of it, takes the ox for his prize, and stands there spitting and complaining: the goddess “stands by Ulysses as though she were his own mother.” The whole army laughs: at divine intervention, the very thing that has been killing them for weeks, now shrunk to the size of a prank at a track meet. Antilochus, collecting third prize, caps it with the book’s driest joke: you all see, friends, that the gods honor the older generation: the man ahead of me is a bit older than I am, and Odysseus is of an earlier generation entirely, yet no Achaean can outrun him “save only Achilles.” Flattery, filed openly as flattery: and paid on the spot: Achilles, delighted, adds half a talent of gold to his prize. Praise in this economy is real work, and the host settles his invoices immediately.

One event goes wrong, instructively. Achilles offers Sarpedon’s armor for a duel in full gear: first man to draw blood wins a Thracian sword. Ajax and Diomedes arm and go at each other three times, and when Diomedes starts working the point of his spear at Ajax’s neck, the crowd itself calls it off, “alarmed for his safety.” The audience has remembered what the game is a model of, and refuses to watch the model run to completion. The shot-put goes to Polypoetes, who throws the iron lump the way a herdsman flings his stick, clean over the whole field’s marks. The archery turns on accounting: Teucer, first up, forgets to promise Apollo a hecatomb and misses the pigeon: though he cuts the tether: while Meriones snatches the bow, promises the lambs mid-draw, and takes the bird out of the sky. Even at a carnival shooting gallery, the gods bill for services, and the man with the open purchase order wins.

And then the last event, which never happens. Achilles sets out a spear and a flowered cauldron for the javelin, and Agamemnon himself stands up to throw, against Meriones. Achilles stops the contest before it starts: son of Atreus, “we know how far you excel all others” in the throw: take the first prize back to your ships, and let’s give the spear to Meriones, if it please you. Agamemnon assents, takes the cauldron, hands it to his herald. That’s the poem’s last prize: awarded unthrown. The Iliad opened with these two men tearing the army apart over who got which prize; it closes the games with one of them handing the other honor he didn’t compete for, free, in front of everyone: and the gift costs the giver nothing, because grief has moved Achilles out of the scarcity market entirely. He has spent the day minting honor and distributing it: to the loser who was best, to the old man who couldn’t enter, to the flatterer, to the king. The army is whole again, laughing, fed, at play. Only one piece of grief in the camp remains unprocessed: it lies face-down in the dust by the ships, kept whole by the gods, waiting. The games taught these men how to be human with each other again. Book Twenty-Four will ask Achilles to extend that to the enemy.

Last one

The final event never happens: Agamemnon stands up for the spear-throw and Achilles hands him first prize without a single throw. What is that gesture?

Option c is the fun reading, but the scene carries none of Homer’s mockery markers: no laughter, no aside, and Agamemnon assents without a flicker; compare Nestor’s urn, where the “you cannot win” is said out loud and received as honor anyway. Option a gets the direction of power wrong: Achilles isn’t beneath anyone at these games: he is host, judge, and bank, and giving from above is the opposite of submission. The point is the currency. The poem began with these two fighting over a prize because honor was scarce; Achilles now manufactures it and gives it away: to Eumelus, to Nestor, to Antilochus, and finally, pointedly, to Agamemnon. What’s still unpaid lies in the dust outside the camp, and that bill comes due in Book Twenty-Four.

TLDR

Patroclus is buried with terrible honors, including twelve Trojan prisoners. Achilles then hosts funeral games where prize disputes are settled with grace, turning the honor system that broke the army into something briefly humane.

Who’s who

Achilles

Host, judge, chief mourner

He buries his friend, slaughters twelve prisoners in his fury, then runs the games with perfect grace: smoothing quarrels, inventing consolation prizes. The man who wrecked the army over a prize now gives them away.

Patroclus

The ghost

He comes back in a dream with two requests: burn me quickly, so the dead will let me across the river, and let our bones lie in a single urn. Achilles reaches for him and he vanishes like a vapour into the earth.

Antilochus

Nestor’s son

He takes his father’s lecture on cunning a little too broadly, forces Menelaus off the racing line, then yields the mare so gracefully: you are my elder and my better: that he gets it back.

Menelaus

King of Sparta

Fouled at the narrow place, he files a formal protest, staff in hand: and the moment Antilochus yields, his anger melts like dew on ripening corn and he hands the mare straight back.

Nestor

Old king of Pylos

His racing lecture is an ode to cunning: the woodman does more by skill than by brute force. Achilles brings him a prize he never competed for: you will box and wrestle no more: and he is delighted.

Eumelus

Best of the charioteers

Leading the race when Athena breaks his yoke: elbows, mouth, and nostrils torn, he drags his wrecked chariot in last: and Achilles, sorry for the best man, finds him a consolation prize.

Diomedes

Son of Tydeus

Winner of the chariot race after Apollo slaps the whip from his hand and Athena hands it back. In the armed duel he keeps aiming at Ajax’s neck until the crowd stops the fight.

Odysseus

The cunning one

He wrestles giant Ajax to a draw with a knee-trick, then wins the foot race by praying to Athena in the final stretch. Skill and patronage, indistinguishable as usual.

Ajax

Son of Telamon

The army’s biggest man cracks backbones with Odysseus until Achilles calls the wrestling a draw, and fights Diomedes in full armor until the spectators refuse to watch the ending.

Agamemnon

Commander-in-chief

He stands up for the spear-throw and Achilles hands him first prize without a single throw: free honor from the man whose prize he confiscated in Book One.

Idomeneus

Captain of the Cretans

From the best seat on the high ground he calls the race correctly, gets abused for it by the lesser Ajax, and offers to settle it with a wager: a quarrel Achilles shuts down in two sentences.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

Still fixing outcomes, now at sporting scale: she returns Diomedes’ whip, breaks Eumelus’ yoke, and trips the lesser Ajax into cow dung so Odysseus can win the foot race.

Hector

Unburied

His body still lies in the dust by the ships, promised to the dogs: but Aphrodite keeps them off with rose oil and Apollo shades him with a cloud. The gods are tending what Achilles is still cursing.
