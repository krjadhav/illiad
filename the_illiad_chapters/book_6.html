The Iliad, retold

Book 6

Book Six, after Samuel Butler’s translation: guest-friends trade armor, a goddess refuses a robe, and Hector goes home to say goodbye.

For the first time since the poem began, the gods leave. No one intervenes in Book Six: no meteor descents, no snapped chin-straps: and what’s left is humans doing war to each other at human scale. Homer opens with the ledger running as usual: Ajax breaks a phalanx; Diomedes kills Axylus of Arisbe: a rich man with a house by the roadside who entertained every traveler who passed, “yet not one of his guests stood before him to save his life.” Hospitality as insurance, and the claim denied. Homer’s one-line biographies keep doing their work: every corpse costs you something.

Then the poem shows you exactly what this war has become. A Trojan named Adrestus crashes his chariot into a tamarisk bush, tumbles out face-first in the dust, and clasps Menelaus by the knees: take me alive; my father is rich; he’ll pay you a great ransom. And Menelaus: the wronged husband, the man with the best claim to hatred: is about to say yes. He’s calling a squire to lead the prisoner away when Agamemnon arrives at a run: my good brother, is this the time for mercy? Did your house fare so well at Trojan hands? Let not one of them escape: not even the child unborn in its mother’s womb: let all in Ilius perish, unmourned and forgotten. Menelaus pushes Adrestus away; Agamemnon spears him and stands on his chest to pull the spear back out. And the narrator adds five words that have bothered readers for three thousand years: for his words were just.

Pause: check yourself

Agamemnon talks his brother out of taking a ransom: kill them all, even the unborn: and the narrator says his words were just. What is the poem doing with that line?

Both wrong answers are live scholarly readings, which is why they tempt. But notice what the scene is built to do: Homer shows you the ransom system working: Menelaus was taking the deal: and then shows the argument that switches it off, delivered by the man whose prizes started the whole poem. “His words were just” is the world grading itself. The horror isn’t that the narrator approves; it’s that inside this logic, the approval is sincere. Atrocity rarely announces itself as atrocity. It arrives as a persuasive argument between brothers.

Nestor follows with an order that completes the thought: no man stop to strip the dead for booty: kill now, loot at leisure afterward. Efficiency, applied to slaughter. The Trojan line is about to break entirely when Helenus: Priam’s son, best of the bird-readers: grabs the two men holding it together, Hector and Aeneas, and issues the plan that shapes the rest of the book: you two rally the line here. And Hector: go into the city. Have our mother gather the noblewomen at Athena’s temple, lay our finest robe on the goddess’s knees, promise twelve heifers: anything to make her stop Diomedes. We did not fear Achilles himself, goddess-born, the way we fear this man. So Hector goes, and Homer follows him with one physical detail: the black rim of his shield, slung on his back, tapping his neck and his ankles as he runs.

Before the city, though, the field produces the strangest meeting in the poem. Glaucus of Lycia and Diomedes advance on each other between the armies, and Diomedes opens with a question and a disclaimer: who are you? I’ve never seen you before, and you’re either very brave or very unlucky. And if you’re a god: I don’t fight gods anymore. He tells the story of Lycurgus, who attacked a god’s nurses with an ox-goad and was struck blind and died hated by heaven. This from the man who put two Olympians in the infirmary yesterday. He read the fine print on his permission slip, and he knows it expired.

Glaucus’s answer begins with the most famous lines in the poem: why ask my lineage? As the generations of leaves, so are the generations of men. The wind sheds the old leaves on the ground; spring buds the new. Men come and go the same way. And then: having just said lineage is meaningless: he recites his for sixty lines, because he’s human: his grandfather was Bellerophon. The beautiful man a queen falsely accused when he refused her; sent to Lycia carrying “lying letters” on a folded tablet: the only writing anywhere in Homer, and it’s a murder order the bearer can’t read. The Chimaera killed, the ambush of picked men annihilated, the kingdom and the king’s daughter won: and then, at the height of everything, the gods turned on him, and he ended wandering the Alean plain alone, “gnawing at his own heart, shunning the path of men.” The same phrase the poem used for Achilles. Blessed careers in this world have a house style for how they end.

Diomedes hears one name in all of that and plants his spear in the ground. Bellerophon? My grandfather Oeneus hosted him for twenty days: they exchanged gifts, a purple belt for a golden double cup; I still have the cup at home. Then you are my guest-friend from my father’s house, and I am yours in Lycia. Let’s avoid each other’s spears: there are plenty of Trojans for me and plenty of Greeks for you: and let’s trade armor, so both armies know the old tie holds. They clasp hands in the middle of a battle. And then Homer, who cannot let a warm moment leave the room unaudited: Zeus stole Glaucus’s wits, because he swapped gold armor for bronze: the worth of a hundred oxen for the worth of nine.

Pause: check yourself

Two enemies meet in no-man’s land, discover their grandfathers exchanged gifts decades ago, and agree to avoid each other for the rest of the war. What survived here that nothing else in the poem has?

Option b is the cynical reading and it half-works: the deal does reduce both men’s risk. But self-interest alone can’t explain the armor swap, which is a public, costly signal (ruinously costly, for Glaucus) whose whole point is that the obligation is real and visible. Xenia binds grandsons through gifts exchanged before they were born: a network with better persistence than any state, treaty, or truce we’ve seen so far; every oath in this poem has broken except this one. That’s also why Axylus’s death in the opening lines stings: hospitality was supposed to be exactly this kind of insurance, and the poem shows it paying out here and failing there.

Meanwhile Hector reaches the Scaean gates and the city closes around him. Wives and daughters come running to ask after their sons, brothers, husbands: and he tells them to pray, which is a way of answering. Homer gives us the tour of Priam’s palace: fifty chambers of polished stone for his sons and their wives, twelve for his daughters and theirs: the physical plant of a dynasty, described just before the poem starts dismantling it. Hecuba meets him with a mother’s triage: you look exhausted, let me bring you wine, pour a libation and drink the rest. And Hector refuses in a sentence that tells you who he is: no wine, mother: it might unman me: and I cannot pour an offering to Zeus with unwashed hands. A man bespattered with blood and filth may not pray. He redirects her to the temple with the robe and the vow of heifers, and adds, about the brother he’s about to visit: would that the earth might open and swallow Paris, for he was bred to be the ruin of Troy. If I saw him going down to Hades, my heart would forget its heaviness.

The robe scene is four sentences and among the coldest in the epic. Hecuba goes to her storeroom and picks the largest, most beautiful robe: Sidonian work, glittering like a star, kept at the very bottom of the chest. Homer notes where it came from: Paris brought it from Sidon, on the same voyage that carried off Helen. Even the offering is contraband from the original sin. Theano, priestess of the temple, lays it on Athena’s knees and prays: break Diomedes’s spear before the gates, and twelve heifers are yours: only pity the town, the wives, the little children. Then the flattest line Homer ever wrote: Thus she prayed, but Pallas Athena granted not her prayer.

Pause: check yourself

The finest robe in Troy, laid on the goddess’s knees, plus twelve promised heifers: the biggest offering in the poem so far. Why doesn’t it work?

The tainted-robe detail is real and Homer plants it deliberately: but as irony, not mechanism; gods in this poem take goods from worse provenances. The actual reason sits two books back: Zeus said no city ever honored him like Troy, and signed it away anyway, and Hera priced her own three favorite cities as the fee. Once you’ve seen that market, this scene reads correctly: Theano is filing a claim against an account that was liquidated at the executive level, and the poem’s flat “granted not her prayer” is the form-letter rejection. Piety is real in the Iliad. It just isn’t purchasing power.

Then Hector goes to find his brother, and finds him exactly where you’d guess: at home, in a beautiful house built by the best builders in Troy, handling his armor: fondling the curved bow, admiring the cuirass: while Helen sits directing her maids at their work. The rebuke is short: people are dying around the city because of you; you’d despise anyone else you caught shirking; get up, before Troy burns. And Paris does what Paris always does: agrees completely: your rebuke is just. I’m not even sulking, really; I was indulging my grief. My wife was just now urging me to battle herself. Victory shifts around, after all. Wait while I arm: or go ahead, I’ll catch up. Nine years into the war, he treats it as a mood he’s entitled to have.

Helen speaks to Hector, and her speech is the book’s quiet devastation: brother: brother of a scheming horror of a woman: I wish the whirlwind had carried me off the day I was born. Or failing that, that I’d been given to a better man: one who could feel dishonor, who smarted under what people say. This one has no such equipment, and he’ll reap what he’s sown. Sit with me a while; you carry the weight of my hatefulness and his folly. And then the line that stops the poem cold: Zeus doomed us both, that we may be a theme of song for those born hereafter. She knows what she’s in. She can see the poem from inside it, and she’s the only one who can.

Hector won’t sit. His reason is the hinge of the book: I must see my household: my wife and my little son: for I know not whether I shall ever come back to them. He goes home; Andromache isn’t there. The housekeeper explains: she heard the Trojans were being driven, and she went to the wall “in frenzied haste,” like a madwoman, the nurse running behind with the baby. So the two of them: the husband leaving for the field, the wife rushing to watch it: meet in the middle, at the Scaean gates, the exact boundary between the war and the city. Homer built this book’s whole geography for that one collision.

She takes his hand and says the thing everyone in the poem knows and no one says: your own courage will kill you. And I have no one else. Achilles killed my father when he sacked Thebe: though he honored him, she adds, even now precise: burned him in his armor, raised a mound, and the mountain nymphs planted elms: and my seven brothers, all in one day, among their sheep and cattle. My mother was ransomed home and died. Hector, you are father to me, and mother, and brothers, and husband. Stay on the wall. And then: because she has been standing on that wall studying the war like a general: the tactical addendum: post the men by the fig tree, where the wall is weakest; their best have already probed it three times. Grief, and a competent defense review, in one breath.

His answer is the most honest thing any fighter says in the Iliad. All of this is in my mind too, wife. But I could not face the Trojans, men or women, if I hung back like a coward: and my own heart won’t allow it; I was bred to be first. And then he tells her what he knows: the day will surely come when sacred Ilius will be destroyed, and Priam, and Priam’s people. Yet it isn’t Troy’s fall that tortures him, or his mother’s death, or his brothers’: it’s the vision of her, enslaved in Argos, working someone else’s loom, hauling someone else’s water, while a stranger points: that was Hector’s wife. May the earth be heaped over me before I hear you scream as they drag you away. He isn’t fighting to win. He has priced the defeat to the last detail and fights anyway.

Then the helmet. He reaches for his son, and the baby shrieks and buries himself in the nurse’s chest: terrified of the bronze, and the horsehair crest nodding down at him. And his father and mother laugh out loud. It’s the only laugh in the book, maybe the most human moment in the epic: Hector takes the helmet off, lays it gleaming on the ground, kisses the boy, dandles him, and prays: Zeus, grant that my son be as I am, first among Trojans: let him rule Ilius in strength: let people say he is far better than his father as he comes home from war carrying the bloody armor of the man he’s killed, and let his mother’s heart be glad. It is the tenderest scene in Greek literature, and the blessing inside it is a wish for more of exactly what is about to destroy them all. The culture cannot imagine a good life for the boy that isn’t this one.

Pause: check yourself

The baby screams at his father’s helmet; Hector takes it off, and both parents laugh. Why does Homer build the scene around the helmet?

The omen reading is ancient and not wrong about the future: the audience knows what happens to this child: but it puts the meaning in the baby, and Homer puts it in the object. Astyanax isn’t afraid of his father; he’s afraid of the helmet, and the instant it comes off, the terror is gone. That’s the scene’s whole argument: “Hector” the defender of Ilius is a thing you put on, and underneath it there was, for one moment on the wall, just a man holding his son. The laugh (option c is right about its effect) is both parents seeing the gap between the two: the only time anyone in the poem gets to.

Andromache takes the boy back, “smiling through her tears”: three words carrying the whole scene: and Hector comforts her with the consolation of fatalists everywhere: no man sends me to Hades before my hour; and no man born, brave or coward, escapes his hour once come. Go home, tend the loom and the distaff and the servants. War is men’s business, and mine above all men in Ilius. He picks the helmet back up off the ground. She walks home turning around again and again, and when she gets there, she and all her women mourn him: a funeral lament for a living man, held in his own house, because none of them believe he’s coming back from the Achaeans’ hands. The poem agrees with them; it just hasn’t happened yet.

And then, because Homer’s cruelty is structural, here comes Paris: gleaming. He bursts from the citadel in his bronze like a stalled horse that snaps its rope and gallops to the river, head high, mane streaming, glorying in himself: laughing out loud as he runs. He catches Hector at the exact spot where the farewell just happened. Sorry, brother: kept you waiting? And Hector, emptied out, answers more gently than he ever has: no one could call you a coward when you actually fight. It’s the willful slacking: it grieves my heart what the Trojans say of you, they’ve suffered so much for your sake. Come on. We’ll settle accounts later, if Zeus ever grants us the day we set the cup of freedom before the gods in our own halls, with the Achaeans driven from Troy. On that if: two brothers walking out of the gate, one immune to shame and one made entirely of it: the book ends.

Last one

Hector states it plainly: Troy will fall, Priam and all his people with it. He goes back out anyway. What actually keeps him fighting?

He supplies all three himself, which is the trap. But watch the order: the first reason out of his mouth, before fate is ever mentioned, is “with what face could I look on the Trojans, men or women”: and his nightmare isn’t death but a stranger’s sentence about his wife: that was Hector’s wife. The fatalism is what he offers Andromache as comfort; the hope is what he offers Paris as courtesy. What drives him is the same currency that has driven every disaster since Book One: how one is seen: except that in Hector the poem finally shows honor’s other face: not Agamemnon defending status he didn’t earn, but a man holding up a doomed city because being what people need him to be is the only self he has.

TLDR

The gods step back and the human costs come forward. Diomedes and Glaucus honor an old guest-friendship, Troy's women fail to move Athena, and Hector leaves Andromache and Astyanax because shame keeps him in the role of defender.

Who’s who

Hector

Commander of Troy

The book belongs to him: he rallies the line, scolds his brother, refuses his mother’s wine, and says goodbye to his wife and son knowing: and saying: that Troy will fall.

Andromache

Hector’s wife

Daughter of a king Achilles killed, sister of seven brothers Achilles killed. Hector is her father, mother, brothers, and husband in one: and she has already done the siege analysis on the weak stretch of wall.

Astyanax

Hector’s infant son

The baby who screams at his father’s plumed helmet and gets the poem’s only family laugh. His name means “lord of the city”: the people named him for his father’s job.

Paris

Prince of Troy

Found at home fondling his armor while the city bleeds for him. His rebukes are always just, he always agrees, and he always catches up late, gleaming like a stallion.

Helen

The cause, self-convicted

She wishes the whirlwind had taken her at birth, wishes she’d been given a man who could feel shame: and knows exactly what she and Paris are: a theme of song for people not yet born.

Hecuba

Queen of Troy

Hector’s mother. She offers him wine and gets sent to Athena with the finest robe in the house instead. The goddess is not moved.

Glaucus

Lycian captain

Grandson of Bellerophon. He answers “who are you?” with the most famous lines in the poem: men as generations of leaves: then trades gold armor for bronze, a hundred oxen for nine.

Diomedes

Son of Tydeus

Troy now fears him more than Achilles. But when he learns his grandfather hosted Glaucus’s grandfather for twenty days, the spear goes into the ground and the gifts come out.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

The women of Troy lay their most precious robe on her knees and ask her to break Diomedes. Homer gives her answer one flat line: she granted not their prayer.

Agamemnon

Commander-in-chief of the Greek army

He catches Menelaus accepting a ransom and talks him out of mercy: not even the unborn child, he says, should leave Ilius alive. The narrator calls his words just.

Menelaus

King of Sparta

The softer Atreid: he’s ready to take Adrestus alive for ransom until his brother arrives at a run. He was persuaded.

Theano

Priestess of Athena

Antenor’s wife, keeper of the temple keys. She lays the robe on the goddess’s knees and makes the prayer that isn’t granted.

Helenus

Trojan prince and augur

Priam’s son, wisest of the bird-readers. The whole Troy sequence is his plan: rally the line here, and send Hector to set the women praying.

Adrestus

A Trojan, briefly a prisoner

His horses bolt into a tamarisk bush and he begs Menelaus for ransom, clasping his knees. Then Agamemnon arrives.

Nestor

Old king of Pylos

One order this book: don’t stop to strip the dead: kill now, loot at leisure afterward. Efficiency, applied to slaughter.

Priam

King of Troy

His palace gets the poem’s real-estate tour: fifty chambers of hewn stone for his sons, twelve for his daughters: the dynasty the war is dismantling.

Achilles

Offstage, everywhere

Absent again, present everywhere: he killed Andromache’s father: and buried him with full honors: and her seven brothers in a day. Troy’s new nightmare is that Diomedes scares them more.

Aeneas

Prince of Troy

Named with Hector as one of the two mainstays of Troy: Helenus assigns them the rally while the women pray.

Zeus

King of the gods

Mentioned constantly, moved never: he takes Glaucus’s wits, hears Hector’s prayer for the boy, and has already decided how all of this ends.
