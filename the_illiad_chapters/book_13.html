The Iliad, retold

Book 13

Book Thirteen, after Samuel Butler’s translation: Zeus glances away, his brother comes up from the sea in borrowed faces, and the whole war turns on where the boss is looking.

The most consequential glance in the Iliad happens in the first five lines of Book Thirteen, and it’s a glance away. Zeus has driven Hector to the ships. As far as he’s concerned the matter is settled, so he turns his “keen eyes” elsewhere: to the horse-breeders of Thrace, the Mysians, the noble Hippemolgi “who live on milk,” the Abians, “justest of mankind.” Distant tribes, quiet lives: a god’s equivalent of gazing out the office window. He posts no guard and delegates nothing, because he doesn’t think he needs to: he “did not think that any of the immortals would go and help either Trojans or Danaans.” The entire book happens inside that assumption.

One immortal “had kept no blind look-out.” Poseidon has been sitting on the topmost crest of wooded Samothrace, watching the battle with pity for the Greeks and fury at his brother. The moment the great eyes turn away, he moves. Homer gives him the most gorgeous commute in the poem: three strides down the mountain, a fourth to the golden palace under the sea at Aegae; the chariot, the horses with manes of gold, the sea-monsters coming out of their lairs to gambol around a lord they recognize, the water opening a path so smoothly that the bronze axle never gets wet. It’s an entrance built for a god arriving to do something spectacular. And then, at the ships, he does the least spectacular thing imaginable: he parks. Horses unyoked in a cave between Tenedos and Imbrus, hobbled in gold. He walks into the war wearing someone else’s face.

The face is Calchas: the army’s own prophet, the man whose job is saying hard things. As Calchas he finds the two Ajaxes and tells them what they already believe: you two can save the ships if you stand right here: then strikes them both with his sceptre, fills them with daring, and is gone like a falcon off a cliff. The lesser of the two knows at once: “I knew him by his feet and knees as he turned away, for the gods are soon recognised.” Then the god rounds on the men weeping by the sterns: “Shame on you, young Argives”: and delivers a speech of real political craft. Yes, Agamemnon caused this by insulting Achilles. “Still this is no reason why we should leave off fighting. Let us be quick to heal, for the hearts of the brave heal quickly.” Acknowledge the grievance, then take it off the table. He never fights; he does morale. The whole intervention is designed to leave no fingerprints.

Why the mask? Homer answers in the middle of the book, in one of the best lines about org charts ever written. The brothers “were of the same race and country, but Zeus was elder born and knew more, therefore Poseidon feared to defend the Argives openly.” That’s the whole grievance: not better: elder. Poseidon isn’t an underling; he’s a brother with an equal claim and a worse seat, and he does what people in that position have done ever since: comply with the letter of the directive and work around its spirit. He never throws a spear. He inspires, shames, quickens legs, stiffens knees. Every effect he produces could plausibly be produced by courage alone. If Zeus turns around, there is nothing to see: just Greeks, inexplicably, fighting harder. It’s insubordination engineered for the audit.

Poseidon defies Zeus the moment his back is turned: but only in disguise, and only by pep talk. Why the elaborate restraint?

Option c is the tempting one because it’s true: morale does scale better than one god’s spear, and Poseidon runs the whole line on it. But it explains the method, not the mask: a god could inspire troops in his own shape. Homer states the real reason flatly: same race and country, “but Zeus was elder born and knew more, therefore Poseidon feared to defend the Argives openly.” Not wiser by nature; elder. And the disguise isn’t for mortals at all: the lesser Ajax sees through it in seconds and nothing happens. It’s for the one observer who matters. This is the art of working around the boss while staying technically compliant, performed at divine scale.

So Book Thirteen becomes a physics lesson in the scarcest resource in any hierarchy: attention. Zeus is still minded to give the Trojans victory: he wants Achilles honored: and his will stalls the instant he stops watching, because his will was never a law, only an enforcement. Homer’s image for the result is exact: the two sons of Cronus “devise a knot of war and battle that none could unloose or break, and set both sides tugging at it, to the failing of men’s knees beneath them.” One god pulling openly, one in secret; the men in between feel it as exhaustion. Nothing about the front has changed: same armies, same wall, same ships: except where the boss is looking. That turns out to be everything.

Pause: check yourself

Zeus still intends a Trojan victory, yet the field starts tilting Greek the moment he looks toward Thrace. What does the poem say actually governs events?

The knot makes b tempting: it sounds like destiny. But look at what the knot is made of: not fate, just two brothers pulling opposite ways, one openly, one not. Nothing here was ordained; Zeus’s own plan stalls the moment he stops enforcing it, which is the opposite of fate. And c is what the soldiers themselves would say: but Homer shows you the sceptre-touch, the quickened legs, the god walking the line in a borrowed face. The battle doesn’t turn on strength, which hasn’t changed, or on destiny, which hasn’t spoken. It turns on the scarcest resource in any hierarchy: where the man at the top happens to be looking.

What Poseidon’s morale-work builds is a wall of men. Around the two Ajaxes gather the picked troops, and Homer describes the formation with the loving precision he usually reserves for armor: “spear to spear, shield to shield, buckler to buckler, helmet to helmet, and man to man,” crests touching when they nod. Against it comes Hector like “a rock that comes thundering down the side of some mountain,” loosened by winter rain, bounding through the forest: until it reaches level ground, “and then for all its fury it can go no further.” It’s one of Homer’s most honest similes, because it concedes the physics up front: momentum is not a property of the man, it’s a property of the slope. Hector has been running downhill since Zeus tilted the field. The serried line is where the ground goes flat.

Then the grinding starts. Teucer kills Imbrius, Priam’s son-in-law, who falls “as an ash-tree” felled on the crest of a high mountain: and the fight over the body tells you what this war has become. Hector’s spear, meant for Teucer, kills Amphimachus instead; the Ajaxes drag Imbrius off like two lions carrying a goat through brushwood; and the lesser one cuts the head from the neck and sends it “whirling over the crowd as though it had been a ball,” until it lands in the dust at Hector’s feet. Homer doesn’t flinch and doesn’t editorialize. But the plot notices: Amphimachus was Poseidon’s grandson, and the god’s response to the death is not grief but escalation. He goes to find Idomeneus: wearing a new face.

The face is Thoas this time, and the pitch is different, because Idomeneus is not a young man who needs daring: he’s a middle-aged captain who needs needling. Where are the threats the Achaeans used to make? Idomeneus answers like a professional: nobody’s slacking; this looks like Zeus’s will. The god’s reply is the recruiting line of the book: get your armor and come, “we must make all haste together if we may be of any use, though we are only two. Even cowards gain courage from companionship, and we two can hold our own with the bravest.” On the way out Idomeneus runs into his squire Meriones, walking back for a spear: he broke his on Deiphobus’s shield: and offers him one of the twenty standing against the wall of his tent, all taken from dead Trojans, “for I am not one to keep my enemy at arm’s length.”

And then, in the middle of a rout, Idomeneus delivers a small lecture on what courage actually is, and it’s the observation of a man who has spent thirty years watching men wait. Put the best men in an ambush, he says: “there is nothing like this for showing what a man is made of.” The coward changes color; he can’t sit still; he shifts his weight from one knee to the other; his heart hammers; you can hear his teeth. The brave man may feel all of it, but he holds his color and holds still and wants the fight to start. Courage, in other words, isn’t the absence of fear. It’s the management of its symptoms: and it’s measured in the waiting, not the charge. Then he catches himself: enough, we’re talking like children; someone will notice. Go fetch the spear.

Watch where he chooses to fight. The center, he reasons, is covered: it has the Ajaxes and Teucer, “the finest archer of all the Achaeans” and good in close too: enough to keep Hector busy unless Zeus throws a firebrand personally. So: the left wing, where the line is weakest. Homer introduces the rampage with the detail that makes it: Idomeneus spread panic among the Trojans “though his hair was already flecked with grey.” And the book keeps honest accounts of what that means. When the glory has run its course his knees lose their spring: he can no longer charge, can’t leap back for his own weapon, can’t dodge; his “heavy feet could not bear him swiftly out of the battle.” When young Aeneas comes for him, he calls his friends to his side and says the quiet part with no shame at all: I go in great fear: he is in the flower of youth, and “if I was of the same age as he is and in my present mind, either he or I should soon bear away the prize.” It’s the most self-aware sentence any fighter in the poem gets. He knows the machine is losing horsepower, and he fights anyway: not on speed but on position, cover, and knowing what a spear will do before it’s thrown.

Pause: check yourself

Idomeneus is gray-haired, admits his legs are going, and still wrecks the Trojan left. What does the book say makes him effective?

Poseidon does help once: Alcathous stands frozen for the spear: so a is half-true; but a god who has to needle you into your armor isn’t carrying you, and every choice after the tent is the man’s. The ambush speech makes b tempting, and it is real expertise. But watch what he does with the knowledge: he doesn’t screen recruits with it, he manages himself. He picks the weak wing, where positioning beats footspeed; fights behind the big round shield; calls for help against young Aeneas “in great fear” and without a flicker of embarrassment; retreats slowly when the knees give out. He has replaced the currency he’s lost: speed: with the one that appreciates: knowing. Competence aging into craft.

The most memorable of his kills is a business story. Othryoneus came to Troy from Cabesus “but lately,” bidding for Cassandra, “the fairest of Priam’s daughters”: and he “offered no gifts of wooing, for he promised a great thing”: he would drive the Greeks from Troy. Priam accepted, promised her, and Othryoneus “fought on the strength of the promises thus made to him.” Zero down; the reward in hand; the debt to be worked off later. Idomeneus’s spear catches him mid-stride, cuirass and all, and then comes the cruelest vaunt in the poem: I shall admire you above all men, sir: if you actually deliver what you promised. Come along to the ships and we’ll draw up a covenant of our own: we’ll fetch you Agamemnon’s loveliest daughter from Argos to marry, if you’ll just sack Troy for us instead. “We will not be hard upon you about gifts of wooing.” He drags the body by the foot as he says it.

Pause: check yourself

Idomeneus kills Cassandra’s suitor, then offers the corpse a marriage deal of his own: Agamemnon’s daughter for the sack of Troy, easy terms on the gifts. What is the vaunt actually doing?

Vaunting is standard, which makes c tempting: but most vaunts assert strength, and this one is drafted like a contract: a covenant “at the ships,” a bride fetched from Argos, and the killer clause, “we will not be hard upon you about gifts of wooing.” It’s a term-by-term parody of the deal Othryoneus struck: nothing down, payment in promised future heroics. And b has it backwards: Idomeneus addresses the dead buyer, not the seller; Priam at least received a bid. The poem’s target is the man who levered a princess with money he hadn’t earned yet. He paid nothing down, and nothing is what his estate collects.

The left wing becomes a ledger neither side can keep straight. Asius comes to avenge Othryoneus and takes a spear through the throat before he can swing; he falls “as an oak, or poplar, or pine which shipwrights have felled for ship’s timber upon the mountains.” Deiphobus, in a mood to vaunt, misses Idomeneus and kills Hypsenor instead, then announces that Asius has gone to Hades with an escort. Idomeneus answers with a scoreboard: “shall we cry quits now that we have killed three men to your one?” Above the tally the gods keep their thumbs on things: when Alcathous stands “stock still like a pillar or lofty tree” while a Cretan spear finds his heart, that’s Poseidon binding a man’s limbs: the god has quietly moved from morale to murder, so long as it’s a mortal’s hand on the shaft. Deiphobus, needing help, recruits Aeneas the way everything gets done in this poem: by obligation. Alcathous was your sister’s husband; he raised you as a child in his house; come avenge him. It works. And when the scrum finally breaks, Meriones ends the argument by putting a spear through Deiphobus’s arm; his brother Polites carries him out of the battle, groaning.

Menelaus gets the book’s strangest aria. He puts a spear clean through Helenus’s bow hand: the seer retires with the weapon literally pinned to his palm: then kills Pisander face to face, axe against sword, and stands over the wreckage asking Zeus a real question: you are said to excel gods and men in wisdom; how is this your favor? Everything else palls, he says: “sleep, love, sweet song, and stately dance”: a man would sooner have his fill of any of them than of war; and it’s war the Trojans cannot get enough of. Homer, master bookkeeper, closes the passage with Euchenor of Corinth, who had a prophet for a father and two quoted prices for his one life: die of disease at home, or die by a spear at Troy. He chose the spear: and dodged, Homer adds, the fine for staying home. Even fate, in this poem, gets processed through accounting.

And Hector knows none of it. He is still hammering the center at the spot where he first broke the gate, unaware that his left wing is being eaten. It takes Polydamas: again: to tell him, and the speech is the best thing an advisor says in the Iliad. It starts with the org-chart problem stated flat: “Because heaven has so richly endowed you with the arts of war, you think that you must therefore excel others in counsel; but you cannot thus claim preeminence in all things.” Heaven makes one man a soldier, another a dancer, another a singer: and in another it plants sound judgment, “of which men reap fruit to the saving of many.” Then the advice itself: the fight has hemmed you in like a circle of fire; half your men are standing off, the rest scattered and outnumbered. Pull back, gather the chieftains, decide together: press or withdraw: because there is a man at the ships “who is never weary of battle,” and he will not hold aloof much longer. Polydamas is the only Trojan pricing Achilles into any plan. Hector’s response is the trap of every advisor’s career: the words please him well. He tells Polydamas to gather the chieftains himself: delegating the advice back to the man who gave it: and charges off to the front to keep doing exactly what he was doing. On the way he takes the roll of his commanders. Deiphobus? Wounded. Helenus? Wounded. Adamas, Asius? Dead. Othryoneus? Dead. He finds Paris, the one brother still standing on the wing that broke, screams that Ilius is undone: and Paris, who for once has been fighting the whole time, says so mildly and follows him back in.

Pause: check yourself

Polydamas tells Hector to halt and regroup: and warns that the man at the ships won’t sit out forever. Homer says the words pleased Hector well. What happens to the advice?

Option b is the pattern from Book Twelve, and it’s where the story is headed: which is exactly why it’s wrong here. This time Hector doesn’t argue; he agrees, warmly. Then he tells Polydamas to gather the chieftains himself and sprints back to the front to keep doing what he was doing. Agreement turns out to be the cheapest way to dismiss an advisor: no debate, no ownership, no change. And note what the advice contained: Polydamas is the only Trojan who prices Achilles into a plan. Being right early, agreed with, and unheeded, book after book: the advisor’s tragedy compounds, and Troy is paying the interest.

The book ends with the two best fighters on the field shouting at each other, which is what happens when neither can move the line. Ajax dares Hector to come closer: the day is near when you’ll pray for horses “swifter than hawks” to carry you back to your city. A bird flies by on the right: the cheap, perfect omen: and the Greeks roar. Hector answers with the tell of a man borrowing status: would that I were as sure of being Zeus’s own son, with Hera for my mother, and honored like Athena and Apollo, as I am that this day destroys the Achaeans. Nobody certain of the sunrise phrases it that way. Then both hosts scream, and Homer places the sound precisely: the cry goes up “to heaven and to the brightness of Zeus’s presence.” Up, that is, to the god who decided all of this was settled and is looking at Thrace. The noise of the deadlock rising, unheard, toward the one pair of eyes that could end it: that’s Book Thirteen. Whoever holds the boss’s gaze holds the war. Right now, nobody holds it at all.

TLDR

With Zeus looking away, Poseidon secretly stiffens the Greek defense at the ships. Idomeneus and Meriones shine in scattered fighting, and Hector finally listens to Polydamas long enough to regroup.

Who’s who

Poseidon

God of the sea, in disguise

Zeus’s brother and, he mutters, his equal in everything but age. The moment Zeus looks away he comes up from the sea, borrows a mortal’s face, and rallies the Greeks: defiance engineered to be deniable.

Zeus

King of the gods, looking elsewhere

He drives Hector to the ships, decides the matter is settled, and turns his keen eyes to Thrace: certain no god would dare touch the war behind his back. The whole book happens inside that assumption.

Hector

Commander of Troy

Still spending yesterday’s momentum. He batters the center like a boulder crashing downhill while his left wing quietly bleeds out: and only learns it when he goes looking for brothers who aren’t there.

Ajax

Bulwark of the Greeks

Anchor of the center: he yields to no man in mortal mould if bronze and great stones can overthrow him. He trades taunts with Hector at the day’s end and gets a lucky bird at exactly the right moment.

Idomeneus

Captain of the Cretans

Gray-haired, slower than he was, and honest about both. He picks the weakest wing on purpose and kills like a man who has replaced speed with knowing exactly where to stand.

Meriones

Idomeneus’s squire

He breaks his spear on Deiphobus’s shield and walks back for another: into a lecture on courage from his captain. Then he spends the afternoon proving every word of it.

Deiphobus

Prince of Troy

Hector’s brother, working the left wing behind a round shield. He avenges Asius, misses Idomeneus twice, recruits Aeneas with kinship guilt, and leaves the field with a spear through his arm.

Polydamas

Hector’s advisor

Made for counsel as Hector was made for war: he says so to Hector’s face. His advice: stop, regroup, count your men, because the man at the ships won’t sit out much longer. He is praised and not obeyed.

Menelaus

King of Sparta

He puts a spear through one prince’s bow hand and another man’s forehead, then stands over the body asking Zeus how anyone can be insatiate of battle. Sleep, love, song, and dance all pall: war, somehow, doesn’t.

Helenus

Trojan prince and augur

The seer fights with a Thracian sword and a bow this time. Menelaus’s spear goes clean through the hand that draws the string, and the Trojan left loses its priest and a prince in one stroke.

Teucer

The Greek archer

Telamon’s other son draws first blood: he kills Imbrius, Priam’s son-in-law, and nearly trades spears with Hector over the body.

Othryoneus

Cassandra’s suitor

He bid for the fairest of Priam’s daughters with no gifts of wooing: just a promise to drive the Greeks from Troy. Idomeneus kills him mid-stride and renegotiates, sarcastically, over the corpse.
