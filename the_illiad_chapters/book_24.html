The Iliad, retold

Book 24

Book Twenty-Four, after Samuel Butler’s translation: Priam crosses the Greek camp, kisses the hands that killed his son, and Achilles remembers how to return a body.

Book Twenty-Four begins after the victory should have ended. Hector is dead, but Achilles cannot stop using the body. For twelve days he drags it around Patroclus’ tomb, and for twelve days Apollo keeps it whole. The body has become the last battlefield, and the gods finally decide that even rage needs an outer wall.

Zeus sends Thetis to her son with the order: take ransom and return the corpse. Achilles agrees almost at once, which is one of the book’s surprises. The obstacle was never that he needed to be convinced by argument. He needed the command to come from a place still authorized to interrupt grief.

Pause: check yourself

Achilles agrees quickly when Thetis tells him to ransom Hector. What does that reveal?

The speed matters. Achilles is not persuaded by a new moral insight yet; he accepts an order through Thetis. The human change comes later, face to face with Priam.

Then Zeus sends Iris to Priam: load a wagon with treasure, go alone into the Greek camp, and ask the killer for your son. Hecuba protests because any sane person would. Priam goes anyway. He prays, receives the bird-sign, and Hermes meets him in disguise, guiding him through the guards as if the whole Greek army were a locked building and the god had the key.

Pause: check yourself

Priam goes to Achilles with treasure, but the decisive risk is personal. Why must he go himself?

The ransom matters, but the treasure is not the argument that breaks the scene open. Priam's body is. A messenger could carry goods; only a father can make Achilles remember fathers.

Priam enters, clasps Achilles’ knees, and kisses the hands that killed his son. Then he says the one name that can cross the distance: remember your father, Peleus. The speech does not ask Achilles to love Hector. It asks him to imagine an old man far away, waiting for a son who will not come home. Achilles weeps for Peleus and Patroclus; Priam weeps for Hector. They are not sharing one grief. They are sitting close enough for two griefs to recognize each other.

Pause: check yourself

Priam tells Achilles to remember Peleus. Why does this work?

Prophecy is in the background, but Priam does not lead with fate. He leads with kinship. Achilles can still refuse Hector the enemy; he cannot fully refuse the father whose suffering mirrors Peleus'.

Achilles answers with the two urns of Zeus: one full of good, one full of evil, and mortals receive from them in mixtures they do not control. It is not comfort exactly. It is a way to put both men back inside the human condition instead of the winner-loser frame. Then he has the body washed and wrapped outside Priam’s sight, because mercy has details. Do not make the father watch the handling of what is left.

Pause: check yourself

Achilles hides the washing and wrapping of Hector's body from Priam. What is the mercy in that detail?

The point is not etiquette. Achilles understands that returning the body can still wound if done brutally. The mercy is procedural: arrange the necessary work so Priam receives his son, not another spectacle.

They eat. That may be the most radical verb in the book. Achilles tells the story of Niobe, who ate even after her children were killed, because grief does not abolish the body. He gives Priam a truce for the funeral, and Hermes wakes the old man before dawn to guide him safely home. Troy receives Hector and the poem gives the last public words to the women: Andromache for the wife and child left exposed, Hecuba for the mother, Helen for the outsider he protected. Then the city burns and buries its defender. The Iliad does not end with Troy saved. It ends with one necessary decency performed before Troy falls.

Last one

The poem ends with Hector's funeral, not Achilles' death or Troy's fall. What kind of ending is that?

The parallel with Patroclus matters, but the larger closure is human rather than strategic. Troy is doomed and Achilles is doomed. What can still be repaired is the treatment of the dead and the recognition of the living who mourn them.

TLDR

For twelve days Achilles tries to abuse Hector’s body, but Apollo keeps it in pristine condition. The gods debate over what to do about this situation, and Zeus decides that. Achilles must be convinced to ransom the body. Zeus summons Thetis to Olympus, and orders her to speak to Achilles about ransoming Hector and to convey to him the displeasure of the gods. This Thetis does, and Achilles readily agrees. Zeus then sends Iris to tell Priam that he must go to Achilles in person and ask for his son’s body. Over the protestations of Hecuba, Priam prepares to enter the Greek camp. Hecuba tells Priam to pour a libation and pray to Zeus before starting out. Priam obeys, and Zeus signals acceptance of the prayer with a bird-sign. On Zeus’ orders, Hermes poses as one of Achilles ‘men, and guides Priam through the Greek camp to Achilles’ shelter. On their arrival, Hermes reveals himself and departs, after reassuring Priam that Hector’s body is still unblemished. Priam enters and supplicates Achilles, strongly reminding him of his own beloved father Peleus. Together Achilles and Priam weep, each for his own. Then Achilles replies to Priam’s speech, voicing admiration for his courage in coming, and painting a famous picture of the inconstancy of mortal happiness (the two urns of Zeus). Achilles orders his servants to clean and wrap the body for moving, but to keep it out of Priam’s sight. He apologizes to Patroclus for breaking his promise and allowing Hector to be buried. Achilles then tells Priam the story of Niobe, whose twelve children all died as a result of her hubris, to illustrate the principle that even amidst great sadness people must eat. They feast, and Achilles agrees to restrain the Achaeans for twelve days while the Trojans have Hector’s funeral. Priam goes to sleep outside of Achilles ‘shelter, until Hermes awakens him in the middle of the night and guides him back to the city. The three women closest to Hector now take turns leading the lament. First is Andromache, who repeats her earlier theme of the hard future ahead for herself and Astyanax, then closes with a wish that the last moments she had with Hector had been more intimate. Second is Hecuba, who asserts that Hector was her favorite son. Third is Helen, who praises Hector for having befriended her when others were harsh. The Trojans gather wood for the pyre for nine days. On the tenth they immolate Hector, and on the eleventh they bury him.

Who’s who

Achilles

The man who returns the body

He begins still abusing Hector's corpse, accepts Zeus' order through Thetis, and finally meets Priam as one mourner facing another.

Priam

Father of Hector

He crosses the Greek camp with ransom, clasps Achilles' knees, and kisses the hands that killed his son to get Hector home.

Hector

The body at issue

Dead but still central: protected by Apollo, washed by Achilles' servants, mourned by Troy, and finally burned and buried.

Thetis

Messenger to Achilles

She carries Zeus' command to her son: the gods are angry, and Hector must be ransomed back.

Zeus

Setter of the final limit

He decides that Achilles' treatment of the body has gone far enough and arranges both sides of the ransom.

Hermes

Guide through danger

Disguised as a young Greek, he escorts Priam through the camp, then wakes and guides him home before dawn.

Hecuba

Mother of Hector

She begs Priam not to go, then later leads one of the laments over the son Achilles finally returns.

Andromache

Widow of Hector

Her lament turns from Hector to the exposed future of Astyanax, the child now left without his city's shield.

Helen

The protected outsider

Her final lament remembers Hector as the one Trojan man who answered her shame with kindness.

Patroclus

The grief behind Achilles

Achilles apologizes to him for returning Hector, showing that the dead friend still governs his sense of debt.

Apollo

Protector of the corpse

For twelve days he keeps Hector's body untouched while Achilles tries to dishonor it.

Peleus

The remembered father

Priam invokes him to make Achilles see the old man in front of him through the father waiting far away.
