The Iliad, retold

Book 22

Book Twenty-Two, after Samuel Butler’s translation: a duel decided by fraud, a body dragged behind a chariot, and bath water heating for a man already dead.

Every soldier Troy has left is now inside the wall. Homer opens on the routed army pouring through the gates “scared like fawns,” wiping off sweat, leaning against the battlements to drink. Then the camera finds the one man still outside. Butler’s sentence says that “stern fate” bade Hector stay where he was, before Ilius and the Scaean gates: and then the poem spends its whole length showing you the actual mechanism, which is not fate, and is worse.

First it settles a bill from the last book. Achilles has spent the rout chasing a Trojan who turns out to be Apollo in costume: a decoy, run to pull him away from the walls while the army escaped. Now the god drops the disguise and teases: why chase me, who am immortal, “you, who are but man”? The answer tells you where Achilles is now: you have robbed me of great glory and saved the Trojans at no risk to yourself: I would indeed have my revenge if it were in my power. He is threatening a god to his face, and means it. Then he turns for the city like the winning horse in a chariot race, and Priam sees him first: a gleam crossing the plain like the star men call Orion’s Hound, brightest of them all and the worst omen, because it brings fever in its train. Homer’s similes have been keeping the moral books all poem; this one files Achilles under natural disaster.

What follows is one of the poem’s cruelest inventions: the parents on the wall, close enough to plead, arguing for their son’s life while the killer closes. Priam tears his gray hair and begins like a negotiator: come inside, you will have the glory of guarding Trojan men and Trojan women, don’t hand this man his triumph: and then stops negotiating and simply shows Hector the future: sons slain, daughters hauled off, infants dashed to the earth, and at the end of it an old man torn apart at his own gates by his own dogs: “hounds that I myself reared and fed at my own table,” who will lap his blood and lie sated in his doorway. A young man dead in battle is seemly, he says; everything shows honorably on him. But dogs defiling an old man’s gray head and beard “and all that men hide for shame”: nothing in the world is more pitiable. He is not describing the fall of Troy in general. He is looking at his own corpse.

Hecuba goes below argument entirely. She bares her bosom, points to the breast, and calls in the oldest debt there is: if I ever gave you comfort here, honor it now and fight from inside the wall: because if he kills you out there, neither I nor your richly-dowered wife will ever weep over your body; the dogs will have you by the ships. Homer’s line for the result is flat as a door closing: they moved not the heart of Hector. He waits for Achilles the way a serpent full of poison waits coiled at its den: the defender of the city rendered, for the space of one simile, as the snake in the wall.

Then Homer opens his head, and we get the real answer. “If I go within the gates,” Hector says to himself, “Polydamas will be the first to heap reproach upon me”: Polydamas, who urged him to bring the army inside the walls the night Achilles came back, and was overruled. Now that my folly has destroyed the host, I dare not look Trojan men and Trojan women in the face, lest a worse man should say, “Hector has ruined us by his self-confidence.” Read it carefully: not fate, not courage, not the defense of Troy: which, as every voice on the wall correctly points out, would be better served by his coming inside. What holds him at the gate is the anticipated sound of an I-told-you-so. In Book Six he said the same thing to his wife: with what face could I look on the Trojans if I hung back: but then it was hypothetical, the price of a role he was still equal to. Now the ledger shows real losses, his fault, and the role collects.

The soliloquy’s second half is stranger and more human: the fantasy of negotiation. What if I lay down the shield, lean the spear against the wall, and walk out to meet him: offer to give back Helen, and with her half of everything the city contains, sworn to by the princes, nothing hidden? For a few lines the war becomes a settlement conference. Then he catches himself: he would kill me “then and there, as easily as though I were a woman, when I had off my armour. There is no parleying with him from some rock or oak tree, as young men and maidens prattle with one another.” Notice where his mind reached for a picture of safe talk: courting couples by a rock. Even his image of parley comes from peacetime, and this is the last time he will see it. Better to fight at once, he decides, and learn to which of us Zeus will give the victory.

Pause: check yourself

Priam shows Hector his own corpse; Hecuba shows him the breast that nursed him. He stays outside anyway, and his soliloquy tells us why. What actually holds him at the gate?

The narrator does say “stern fate”: and then Homer immediately opens Hector’s head and shows machinery, not destiny: “Polydamas will be the first to heap reproach upon me… I dare not look Trojan men and Trojan women in the face.” He is held not by the gods but by an imagined quote from “a worse man.” Duty fails even faster: guarding Troy argues for going inside, and Priam says exactly that: come in and be our guardian. This is Book Six’s answer completing itself. The job called “Hector” exists only in other people’s eyes, and today it forecloses.

Achilles arrives like Ares himself, the ash spear over his right shoulder, bronze flashing like fire or the rising sun. And Hector: the man who has just talked himself into standing: runs. Homer neither hides it nor sneers at it: fear fell on him, the falcon swooped, the dove fled before it. Then the poet’s own verdict, evenhanded as a race official’s: good was the man that fled, but better far was he that followed after. They run the wagon-road under the wall, past the lookout station, past the weather-beaten wild fig-tree, to the two springs that feed the Scamander: one running warm, steam standing off it like smoke from a fire; the other, even in midsummer, cold as hail.

And there, mid-chase, Homer stops. Beside the springs stand “the goodly washing-troughs of stone, where in the time of peace, before the coming of the Achaeans, the wives and fair daughters of the Trojans used to wash their clothes.” The most consequential footrace in literature pauses for laundry. It is not a digression; it is the stakes. The peacetime city isn’t somewhere else: it is embedded in the ground the war is being run on, one landmark among the landmarks, and the two men sprint past it three full times: wives, daughters, clean linen, unhurried mornings, sitting beside the racecourse in stone. Then Homer prices the race itself: they ran as men run at funeral games, but the prize was no tripod or ox-hide: they ran for the life of Hector. And their locked speeds get the poem’s eeriest simile: as in a dream, where the pursuer can never quite catch, and the pursued can never quite escape.

Pause: check yourself

Mid-chase, with Hector running for his life, Homer describes the stone troughs where Trojan wives washed clothes in the days of peace. Why put laundry in the poem’s most desperate mile?

Homer does use landmarks to track the laps: but the troughs get details no map needs: the steam off the warm spring, the wives and daughters, “before the coming of the Achaeans.” That last phrase is the tell. The domestic past isn’t contrast for its own sake (the pacing reading); it’s buried in the racecourse like a fossil: the two men are literally running over what the war is about. Homer then says the price out loud: not a tripod, not an ox-hide: the prize was the life of Hector, which is to say, everything the washing-tanks stood for.

The gods watch the laps like spectators with money down. Zeus, who genuinely pities, says so: my heart grieves for Hector, who burned the thigh-bones of many a heifer to me on Ida and on the citadel: shall we save him, or let him fall? Athena answers with a lawyer’s edge: this man’s doom “has long been decreed”: do as you will, but the rest of us will not praise you. And Zeus folds at once: take heart, my child, “I did not speak in full earnest.” Do as you are minded. Below, meanwhile, Achilles is running the honor accounting at a dead sprint: shaking his head at his own army so that no one throws a dart at Hector, “lest another might win the glory of having hit him and he might himself come in second.” On the fourth pass by the springs, Zeus lifts the golden scales, sets a doom in each pan, and holds the balance by the middle. Hector’s side sinks toward the house of Hades: and Apollo, who has been sustaining his strength and nerving his running the whole chase, leaves him. No farewell, no last word. Gods do not stay past the verdict.

What Athena does next is the coldest act any god commits in the poem. She tells Achilles to stop and take breath: I will bring him to you: and goes to Hector wearing the shape and voice of his brother Deiphobus: dear brother, let us stand and meet him together. Hector’s reply breaks your heart before the trap even closes: you were always dearest to me of all my brothers, and I shall rate you higher still now, for “you have ventured outside the wall for my sake, when all the others remain inside.” The lie is precision-tooled. Fake Deiphobus says his father and mother went down on their knees begging him to stay, and he came anyway: Athena has fabricated, out of air, exactly the loyalty Hector just refused his own parents, and handed it to him as a mirror. Butler’s verb is exact: thus did the goddess inveigle him.

So Hector turns and stands, and offers what fighters in this poem have always offered: terms. Let our gods be witnesses: if Zeus gives me the day, I will not treat your body in any unseemly way; I take the armor and give the dead man back. Do the same for me. It is the minimal deal, the floor of civilization: not mercy, just funerals. Achilles glares at him. Fool, prate not to me about covenants. There can be no covenants between men and lions, wolves and lambs can never be of one mind. Until one of us falls and gluts Ares with his blood, there is nothing to discuss. Remember Book Six: two enemies found one inherited thread of obligation in the middle of a battle and traded armor on the strength of it. That machinery: oath, ransom, guest-friendship, the whole apparatus that keeps enemies inside one species: has burned out of Achilles completely, and he says so in species terms: he has reclassified himself as another kind of animal. He even discloses the fix, openly, before the first throw: Pallas Athena will forthwith vanquish you by my spear.

Pause: check yourself

Hector proposes terms any duel so far would honor: winner returns the loser’s body. Achilles answers that there are no covenants between men and lions. What is the poem measuring with that refusal?

The decay reading tempts most, because the poem has tracked it since “not even the unborn” in Book Six: but two books from now Achilles himself will hand this same body back to Priam, so the code isn’t dead in the world; it’s dead in him, for now. He frames the refusal in species terms: lions and men, wolves and lambs: announcing that he has left the group that makes agreements. Book Six staged the exact opposite scene: Glaucus and Diomedes finding one thread of obligation mid-battle and trading armor on it. And the trap reading has the scene backwards: the man with divine backing is Achilles, and he says so out loud.

Achilles throws first and misses: Hector crouches, the spear passes over him and sticks in the ground. Athena plucks it out and returns it, invisibly, while Hector: not seeing: taunts the thrower: you missed, you false-tongued liar; and you will not plant your spear in the back of a runaway: drive it through my chest as I come straight at you. Then his own throw, and it is true: dead center of the wondrous shield. It rebounds. He calls to Deiphobus for a second spear: and there is no man there. And he understands everything at once, and says it with the calm of a man reading his own audit: the gods have lured me on to my destruction… Deiphobus is within the wall, and Athena has inveigled me; death is now indeed exceedingly near at hand, and there is no way out of it. So Zeus and Apollo have willed it: the gods who before were always ready to protect me. No wailing, no bargaining with heaven. One sentence of recognition, then the pivot on which his whole dignity turns: let me not then die ingloriously and without a struggle, but let me first do some great thing that shall be told among men hereafter. He draws the sword and comes on like an eagle dropping through cloud. The duel that decides the Iliad is rigged; the poem has shown you every component of the cheat and does not flinch from any of it. What it grants Hector is the one unrigged thing left: knowing everything, he still charges.

Achilles picks his spot like a craftsman. Hector is sheathed head to foot in the armor he stripped from Patroclus: Achilles’ own old armor; the man dies wearing the evidence: and the one person alive who knows that armor’s single gap is the man scanning it for one. The throat, where the collarbones part the neck from the shoulders. The spear goes through: “but it did not sever his windpipe, so that he could still speak.” Homer engineers the wound itself so the scene can finish talking. Hector, dying, asks once more for the floor of civilization: I pray you by your life, your knees, your parents: take the gold my father and mother will weigh out, and send my body home for the fire. And Achilles: Dog, talk not to me neither of knees nor parents; would that I could be as sure of being able to cut your flesh into pieces and eat it raw: not for twentyfold ransom, not for your weight in gold, will your mother ever lay you out and mourn you; the dogs and birds will have you utterly. Hector spends his last breath on a prophecy: look to it that I bring not heaven’s anger upon you, on the day Paris and Apollo kill you, valiant as you are, at the Scaean gates: this gate, the one whose shadow they are standing in. And Achilles answers his own death notice without a flicker: Die; for my part I will accept my fate whensoever Zeus and the other gods see fit to send it.

Pause: check yourself

Hector calls for Deiphobus, finds no one, and understands: Athena tricked him, the gods have called him, death is near. What does he do first with that knowledge?

He does eventually plead for his body, and his dying breath is the prophecy: both other options quote the scene truthfully, just out of order. The first thing he does on understanding is neither: “let me not then die ingloriously and without a struggle, but let me first do some great thing that shall be told among men hereafter”: and then the eagle-dive at a man the gods have already armed twice. The duel was rigged and he knows it; his dignity is that the knowledge changes nothing. The shame that pinned him outside the gate converts, in his last minute, into the choice of how the story will tell it.

What follows the death is worse than the death. The Achaeans come running up to view Hector’s “wondrous strength and beauty”: and no one, Homer says, came near him without giving him a fresh wound. The joke goes around the circle as the spears go in: easier to handle him now than when he was flinging fire onto our ships. Achilles starts drafting strategy over the body: should we storm the city now, while they reel?: and stops himself mid-sentence, because grief interrupts even his tactics: why argue with myself, while Patroclus lies at the ships unburied, unmourned. Then the thing itself. He pierces the sinews of both feet from heel to ankle, threads ox-hide thongs through the slits, lashes the body to his chariot, loads the captured armor, mounts, and whips the horses: “letting the head trail upon the ground.” Dust rises off the dark hair; the head “once so comely” plows the earth of its own country. Homer names who signed the order: Zeus had now delivered him into the hands of his foes, to be outraged in his own land.

On the wall, Hecuba tears her hair and flings her veil away with a cry; Priam makes his piteous moan; and the whole city takes up the wail: and Homer reaches for the exact right comparison: it was as though all of frowning Ilius “were being smirched with fire.” The city grieves this one death as the sack itself, because it is; everything after is paperwork. Priam has to be physically held back: he grovels in the mire, calling each man around him by name: let be, my friends, and suffer me to go alone to the ships of the Achaeans and beseech this cruel and terrible man. His own father is old, as I am; perhaps he will respect the feeling of his fellow-men. Hold that thought: it is Book Twenty-Four arriving two books early. Would that my son had died in my arms, he says; then his mother and I could at least have wept over him. Hecuba’s lament is simpler: you were my glory night and day, and a tower of strength to all in Troy; men and women hailed you as a god.

And then Homer cuts to the one person in Troy who does not know. Andromache has heard nothing: no messenger even to tell her that her husband stayed outside the gates. She is deep in the house at her loom, weaving a double purple web and embroidering it with flowers, and she has just told her maids to set the great tripod on the fire, “so as to have a warm bath ready for Hector when he came out of battle: poor woman, she knew not that he was now beyond the reach of baths.” This is the cruelest device Homer owns: not the death but the lag. Her ordinary hour keeps running: flowers going into cloth, water warming on the fire: inside a world that ended an hour ago on the far side of the wall. Then the wailing reaches the inner room, and the shuttle drops from her hands. And listen to her guess, before she knows anything: I fear Achilles has cut off my husband alone on the plain: has ended “the reckless daring which possessed him, for he would never remain with the body of his men, but would dash on far in front, foremost of them all in valour.” She diagnoses his death precisely, sight unseen. She always knew. In Book Six she said it to his face: your own courage will kill you. Now she runs for the wall “like a maniac”: the same frantic run the housekeeper described back then, when the dash to the wall still ended at the Scaean gates with a laugh, a kiss, and a helmet set gleaming on the ground. This time she reaches the battlements in time to see the horses dragging him toward the ships. Night shrouds her eyes; she falls backward; and off her head fly the bindings: frontlet, net, plaited band, and the veil golden Aphrodite gave her on the day Hector led her from Eetion’s house with countless wedding gifts. The marriage arrived with that veil. It leaves with it.

Her lament, when she can speak, is the strangest and truest in the poem, because it is not about Hector’s glory and barely about her own ruin. It is about a boy’s place at a table. Astyanax’s future arrives not as death: the audience knows that worse truth: but as a catalog of small social humiliations: he will go about among his father’s old friends “plucking one by the cloak and another by the shirt”; someone, pitying, may hold a cup to his mouth long enough to wet his lips but not the roof of his mouth; and a child with both parents living will drive him from the feast with blows and taunts: out with you; you have no father here: this, for the boy who used to eat none but the daintiest morsels, sitting on his father’s knees. She understands her world exactly: for an orphan, status is the food supply, and the account closes the day the father dies. The Trojans, she says, call him Astyanax: lord of the city: “because you, O Hector, were the only defence of their gates and battlements.” In Book Six the helmet came off, and for one minute on the wall there was a man underneath the role. The city had named the baby after the job. Now the job is finished, and everything named for it: the son, the wife, the gates: is forfeit with it. She ends with the only gift she has left: his fine raiment, made by women’s hands, useless now to a body lying naked by the ships. She will burn it in his honor, before the Trojans, men and women: an honor made entirely of smoke, which is what Troy has left to give.

Last one

Andromache is weaving flowers into cloth and heating Hector’s bath when the wail reaches her. Why does Homer build the discovery scene around the bath water?

The inverted-ritual reading is real, and readers have felt it for millennia: bath water for a man “beyond the reach of baths” does shadow the washing his body won’t get. But Homer’s own aside points elsewhere: poor woman, she knew not. The horror isn’t the water’s future use; it’s that she is still tending a husband who has been dead for an hour: still living inside a world that has already ended. The Book Six callback is there too (loom and bath are exactly the duties he assigned her), but it works because of the lag, not instead of it. Homer knows many ways to hurt you; the cruelest is information arriving late.

TLDR

Hector stays outside Troy, runs from Achilles, is deceived by Athena, and dies when the divine setup leaves him without help. Achilles refuses the body bargain, drags Hector behind his chariot, and Troy learns what it has lost.

Who’s who

Hector

Commander of Troy

Alone outside the gate while all Troy watches from the wall. What keeps him there is not courage but shame: Polydamas will say I told you so: and when the gods cheat him, he charges anyway.

Achilles

Son of Peleus

He refuses covenants, refuses ransom, wishes he could eat his enemy’s flesh raw: and takes the prophecy of his own death at the same gate without breaking stride. Patroclus’s killer is dead; nothing is better.

Priam

King of Troy

First to spot the gleam on the plain. He tears his gray hair on the wall, foresees his own dogs lapping his blood at his own door, and by nightfall must be held back from going alone to the killer’s ships.

Hecuba

Queen of Troy

She bares the breast that nursed Hector and asks him to honor it. It moves him no more than his father’s tears, and she is left to fling her veil and lead the city’s wailing.

Andromache

Hector’s wife

The last in Troy to know. She is at the loom weaving flowers, with bath water heating for her husband, when the wail reaches her: and her lament is not for his glory but for their son’s future.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

She wears Deiphobus’s shape to talk Hector into standing, then hands Achilles back his missed spear. The climactic duel of Western literature is decided by her fraud.

Apollo

God of the silver bow

He spends the first half of the chapter saving Hector: playing decoy, feeding strength into his legs: and the moment the scales tip, he leaves. Gods do not stay past the verdict.

Zeus

King of the gods

He pities Hector, who burned so many thigh-bones in his honor, and wonders aloud about saving him. Then he hangs the golden scales, and lets what sinks, sink.

Deiphobus

Hector’s brother

The real one never leaves the city. Athena borrows his shape and voice to promise Hector a second spear, and when Hector turns and calls for one, no man is there.

Astyanax

Hector’s infant son

The baby who screamed at the helmet in Book Six. His mother maps his orphan future in small humiliations: a pulled cloak, a sip that wets the lips, a child shouting “you have no father here.”

Patroclus

The debt being paid

Dead since Book Sixteen, he governs every line Achilles speaks. Hector dies wearing the armor he stripped from Patroclus’s body: and the wound goes into the one gap it leaves open.

Polydamas

The feared told-you-so

He advised retreat behind the walls; Hector overruled him and lost the army. He never appears in this book. He doesn’t have to: the reproach Hector imagines from him is what holds Hector outside the gate.
