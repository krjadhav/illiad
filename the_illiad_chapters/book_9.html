The Iliad, retold

Book 9

Book Nine, after Samuel Butler’s translation — Agamemnon prices remorse, three good speakers try three different keys, and Achilles discovers that no gift can buy back trust.

Book Nine is the night the Greeks finally try the obvious thing. The Trojans are camped on the plain, their fires visible from the ships, and Agamemnon does what he does whenever pressure becomes unbearable: he proposes going home. This time Diomedes stops him cold. Stay or leave as you please, he says, but I will remain until Troy falls. Nestor translates the rebuke into policy: post guards, feed the chiefs, and send an embassy to Achilles.

The offer is enormous and also exactly shaped like the original mistake. Agamemnon will return Briseis, swear he has not touched her, add gold, tripods, horses, women, cities, and future marriage into his house. He can price the insult down to the last cauldron. What he cannot do is make the price feel like respect.

Pause — check yourself

Agamemnon offers gifts, Briseis, cities, and marriage. Why is the offer still unstable?

The timing matters and the scale is meant to impress, but the deeper failure is mechanical. Agamemnon still thinks the thing taken can be replaced by more things. Achilles heard the first insult as a public statement of worth; a larger inventory does not erase the statement.

Odysseus makes the polished version of the case. He starts with fear: Hector will burn the ships. Then he borrows Peleus’ voice, reminding Achilles of the father who sent him to Troy and told him to master his temper. Only then does he recite the gifts, editing out the clumsiest part of Agamemnon’s speech and replacing it with a cleaner bait: come back and kill Hector.

Pause — check yourself

Odysseus quietly edits Agamemnon’s offer before delivering it. What is the craft in that edit?

Odysseus does not hide Briseis; the oath about her is part of the pitch. His real skill is translation. He takes Agamemnon’s awkward, status-heavy offer and recasts it in Achilles’ language: danger, father, glory, Hector.

Achilles answers by changing the accounting system. The brave and the coward die alike, he says. A man can replace cattle, tripods, and horses, but once his life has crossed the teeth it does not come back. Then he says the quiet part of the whole poem aloud: if the Greeks can fight for Helen, why can he not fight for Briseis? Their cause is a woman taken from a husband. His grievance is a woman taken from him. The difference is not logic. It is rank.

Pause — check yourself

Achilles says cattle and tripods can be won back, but life cannot. What has shifted in him?

The speech is not a bluff about getting a higher bid. It attacks the bidding system. Achilles is the poem's greatest earner of honor, and here he asks whether honor is worth dying for when the people who distribute it can also steal it.

Phoenix tries a different route: not tactics, not prizes, but childhood. He raised Achilles in Peleus’ house, and he speaks like a man asking not a warrior but a son to come back inside the human circle. His myth of Ruin and Prayers is a social theory. Injury runs ahead, fast and destructive; apologies come limping after it. Wise men receive them anyway, because a world where no one can be reconciled is unlivable.

Pause — check yourself

Phoenix tells Achilles about Ruin, Prayers, and Meleager. What is his real argument?

Phoenix uses myth because direct command has already failed. His pressure is social: anger may begin as justice, but if it refuses every repair, it becomes exile. Meleager saves his city too late to receive the gifts; Phoenix wants Achilles to return before that happens.

Last comes Ajax, and his bluntness is a relief. People accept blood-money even for a murdered brother, he says. You have been offered more than anyone has ever offered. Be gracious to your friends under your roof. It is the simplest and strongest appeal because it names the norm directly: society survives by letting compensation end things.

Achilles still refuses. He will not fight until Hector reaches the ships and the fire touches the Greek hulls. The embassy goes back with the answer no one wanted, and Diomedes closes the night with the only practical response left: stop begging him; arm at dawn.

Last one

Ajax says even murder can be compensated, but Achilles still refuses. What does that refusal show?

Ajax is right about the norm, which is why his speech matters. The problem is that norms need a trusted table of values. Achilles no longer trusts Agamemnon, or the army that let Agamemnon take the prize, to measure his worth fairly.

TLDR

Agamemnon sends Odysseus, Phoenix, and Ajax with gifts and arguments for Achilles. Each appeal fails because Achilles no longer trusts honor as payment, and he refuses to fight until the Trojans reach the ships.

Who’s who

Agamemnon

Commander-in-chief

He finally admits ruin but translates remorse into an inventory: Briseis, gold, horses, women, cities, marriage. He can offer payment more easily than changed standing.

Achilles

The holdout

He hears three forms of persuasion and rejects all of them, because the insult taught him that honor can be confiscated by the same men who award it.

Odysseus

First ambassador

He makes the clever case: danger at the ships, Peleus speaking through memory, gifts edited into their strongest form, and the chance to kill Hector.

Phoenix

Foster father

He argues from intimacy and myth: Ruin runs fast, Prayers limp after it, and a man who rejects every repair may win his grievance and lose the world.

Ajax

Plain speaker

He strips the embassy down to its social core: even murder can be compensated, so take the gifts and honor your friends. It still fails.

Nestor

Planner of the embassy

He turns panic into process: guards, feast, gifts, ambassadors. His counsel gives the Greeks one last peaceful route back to Achilles.

Diomedes

The man for dawn

He refuses Agamemnon's flight plan at the start and refuses more pleading at the end. If Achilles will not come, the army must fight without him.

Briseis

The returned prize

She is the named object around which the insult was staged. Agamemnon can return her body and swear an oath; he cannot undo what taking her announced.

Peleus

Achilles' father

Absent from the camp but present in every appeal to home, restraint, and the two futures Achilles keeps measuring against each other.

Helen

The public cause

Achilles uses her to expose the double standard: the Greeks call one seized woman a war cause and another a private prize.
