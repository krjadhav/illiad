The Iliad, retold

Book 14

Book Fourteen, after Samuel Butler’s translation: the commander proposes running again, Hera dresses for a war of her own, and Zeus sleeps through the turning of the tide.

The Iliad has been asking one question since its opening quarrel: what do you do about power you cannot fight? Book Fourteen answers it twice in a single afternoon. On the ground, the Greeks face Hector with Zeus behind him, and their commander-in-chief produces the only answer he ever produces: leave. On Olympus, Hera faces Zeus himself: a husband who cannot be outvoted, outfought, or outargued, who has threatened to lay hands on gods for less than what she’s about to do. Her answer is the interesting one. You don’t fight an absolute ruler. You get him into bed and put him to sleep.

It starts with Nestor, sitting over his wine with the wounded surgeon Machaon, when the cry of battle gets too loud to talk over. He goes out to look: taking the shield of his son Thrasymedes, “for Thrasymedes had taken his father’s”: a camp so scrambled that even armor is improvised: and sees the disaster whole: the wall down, the Achaeans in flight. Homer gives the old man a simile of indecision, the sea in a heavy swell before a storm, waves standing dumb, waiting to see which wind will pick a direction. He picks his: find Agamemnon. He meets him coming the other way, up from the beached ships, with Diomedes and Odysseus: three kings leaning on their spears like crutches, all wounded, coming out to watch a battle they can’t join.

Agamemnon’s greeting is pure despair, and worth reading closely for what he counts: Hector’s boast is coming true, and the Achaeans “are in anger with me” like Achilles, refusing to fight. The army is dying and he is tallying who’s mad at him. Nestor reports the facts: the wall we relied on is fallen, Zeus himself couldn’t stop it now, and wounded men can’t fight, so let’s think. And Agamemnon thinks the one thought he has. Drag the nearest ships down into the water, moor them a little way out, wait for dark, then launch everything and go: there is nothing wrong in flying ruin even by night. It is better for a man that he should fly and be saved than be caught and killed. This is the third time. In Book Two he proposed it as a test and nearly emptied the beach by accident. In Book Nine he proposed it in earnest and was shouted down. Now, with the wall gone, he reaches for it again. Under pressure, this commander’s imagination produces exactly one shape, and it is an exit.

Odysseus “looked fiercely at him.” The rebuke that follows is the harshest thing any Greek says to Agamemnon in the poem: Wretch, you should have commanded some other and baser army, and not been ruler over us: over men allotted hard fighting from youth to old age until every one of us perishes. I despise your judgement utterly. But the speech isn’t just contempt; it’s a mechanism. Draw the ships into the water while the battle is raging, and the Achaeans will not go on fighting: they will keep turning their eyes toward the water, holding the line with half their attention on their own escape route, and the fighting withdrawal becomes a rout: your counsel, Sir captain, would be our destruction. And notice the other half of the speech: hold your peace, lest some other of the Achaeans hear you. Part of managing this commander is making sure the men never find out what he almost ordered.

Agamemnon then does the thing that is somehow both his best quality and his worst: he folds instantly. Your rebuke has stung me to the heart: I’m not ordering anyone to launch anything; someone, old or young, offer better counsel, and I shall rejoice to hear it. Diomedes offers it: after first reciting his grandfather, his father, and the family estates, because he is the youngest man present and in this culture a plan is weighed together with the pedigree of the man proposing it. The plan itself is modest and correct: we go back to the field, wounded as we are; we stand out of spear range; and we put heart into the men who’ve been nursing grudges at the edge of the fight. They go: “Agamemnon leading the way,” Homer adds, with a straight face: the commander marching at the front of a column headed in the direction his subordinates chose.

Pause: check yourself

For the third time in the poem, Agamemnon’s plan is to launch the ships and slip away in the dark. Odysseus kills it in one speech. What actually stops the retreat?

The insult is what you remember: “you should have commanded some other and baser army”: but insults have bounced off Agamemnon before, and shame alone has never changed his mind. What kills the plan is the operational argument: retreat is a visible signal, and the moment the men see hulls sliding toward the sea they will stop holding the line and start racing for a berth. Ruin, not dishonor, is the word Odysseus ends on. Note, too, what happens next: Agamemnon folds at once and asks for better counsel. That is the pattern of the whole council: this commander is not so much obeyed as managed, by subordinates who treat his despair as one more front to hold.

Poseidon has been waiting for exactly this. He comes up in the shape of an old man, takes Agamemnon by the right hand, and says the thing every Greek wants said out loud: Achilles is glad right now, watching you routed and slain, for he is utterly without remorse: may he come to a bad end. The gods aren’t finished with you; you’ll see the Trojans running for their city yet. Then he lets out a battle cry with the voice of nine or ten thousand men in it. It’s a strange combination: anonymous, and deafening: and it maps his exact constraint: he can pour courage into an entire army, but he cannot yet be seen doing it. On the crest of Ida, Zeus is watching.

Which is where Hera comes in. She watches from a peak of Olympus, glad at the sight of her brother working the lines: then turns her eyes to Ida, to her husband sitting on the summit, “and loathed him.” Homer gives her no speech, just the verb and the planning that follows it. Consider her options. She cannot fight him; he has boasted he outweighs all the gods together. She cannot outvote him; Olympus is not a democracy. She cannot persuade him; she has been trying since Book One and been threatened with unspeakable handling for it. What’s left is the one channel his power does not guard, because it never occurs to a king that it needs guarding: he wants her. She sets herself to think how she might “hoodwink” him: Butler’s word, wonderfully undersized for what she is about to do to the lord of the universe.

What follows is an arming scene. Homer gives it the same slow ceremony he gives a hero putting on bronze, piece by piece, because that is what it is. The private room Hera’s son Hephaestus built her, with a secret key no other god can turn. Ambrosia to cleanse her skin; oil so fragrant that if the flask is so much as shaken in Zeus’s bronze-floored house, “the scent pervaded the universe of heaven and earth”; her hair plaited into golden tresses; the wondrous robe Athena worked for her, embroidered with manifold devices; golden clasps; a girdle with a hundred tassels; three-pendant earrings; a new veil; sandals. She is not getting dressed. She is dressing for the front.

But for this target her own arsenal isn’t enough, so she goes to borrow the decisive weapon from: of all people: Aphrodite, patron of Troy, her enemy in the war. She even names the awkwardness up front: will you do what I ask, or refuse me because we back different armies? Then the lying tale: she is off to the world’s end to reconcile Oceanus and Tethys, the foster parents who raised her, estranged so long they no longer share a bed; a little borrowed charm would help her mend the marriage. Aphrodite’s answer is the most revealing sentence in the book: I cannot and must not refuse you, for you sleep in the arms of Zeus who is our king. And she looses from her bosom the embroidered girdle “into which all her charms had been wrought: love, desire, and that sweet flattery which steals the judgement even of the most prudent”: and hands her enemy the war, without ever learning she’s done it.

Pause: check yourself

Hera needs Aphrodite’s girdle, so she invents an errand: reconciling the estranged Oceanus and Tethys. Aphrodite backs the other side of the war. Why does she hand her enemy the weapon?

The lying tale is doing real work: it gives Aphrodite a version of events she can repeat later without blushing, which is exactly what a pretext is for. But listen to the order of her answer: “I cannot and must not refuse you” comes before any weighing of the errand, and the reason she gives is Hera’s marriage, not Hera’s story. She is answering the asker, not the request. That is the mechanism the whole book runs on: in the divine household, power flows through proximity to the king: and the wife of an absolute ruler can requisition almost anything, including, from her enemy, the tool she is about to use against him.

Next stop, Lemnos, and the strangest negotiation in the poem. Hera finds Sleep, “own brother to Death,” catches him by the hand, and makes her offer: close Zeus’s keen eyes in slumber while I hold him in my arms, and Hephaestus will forge you a golden seat that can never fall to pieces, with a footstool for your feet at dinner. The refusal she gets back is a history lesson. Any other god, gladly: the very streams of Oceanus, from whom they all proceed. Not Zeus. He tried it once before, at her bidding, the day Heracles sailed from Troy: he laid the king to rest, she raised storms and drove the hero to Cos, and Zeus woke and went “hurling the gods about all over the house,” hunting Sleep above all, ready to fling him out of the sky into the sea, gone for good: and he survived only by running to Night, “who cows both men and gods.” Even Zeus at full fury stopped at Night’s door. That is what crossing the king looks like from below: not awe: a survivor’s exact accounting of how close it came.

So Hera raises her bid to the one asset furniture can’t match: marriage to Pasithea, one of the youngest of the Graces, “whom you have always wanted to marry.” Sleep is pleased: and still doesn’t move. Swear it, he says: by the dread waters of Styx, one hand on the bounteous earth and one on the sheen of the sea, with the Titans under the world as witnesses. The only oath in the cosmos with enforcement behind it, and he won’t take a step without it. Now count what the plan has cost before Zeus so much as sees her: a lie told to Aphrodite, a bride and a binding oath paid to Sleep, favors mortgaged in two directions. This is what Homer keeps showing underneath the theology: even the queen of heaven cannot simply act. Power that can’t operate in the open has to be assembled: a coalition built link by link, every link paid for, every accomplice given a story he can live with.

Pause: check yourself

Sleep turns down a golden throne forged by Hephaestus, footstool included. He accepts marriage to Pasithea: but only after Hera swears by Styx, hands on earth and sea, Titans as witnesses. What is his negotiation telling you?

“Everyone has a price” is half right, which is what makes it tempting. But if price were the whole story, more gold would have worked, and Sleep is explicit that no furniture covers this risk: he remembers being hunted through heaven and saved only because Night took him in. What he actually does is what any survivor of a tyrant’s anger does: demand the maximum payoff and enforcement. The Styx oath is the cosmos’s one binding contract, and he will not move without it: his fear doesn’t fade, it gets collateralized. That is Zeus’s power seen from below: not reverence, but actuarial memory, priced from experience.

They travel together to Ida, mother of wild beasts, where Sleep stops short and climbs the tallest pine on the mountain, hiding in its branches in the shape of a mountain songbird: the one the gods call Chalcis and men call Cymindis. Hera walks up Gargarus alone, and Zeus sees her: and desire takes him “as when they had first enjoyed each other’s embraces, and slept with one another without their dear parents knowing anything about it.” Note the simile Homer reached for: sneaking around. Then the king of gods and men opens his mouth to woo his own wife, and out comes: a list. Never has passion so overpowered me, he says: not for Ixion’s wife, nor dainty-ankled Danae, nor the daughter of Phoenix, nor Semele, nor Alcmena in Thebes: naming, as he goes, the children each of them bore him: nor queen Demeter, nor lovely Leto, nor yet yourself, before now. Seven affairs, several with offspring cited, recited to his wife as a compliment.

Pause: check yourself

Zeus courts his own wife by listing the women he has had: Ixion’s wife, Danae, Semele, Alcmena, and more: ending “with none of these was I ever so much enamoured as I now am with you.” What is Homer doing with the catalogue?

The menace reading is genuinely there: every name on that list is an old injury to the woman he is saying it to, and Hera does have to keep smiling through it. But menace requires intent, and Zeus has none: he believes he is being romantic. That gap is the joke, and the joke is load-bearing. A king who has never once needed to manage another being’s feelings has no idea how he sounds: absolute power has atrophied in him exactly the skills Hera is using on him at that moment. All poem long Zeus has been thunder and threat; the first time Homer shows him privately instead of officially, he is absurd. That is not decoration. It is the argument.

Hera keeps her head: she has to; the catalogue must be survived smiling. She answers with a performance of modesty: here, on the open top of Ida, where everything can be seen? What if some god saw us sleeping together and told the others: the scandal! Come to the room Hephaestus built me; it has good strong doors. She is steering him even now, offering an objection engineered to be overcome. And Zeus, all tenderness, solves the problem she is pretending to have: fear nothing: I will wrap us in a golden cloud so dense “that the very sun for all his bright piercing beams shall not see through it.” Then Homer spends some of his most beautiful lines: beneath them the earth sprouts new grass, dew-hung lotus and crocus and hyacinth, so soft and thick it lifts them off the ground; the golden cloud closes overhead; glittering dewdrops fall. The loveliest nature poetry in the epic, lavished on a con. And so “the sire of all things” reposes on the crest of Ida, “overcome at once by sleep and love,” his wife in his arms.

Sleep, a professional to the end, goes straight to the ships to file his report with Poseidon: now you can help the Danaans with a will: for a short time, while he’s still under; I have sent him into a sweet slumber, and Hera has beguiled him into bed. The disguise comes off the same minute. Poseidon addresses the Argives in his own voice: shall we let Hector take the ships and cover himself with glory?: and reorganizes the line on the spot: biggest shields to the best men, and if a good soldier carries a small one, swap it down to a worse man. The wounded kings themselves, too hurt to fight, hobble the ranks reallocating armor. Then he takes the front in person, sword in his fist, “keen of edge and flashing like lightning.” Homer hands him the king’s own imagery: the lightning: while the king naps.

The armies come together with a roar Homer out-bids three times: louder than surf before the north wind, than forest fire in the mountain glens, than a gale in the high oaks. And then, the stone. Hector throws first and hits Ajax square: but exactly where the two bands cross his chest, shield strap over sword belt, and the doubled leather saves him. As Hector falls back angry into the crowd, Ajax snatches up one of the stones lying about under the men’s feet: chocks for the beached ships’ hulls: and hits him above the shield rim, close to the neck, sending him spinning “like a top.” The simile for his fall: as an oak goes down whole under the lightning flash of father Zeus, with a terrible smell of brimstone, and no man standing near can help being dismayed. Homer chose that comparison on purpose. Hector falls like a man struck by Zeus’s own bolt, in the one hour Zeus isn’t throwing any. His comrades roof him with shields, carry him to the ford of the Xanthus, and pour water over him; he comes to, kneels, vomits blood, and goes dark again.

With him gone, the Greeks go forward like men unchained, and the killing turns exultant and ugly: taunts over every corpse, a severed head held up “as though it had been a poppy-head” for the Trojans to look at, and fear falling on them until every man scans for a way to run. Oileus’s fleet son kills more than anyone, because no one is faster at running down men in flight. Now step back and look at what turned this battle. Not reinforcements. Not tactics. Not the return of Achilles. One nap. The entire momentum of the war: the design Zeus has been imposing since Book One: turned out to require Zeus, continuously, and the moment his eyes closed, everything downstream of him reversed within the hour. That is the fragility of top-down control, divine or otherwise: it doesn’t have to be defeated, only interrupted. And absolute power turns out to mean that everything depends on you: including, once your wife has learned there is no other way to be heard in your house, your afternoons.

Last one

One god dozes in a golden cloud, and within the hour Poseidon leads the Greeks openly, Hector is carried off spitting blood, and the war runs backward. What is the poem saying about how this war is held together?

The stone-and-nap symmetry makes the luck reading tempting, but neither event is an accident: the stone lands because Poseidon has re-nerved the whole line, and the nap exists because Hera engineered it through three separate negotiations. And the gods-are-everything reading proves too much: the Greek courage Poseidon rallies is real; it was waiting for permission, not invented by it. What Book Fourteen isolates is the structure: Zeus’s design requires Zeus, continuously. He has centralized the war so completely that its momentum simply is his attention, and a system with one indispensable node doesn’t have to be beaten. It has to be interrupted. Hera didn’t win an argument or a battle. She scheduled an outage.

TLDR

The wounded Greek leaders consider flight, but Odysseus and Diomedes keep them from it. Hera seduces Zeus and Sleep disables him, letting Poseidon help the Greeks drive the Trojans back and wound Hector.

Who’s who

Hera

Queen of the gods

She cannot outfight her husband, outvote him, or outargue him. So she bathes, borrows a girdle under false pretenses, bribes Sleep with a bride, and takes the supreme power of the cosmos out of play for an afternoon.

Zeus

King of the gods

The one power no god can fight, undone on a mountaintop by his own marriage: wooing his wife with a list of his affairs, then sleeping through the reversal of his entire war.

Aphrodite

Goddess of love

She backs Troy, and the favor is for the enemy: but “I cannot and must not refuse you, for you sleep in the arms of Zeus who is our king.” The girdle changes hands, and with it the war.

Hypnos

Sleep, brother of Death

Sleep, own brother to Death. He has put Zeus under for Hera once before and barely survived the waking; this time the price is the Grace Pasithea: secured by an oath on the waters of Styx.

Poseidon

God of the sea

He has been helping the Greeks in disguise, rationed to whispers. The moment Sleep reports Zeus unconscious, he takes the front in person, sword flashing like lightning.

Agamemnon

Commander-in-chief of the Greek army

Third crisis, third plan to launch the ships and slip away in the dark: “there is nothing wrong in flying ruin even by night.” His subordinates talk him down. Again.

Odysseus

King of Ithaca

He hears the evacuation order and kills it in one speech: you should have commanded some other and baser army. Let the men see the ships taking the water, and the whole line dissolves.

Diomedes

Son of Tydeus

Youngest man in the council, so he recites his pedigree before daring to plan: go back wounded, stand out of spear range, and put heart into the men still whole.

Nestor

Old king of Pylos

He leaves his wine at the sound of the rout, grabs his son’s shield because his son took his, and carries the news to the wounded kings: the wall is down, and counsel is all that’s left.

Ajax

Bulwark of the Greeks

Hector’s spear finds the one spot where two straps cross and wastes itself. His answer is a ship-prop stone above the shield rim that sends Hector spinning like a top.

Hector

Commander of Troy

At the peak of his triumph, one stone drops him like an oak under a lightning stroke: carried from the field to the ford, vomiting blood, while the war reverses behind him.

Achilles

Offstage, everywhere

Still at his ship, still in his anger. Poseidon uses his name as a goad, Agamemnon as an excuse: the most important man in the war measures it by staying out of it.
