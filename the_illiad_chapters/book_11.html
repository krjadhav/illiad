The Iliad, retold

Book 11

Book Eleven, after Samuel Butler’s translation: the Greek captains go down one by one, Achilles watches from his deck and likes it, and an old man plants the idea that ends the poem.

Book Eleven opens with the last divine cameo for a while: at dawn Zeus sends Discord down to the Greek ships, “the ensign of war in her hands.” She picks her spot like an acoustics engineer: Odysseus’s ship, the middlemost of the line, so one scream carries to Ajax’s camp at one end and Achilles’s at the other. Then the gods go home. The rest of Olympus sits this day out, grumbling in their valleys; Zeus sits apart on Ida with a thunderbolt, looking down “alike upon the slayers and on the slain.” One god to start the fight, one to watch it. Everything else in Book Eleven, humans do to each other: and what they mostly do is remove each other’s leadership, one manager at a time.

Agamemnon arms first, and Homer inventories the kit the way he’d inventory a treasury. The breastplate was a guest-gift from Cinyras of Cyprus, who heard the Achaeans were sailing for Troy and sent hardware instead of showing up: the ancient art of contributing to a war you intend to skip. The shield carries a Gorgon’s head with Rout and Panic on either side: a man wearing his doctrine on his arm. And for one morning the doctrine works. He goes through the Trojan line like weather: Homer reaches for reapers mowing swathes of barley, then a lion crushing fawns, then a forest fire: and underneath the similes runs the accounting the poem never suspends: “many a noble pair of steeds drew an empty chariot along the highways of war,” their drivers lying on the plain, “more useful now to vultures than to their wives.”

Twice in the rampage, men offer him money. The sons of Antimachus beg from their chariot: take us alive, our father is rich, he will pay a very large ransom. Agamemnon answers with the family ledger: your father is the man who took Paris’s bribes to keep Helen from being returned, the man who proposed at council that Menelaus and Odysseus, come as envoys, be murdered on the spot: and then he kills them both, sending one head “rolling in among the crowd as though it were a ball.” Earlier he had cut down Isus and Antiphus, two sons of Priam whom Achilles once caught herding sheep on Ida, tied with fresh willow shoots, and released for a price. That detail does quiet work: there used to be a market here. Prisoners had prices, and Achilles collected them. Book Six showed the market closing. Book Eleven shows it gone: the war has stopped being an economy and become a liquidation.

Meanwhile Zeus runs the day like a program. He sends Iris to Hector with instructions of surgical strangeness: as long as you see Agamemnon raging in the front ranks, hold back and let others bear the brunt. But when he is wounded: “either by spear or arrow”: and takes to his chariot, “then will I vouchsafe you strength to slay till you reach the ships, and till night falls.” Read it twice. The best hour of Hector’s military life: the one his men will remember: is a conditional clause with a trigger event and an expiry time. He obeys. He waits. He watches the front like a man watching for a signal, because that is now exactly what a wound is.

Pause: check yourself

Zeus’s message to Hector: hold back while Agamemnon rages; when he bleeds, you get strength to kill until you reach the ships. What does the message do to everything Hector wins today?

The test reading imports a god who cares how Hector behaves, and the mercy reading a god who cares whether he lives. Book Eleven’s Zeus is neither: he sits alone on Ida watching “alike upon the slayers and on the slain,” and his message is pure logistics: a trigger condition and a time limit. Hector will execute the plan brilliantly, and it changes nothing about what the glory means, because we read the memo before he got it. Homer keeps doing this: he tells you the outcome in advance, then makes you watch men experience an allocation as an achievement.

The trigger arrives with a biography, because Homer refuses to kill anonymous men. Iphidamas of Thrace married a week before sailing: a hundred head of cattle down, a thousand sheep and goats promised, “no joy of her” ever collected: and his spear-point folds against Agamemnon’s silver girdle “as though it had been lead.” He dies for the attempt, “sleeping a sleep as it were of bronze,” far from the wife he paid for. His brother Coon sees it, comes in from the blind side, and puts a spear through Agamemnon’s forearm below the elbow: then dies too, beheaded across the body he was trying to drag home. And now the detail Homer has been saving: the king fights on “as long as the blood still welled warm from his wound,” but when the wound dries, the pain arrives, and the simile Homer picks is the sharpest one a poet has: the pangs the goddesses of childbirth send on a woman in labor. The commander-in-chief, graded against labor pains, climbs into his chariot and quits the field. One.

Hector sees him go and reads the memo’s second clause aloud to his men: their best man has left them, and Zeus has vouchsafed me a great triumph. Then he uses his window: nine named chieftains, and after the names, “the rank and file.” The retreat curdles toward rout, and the middle managers hold it. Odysseus calls to Diomedes: “we shall be shamed for ever” if the ships are taken: and gets back the bleakest yes in the poem: I’ll stand, “but we shall have scant joy of it,” because Zeus has decided for Troy. Two men holding a line they believe headquarters has written off. Diomedes even gets his moment: a spear square off Hector’s helmet that drops Troy’s commander to his knees, darkness over his eyes. Bronze turned bronze; the helmet was a god’s gift. Ten more feet of luck and the epic ends here.

Then Paris. He is leaning against the pillar on the tomb of Ilus: a dead king’s monument, which is very him: using the past as cover: while Diomedes is bent over a corpse, stripping armor. The arrow goes through the flat of the right foot and pins it to the earth. And Paris springs out of hiding with “a hearty laugh,” wishing aloud it had hit the belly instead. Diomedes, with Odysseus standing over him as a screen, answers with the entire aristocracy of violence compressed into one speech: “Archer, you who without your bow are nothing, slanderer and seducer”: you have scratched my sole like “a girl or some silly boy”; a coward’s wound is light; when I hit a man, his wife tears her cheeks and the vultures gather. Killing, it turns out, has a caste system, and the bow: range, cover, no exposure: sits on its bottom rung. But watch what the wound does rather than what the speech says: he sits down, draws the arrow through his foot, and rides for the ships “sick at heart.” Two.

Pause: check yourself

An arrow through the foot, shot from behind a pillar, and Paris springs out laughing. Diomedes answers with a speech: coward, archer, “a girl or some silly boy.” What is the speech defending?

The morale reading fails on staging: the army is in flight and the only audience is Odysseus, holding a shield over him. And the “scratch” claim is refuted by what happens next: the wound sends Diomedes to the ships and out of the fighting for the rest of the poem. The speech is louder than the wound precisely because the wound disproves it. Diomedes’s worth is denominated in one currency: face-to-face killing, witnessed and answered: and Paris just demonstrated a cheaper route to the same result. The rage isn’t about pain. It’s about devaluation. And the poem, deadpan, lets the archer keep the win.

Odysseus is now alone; Homer says it flatly: not one of the Argives stood by him, “for they were all panic-stricken.” What follows is the most interesting minute in the book: a man talking to himself in the middle of a collapsing battle. It is ill to run. It is worse to be taken. And then he cuts his own spreadsheet off mid-column: “But why talk to myself in this way? Well do I know that… a hero, whether he wound or be wounded, must stand firm and hold his own.” The Trojans close around him like hounds on a boar. Socus tears the flesh from his side with a spear: the goddess of wisdom, still quietly on the payroll, keeps the point out of anything vital: and dies for it in flight, a spear between the shoulders and a vaunt about vultures for a shroud. Bleeding, Odysseus gives ground and shouts three times. Menelaus hears: the only man who does: and fetches Ajax, whose shield comes up “like a wall.” The Trojans scatter the way jackals leave a carcass when the lion arrives, and Menelaus takes the wounded man by the hand and leads him out of the press. Three.

Pause: check yourself

Cut off and alone, Odysseus runs the numbers out loud: flight is bad, capture worse: and then stops mid-calculation and stays. What actually makes the decision?

The arithmetic is the tempting answer because he starts there: it’s a real calculation, and it’s going badly in both directions. But notice how it ends: not with a conclusion, with an interruption: “why talk to myself in this way?” The rule doesn’t win the argument; it terminates it. That is what roles are for in this poem: they spare you deciding at the exact moment deciding is impossible. And the gods option reverses cause and effect: Athena deflects the spear after he stays, which is how this poem’s heaven usually invests: in decisions already made.

Paris keeps working his trade, and his target selection is getting pointed. A triple-barbed arrow drops into the right shoulder of Machaon: the surgeon. Idomeneus states the actuarial case out loud, because someone has to: get this man to the ships first, “for a physician is worth more than several other men put together, for he can cut out arrows and spread healing herbs.” Nestor hauls him into his chariot and drives. Wound a fighter and you subtract one man; wound the healer and you subtract a piece of every wounded man to come. A third arrow catches Eurypylus in the thigh as he steps up to cover Ajax. Now count the day’s casualty list: Agamemnon, Diomedes, Odysseus, Machaon, Eurypylus: and notice that most of it was produced from cover, by the one man in Troy the heroes have agreed doesn’t count.

That leaves Ajax, and it takes Zeus personally to move him: a fear dropped straight into his heart, no missile required. Homer gives the retreat two similes and lets them argue with each other. First a lion driven off a stockyard by dogs and torches: frustrated majesty. Then: for the second-best fighter of the Achaeans: a donkey that has gotten into a cornfield, whom boys beat with sticks until the sticks break, and who leaves only when he has eaten his fill. It isn’t mockery; it’s precision. There is no plan left, no line, no orders. There is one man backing up slowly with an army’s worth of spears standing in his shield, too obstinate to be routed.

And where has the best fighter been? Homer answers with stagecraft: Achilles “was standing on the stern of his ship watching the hard stress and struggle of the fight.” The whole day. Watching. When Nestor’s chariot goes past with a wounded man aboard: the horses too fast for him to see the face: he calls Patroclus out of the tent, and Homer interrupts his own poem for one flat aside: Patroclus came out, “and here indeed was the beginning of the ill that presently befell him.” Then Achilles says the quiet part without a trace of shame: “I take it that I shall now have the Achaeans praying at my knees, for they are in great straits.” Go and ask Nestor who that was; from the back it looked like Machaon. The hinge of the whole epic is not a duel and not a god. It is a curious, gratified man on his own quarterdeck, sending a friend to confirm some good news.

Pause: check yourself

Achilles has watched the rout all day from his ship. When Nestor’s chariot carries a wounded man past, he sends Patroclus to ask who it was: and Homer calls this the beginning of Patroclus’s evil. What is the errand for?

Concern is what the question about Machaon sounds like, and the sentence before it refutes it: “I take it that I shall now have the Achaeans praying at my knees”: spoken with relish, by a man for whom every casualty is leverage accruing. The fate reading fails on the aside itself: Homer names no god here, and that’s the horror of it. The catastrophe that will consume Patroclus, Hector, and Achilles in turn enters the poem through its smallest door: an idle question, invented by a spectator, about somebody else’s pain.

Nestor’s tent is the one room in Book Eleven where civilization still runs. Hecamede: a war prize, Homer notes, awarded to the old man because “he excelled all of them in counsel”: lays a table: an onion as relish for the drink, honey, barley cakes, Pramnian wine with goat’s-milk cheese grated in, served in a gold-studded cup so heavy that only Nestor lifts it easily. Into this arrives Patroclus, and stands in the doorway refusing the offered seat: no time: “he that sent me is not one to be trifled with.” The question answers itself; he can see Machaon across the tent. He turns to go. What follows is Nestor not letting him.

The old man opens with the correct, unanswerable jab: why should Achilles care which particular man is hurt, while the army’s whole leadership lies disabled?: runs the casualty list, and then does the thing everyone who has ever met an old soldier knows is coming: “would that I were still young and strong, as in the days when…” And it is a hundred lines about a cattle raid. Fifty herds seized, chariots taken by the dozen, a boy on foot outfighting his mounted elders. Easy to smile at: until you count what it’s doing. The story is a credential: such was I among my peers. The credential buys the next move, which is aimed with a marksman’s care at the listener, not the absent man: Patroclus, I was in the room when your own father charged you: Achilles is the nobler born, “but you are older than he… counsel him wisely, and he will follow you to his own profit.” That is the lever: not the great man’s pride but the plain man’s single point of seniority. And then the proposal arrives dressed as a fallback to a fallback: if some oracle is holding him back, “let him send you into battle clad in his own armour, that the Trojans may mistake you for him.” Fresh troops, breathing room: it sounds tactical, modest, almost bureaucratic. Homer scores it in one clause: “With these words he moved the heart of Patroclus.” The deadliest weapon in Book Eleven is not the bow. It is an old man’s reminiscence with a payload folded inside.

Patroclus runs for the ships carrying it. And on the way, the book stages its final scene: Eurypylus limping out of the fight with the arrowhead still in his thigh, sweat raining from his head and shoulders, black blood welling: “but his mind did not wander.” He asks for the one thing left to ask: save me: cut it out, warm water, those “gracious herbs” they say Achilles taught you, the ones Chiron taught him; both real surgeons are out of service, one wounded, one still fighting. Patroclus answers like a man with a job: how may these things be? I am carrying a message, and the man waiting for it is not patient. Then he takes Eurypylus around the middle and helps him in anyway. Ox-hides spread on the floor. The knife. Warm water. A bitter root crushed between two palms, and the pain stops, and the wound dries. That is the last image of Book Eleven: the most consequential courier in Greek literature, message on hold, on his knees doing surgery, because a hurt man asked him.

Last one

Patroclus is carrying the most consequential message in the epic, and the book ends with him stopped in a tent instead, cutting an arrow out of Eurypylus’s thigh. Why end here?

The suspense reading is true about the plumbing: the message does have to wait books before it’s delivered: but Homer could have delayed his courier with anything, and he chose an act of tending, described down to the crushed root. The irony is real but decorative. The load-bearing thing is a character test passed in the wrong direction: Eurypylus asks, Patroclus explains he can’t stay: and stays. Nestor’s proposal needs exactly this man, one who feels other people’s pain as a command; the softness the old man’s speech worked on is the same softness that stops him in this doorway. Homer flagged him as doomed pages ago. Here he shows you why it had to be him.

TLDR

Agamemnon dominates the morning battle until he is wounded, and then the Greek leaders fall one by one. Nestor sends Patroclus back toward Achilles with news that starts turning pity into action.

Who’s who

Agamemnon

Commander-in-chief of the Greek army

He arms in gold and serpents and mows the Trojan line like a reaper: until a spear finds his arm below the elbow. He fights while the blood runs warm; when it dries, Homer measures his pain against childbirth.

Hector

Commander of Troy

Zeus tells him to wait until Agamemnon bleeds, and he waits. Then the window opens and he falls on the Greeks like a tempest: nine chieftains, then the rank and file.

Paris

Prince of Troy, archer

The book’s most efficient killer never closes with anyone: three arrows from cover take out Diomedes, Machaon, and Eurypylus. He laughs out loud at the first and gets away with all of it.

Diomedes

Son of Tydeus

He holds the center with Odysseus after the king goes down: then Paris pins his foot to the earth with an arrow. He calls it a scratch from a coward, and it removes him from the war.

Odysseus

King of Ithaca

Cut off alone, he reasons his way to the rule: a hero stands firm, wounded or not: takes a spear through the flesh of his side, and kills the man who threw it. Three shouts bring the rescue.

Ajax

Bulwark of the Achaeans

The last officer standing. Zeus himself has to frighten him into retreat, and even then he goes like Homer’s donkey in the cornfield: beaten with sticks and staying till he’s done.

Menelaus

King of Sparta

The only man who hears Odysseus shouting. He fetches Ajax, and while the big shield scatters the Trojans like a lion among jackals, he takes the wounded man by the hand and walks him out.

Machaon

Surgeon of the army

The army’s surgeon, shot through the right shoulder: and suddenly the most valuable casualty on the field. Idomeneus prices him plainly: a healer is worth more than several other men put together.

Eurypylus

Achaean captain

Hit in the thigh by Paris while covering Ajax’s retreat, he limps out asking for the one thing left: someone to cut the arrow out. He finds the one man in the camp who can’t say no.

Nestor

Old king of Pylos

He drives Machaon to safety, then does the day’s real damage over a cup of wine: a hundred-line memoir of his own youth, aimed at Patroclus, with the borrowed-armor idea folded inside.

Patroclus

Companion of Achilles

Sent to ask a single question, he refuses a chair, gets a speech instead of an answer, and leaves carrying the plan that will kill him. Then he stops anyway to tend another man’s wound.

Achilles

The spectator

He has watched the rout all day from the stern of his ship, satisfied: now they’ll come praying at his knees. The idle errand he invents: go ask who was wounded: is the hinge of the epic.

Zeus

King of the gods

The only god working today. He sits apart on Ida, thunderbolt at hand, scheduling Hector’s triumph around Agamemnon’s wound and watching alike upon the slayers and the slain.
