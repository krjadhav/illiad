The Iliad, retold

Book 1

Book One, after Samuel Butler’s translation: the plague, the quarrel, and a favor called in on Olympus.

The Iliad is supposed to be about a war. But Book One barely mentions the Trojans. It’s about an argument between two men on the same side. Homer understood something it takes most people a whole career to learn: the dangerous conflicts aren’t between enemies. They’re between allies.

Here’s what happens.

The Greeks have been camped outside Troy for nine years. Armies that sit that long develop side businesses, and one of theirs is raiding the towns nearby. In one raid they take two women. Chryseis goes to Agamemnon, the commander-in-chief. Briseis goes to Achilles, the best fighter. Both women are treated as prizes, like tripods or horses, which tells you what moral universe we’re in. Homer doesn’t apologize for it. He just watches what it does to people.

Then Chryseis’s father shows up. His name is Chryses, and he’s a priest of Apollo. He does everything right: he brings a big ransom, he’s polite, he invokes the gods, he wishes the Greeks victory. The whole army says take the deal. Agamemnon says no, insults the old man, and threatens to hurt him if he ever comes back. This is the first bad decision in western literature, and like most bad decisions it’s about status, not money. Accepting the ransom would look like backing down.

Pause: check yourself

Agamemnon turns down a generous ransom the whole army wants him to take. What is he actually protecting?

Status. The ransom is generous and everyone tells him so. He refuses because a leader who yields in public feels smaller: and people wreck real interests to avoid feeling smaller. Watch him do it again, at higher stakes, in a few paragraphs.

Chryses walks off along the shore and prays. And here’s the thing about insulting a priest: he has an escalation path. Apollo hears him and comes down from Olympus “with a face as dark as night,” and starts shooting plague arrows into the camp. First the mules and the dogs: a detail so specific it has to be observed: then the men. For nine days the funeral pyres burn.

On the tenth day Achilles calls an assembly. Notice who’s doing it: not Agamemnon, whose actual job this is. (Hera nudged Achilles into it; the gods in this poem are constantly reaching down to press buttons.) He proposes the obvious: ask a prophet why Apollo is angry.

They have one. Calchas, the seer who guided the fleet to Troy in the first place. What he says next is one of my favorite moments in the book, because it’s so precisely observed. He says, roughly: I know the answer, but it’s going to offend a powerful man, and a king’s grudge outlives a commoner’s complaint: he’ll swallow his anger today and collect later. Swear you’ll protect me first. Calchas may or may not see the future, but he has a perfect model of organizational politics. Prophets without one don’t live to become old prophets.

Pause: check yourself

Calchas knows the answer but won’t speak until Achilles swears to protect him. What has he understood about power?

The danger isn’t the king’s reaction now, in front of everyone. It’s the grudge he nurses afterward, when no one is watching. Anyone who has delivered bad news to a boss knows exactly what Calchas is negotiating for.

Achilles guarantees his safety: even, he adds pointedly, if the guilty man is Agamemnon. So Calchas says it: the plague is Agamemnon’s fault. Return the girl, no ransom, throw in a shipload of sacrifices, and Apollo relents.

Agamemnon’s reply is a small masterpiece of how powerful people absorb bad news. First he attacks the messenger: you never prophesy anything good about me. Then he concedes: fine, I’ll give her back, I’d rather the people live than die: and he probably even means it. Then comes the “but” that undoes everything: but I want a replacement prize, right now, or I’ll take someone else’s.

Do the math on that. The loot was distributed years ago. There is no unallocated prize sitting in a warehouse. “Find me one” means “take one from somebody,” and every man in the assembly knows it. Achilles stands up and says so: there’s no common store; give her up, and when Troy falls we’ll repay you three and four times over.

That’s a reasonable offer, and it lands the way reasonable offers usually land on a man defending status rather than interest. Agamemnon says: don’t try to outwit me. I’ll take a prize: maybe yours, maybe Ajax’s, maybe Odysseus’s. And then, having just threatened his three best men, he pivots mid-speech to logistics: anyway, let’s fit out a ship for Chryseis. The casualness is the insult.

Achilles detonates. The speech he gives is the best statement of the mercenary’s grievance ever written: I have no quarrel with the Trojans. They never raided my cattle or burned my crops in Phthia; there’s a lot of mountain and sea between us. I came here for your honor, and your brother Menelaus’s. I do most of the fighting; you take most of the loot; I carry my small share back to my ships and I’m expected to be grateful. Fine: but now you’d take even that? I’m going home.

Agamemnon could let him go with a face-saving formula. Instead he says: run, I won’t beg you to stay, I hate you anyway: and to make the lesson stick, I’ll come take Briseis myself, “that another may fear to set himself up as equal to me.” Which is the real motive, finally said out loud. This was never about a girl. It’s about making an example.

What happens next is my favorite passage in the book. Achilles’s hand is on his sword hilt, and Homer tells us he’s genuinely undecided: kill the king, or swallow it? At that exact instant Athena: sent by Hera, who likes them both: grabs him by the hair from behind. Only he can see her. And she doesn’t tell him murder is wrong. She makes him an offer: hold off, and you’ll be repaid three times over for this insult. Achilles gives the most Greek answer imaginable: when the gods ask, a sensible man complies, “for the gods hear the prayers of him who obeys them”: and slides the sword back in.

Pause: check yourself

Athena stops Achilles mid-draw. How?

No sermon, no force: a deal. Homer’s gods don’t appeal to conscience; they appeal to interest. Restraint here isn’t virtue conquering rage. It’s a man with a long grievance calculating that patience pays better. That’s a darker, and more honest, picture of self-control.

Then, since Athena said insults were still allowed, he insults: wine-bibber, face of a dog, heart of a deer, a king who devours his own people because they’re too feeble to stop him. He throws the speaker’s sceptre on the ground and swears an oath on it: a day is coming when you’ll miss me: when Hector is cutting your men down and you can’t stop him: and you’ll tear your heart out for having insulted the best man you had.

Old Nestor gets up: king of Pylos, ruler of three generations of men, veteran, he assures everyone, of wars beside heroes greater than either of these two. His advice is genuinely wise: Agamemnon, don’t take the girl. Achilles, don’t fight the king. Each of you is strong in a different currency: you have a goddess for a mother, he has more people under him. It changes nothing. When both sides of an argument are defending status, being right isn’t a resource either one can spend.

The assembly breaks up. Agamemnon sends Chryseis home by ship, with Odysseus in command: Butler, using the Roman names, calls him Ulysses; there’s a decoder below: because you send your safest pair of hands on the errand that must not go wrong. Then, “not forgetting the threat that he had made,” he sends two heralds to Achilles’s tent for Briseis.

The heralds are terrified. Achilles is courteous to them, which is somehow the saddest detail in the book: my quarrel isn’t with you. Patroclus, bring her out. And Patroclus: remember that name; the whole epic is loaded on it: leads Briseis out to them, “and the woman was loth to go.” That’s all she gets in the whole transaction: one clause. Homer notices exactly what his heroes don’t.

Then Achilles, the greatest warrior alive, walks down the beach alone and cries for his mother. This isn’t a metaphor. His mother is Thetis, a sea goddess, and she rises out of the water like a mist and asks what’s wrong. His request is the pivot of the entire epic: go to Zeus. You saved him once, when the other gods tried to chain him: you fetched the hundred-handed giant and broke the coup before it started. Call in the favor. Ask him to let the Trojans win for a while, so the Greeks find out what I was worth.

Read that again, because it’s easy to slide past: the best soldier in the Greek army asks heaven to make his own side lose. That’s how completely honor outranks team in this world. Thetis grieves over him: you were doomed to a short life, and now it will be a bitter one too: but she agrees. There’s a twelve-day delay, because Zeus is off feasting with the Ethiopians. Even divine intervention has scheduling constraints.

Pause: check yourself

Achilles asks Zeus to let his own side lose. What does that reveal about the world he lives in?

In an honor culture your worth isn’t what you privately are: it’s what others publicly acknowledge. Strip the recognition and the self goes with it. So Achilles needs the Greeks to bleed, not because he hates them, but because their losses are the only currency that can restore what Agamemnon took. Honor accounting, carried to its logical, awful end.

Meanwhile the machinery of appeasement runs, and runs smoothly. Odysseus sails to Chryse and delivers Chryseis to her father. Chryses, who prayed the plague in, prays it out again: same god, same formula, sign flipped. Sacrifices, roasted meat, wine, a day of hymns; Apollo is pleased and sends them a fair wind home. The whole plague subplot resolves in about a page, and I think that’s the point. The god’s quarrel was cheap to fix. The men’s quarrel will cost the rest of the poem.

Twelve days later Thetis surfaces at dawn, climbs Olympus, and finds Zeus sitting alone on the top ridge. She takes his knees with one hand and his chin with the other: the full formal supplication, no shortcuts: and asks: honor my son. Let the Greeks lose until they pay him what he’s worth. Zeus sits silent. She grips harder: nod yes, or refuse me to my face. And his answer is wonderful, because it isn’t about geopolitics at all: this is going to be trouble, because Hera will find out, and she already needles me in front of the other gods. The king of the universe agrees to tilt a world war, and his stated concern is that his wife is going to give him a hard time at dinner. Then he nods, and all Olympus shakes. The nod is the one thing in the cosmos that can’t be taken back.

Hera finds out immediately, of course. She saw Thetis at his knees that very morning. The scene that follows is a domestic argument, written centuries before anyone else thought gods could have one. She: which of them have you been scheming with now? You never tell me anything. He: you don’t need to know all my counsels, wife; you’d find them hard to understand. And when she keeps pressing, the mask comes off into open menace: sit down, be silent, or all heaven won’t save you when I lay hands on you. It’s an ugly moment and Homer doesn’t soften it. Hera is afraid, and sits.

What rescues the evening is Hephaestus, the lame smith god, Hera’s son. He plays the child of the quarreling household: a role he has clearly played before. Don’t wreck dinner over a pack of mortals. Mother, make peace; there’s no standing against him. Last time I took your side he threw me off Olympus, and I fell for a whole day before I hit the ground on Lemnos. He tells this: his own maiming: as a joke, and pours the nectar, bustling and limping around the hall until the gods burst out laughing. It works. Apollo plays the lyre, the Muses sing, and at sundown the immortals go home to bed, Zeus with Hera beside him, as if nothing were wrong.

That’s the joke, and the horror, of ending Book One in heaven. The gods have exactly the same quarrels as the mortals: status, favorites, who must yield to whom: but theirs dissolve into laughter by bedtime, because nothing is ever really at stake for beings who can’t die. That same evening, on the beach below, Achilles sits by his ships, out of the war, “gnawing at his own heart.” His quarrel doesn’t dissolve. It compounds. And fifteen thousand lines of consequences are on their way.

Last one

The gods’ quarrel ends in laughter by bedtime. Achilles’ quarrel will consume the whole poem. Why the difference?

The gods have the same status games, the same jealousies, even the same threats of violence: and none of it matters, because no god can lose anything permanently. Achilles has one short life, and every day of dishonor spends it. That asymmetry is the point of ending Book One in heaven: the same quarrel, weightless above, fatal below. Being mortal is what makes anything matter at all.

TLDR

Apollo sends a plague after Agamemnon insults Chryses. Agamemnon gives up Chryseis, seizes Briseis from Achilles, and Achilles withdraws from the war while Thetis gets Zeus to make the Greeks pay for the insult.

Who’s who

Achilles

The Greeks’ best fighter

The best fighter the Greeks have, and he knows it. His mother is a goddess, his lifespan is short, and his sense of honor is the hinge the whole epic turns on.

Agamemnon

Commander-in-chief of the Greek army

Commander of the Greek forces at Troy and brother of Menelaus. He has more power than anyone present, which is exactly why he can’t afford to look like he’s backing down.

Chryses

Priest of Apollo

A priest of Apollo whose daughter was captured in a raid. When ransom and courtesy fail, he goes over Agamemnon’s head: to his god.

Chryseis

Chryses’s daughter

Chryses’s daughter, taken as a war prize by Agamemnon. His refusal to return her for ransom is what brings the plague down on the camp.

Briseis

Achilles’ prize

Achilles’ war prize, seized by Agamemnon to save face. She leaves Achilles’ tent unwillingly, and her seizure sets the plot of the whole epic in motion.

Patroclus

Achilles’ closest companion

Achilles’ closest companion. In Book One he does one small thing: hands Briseis over to the heralds: but if you know how the story ends, watch him.

Odysseus

King of Ithaca

The most useful man in the Greek camp. When something delicate needs doing: like returning Chryseis to her father: he’s the one who gets sent. Butler calls him Ulysses.

Nestor

Old king of Pylos

King of Pylos and the oldest of the Greek leaders: he has ruled through three generations of men. He tries to talk both sides down. It doesn’t work.

Calchas

Prophet of the Greek army

The Greeks’ seer, who guided the fleet to Troy. He knows exactly why Apollo is angry and refuses to say until Achilles guarantees his safety: he understands what happens to men who give kings bad news.

Menelaus

King of Sparta

King of Sparta, Agamemnon’s brother, and the husband Helen left. The whole war is nominally being fought on his behalf.

Zeus

King of the gods

King of the gods. He can overrule anyone in heaven, but he’d still rather not have this argument with his wife.

Hera

Queen of the gods

Queen of the gods, Zeus’s wife, and firmly on the Greek side. She misses nothing that happens on Olympus, and Zeus knows it.

Apollo

God of archery, prophecy, and plague

God of archery, prophecy, and plague. When his priest is insulted, he answers with nine days of arrows: mules and dogs first, then men.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

Goddess of wisdom and war. Sent by Hera, she grabs Achilles by the hair: visible to him alone: at the exact moment his sword is coming out.

Thetis

Sea goddess, Achilles’ mother

A sea goddess, and Achilles’ mother. She once saved Zeus from a palace coup, and in Book One she climbs Olympus to call in that favor.

Hephaestus

Smith of the gods

The smith of the gods and Hera’s son, lamed when Zeus once threw him off Olympus. He defuses the dinner-table quarrel with a cup of nectar and a joke at his own expense.
