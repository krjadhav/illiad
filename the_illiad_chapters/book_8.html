The Iliad, retold

Book 8

Book Eight, after Samuel Butler’s translation: Zeus bans the gods from the field, weighs the day in golden scales, and Troy camps out under a thousand fires.

Every war in this poem has been two wars: one on the ground and one upstairs, where the gods lobbied, schemed, and slipped down to the field in disguise. Book Eight is the morning the second war gets cancelled. At dawn Zeus calls the gods into council on the highest crest of Olympus and issues a one-paragraph decree: nobody helps either side today. Anyone caught “acting apart” comes home beaten, or gets thrown into Tartarus: the pit below Hades, iron gates, bronze floor, “as far beneath Hades as heaven is high above the earth.” And then, in case anyone in the room is quietly doing the math on a coalition, he does the math for them.

The golden chain is the strangest boast in Homer, and the most precise. Hang a chain from heaven, he says. All of you take hold of the other end: every god, every goddess: and pull. You will not drag me down. But if I pull, I haul up the lot of you, and the earth and the sea into the bargain, tie the chain around a peak of Olympus, and leave everything dangling in mid-air. It isn’t an argument. It’s a physics report: here is the ratio. And notice what nobody does: nobody disputes the number. “They were frightened and all of them held their peace, for he had spoken masterfully.” Athena finally answers with a sentence a lawyer would bill for: we will refrain from actual fighting, since you insist, but we will offer the Greeks “serviceable suggestions.” Compliance, with a loophole filed in advance. Zeus smiles at her: take heart, my child: “I am not really in earnest.” He can afford the smile. Gentleness is cheap once the threat is believed.

Pause: check yourself

Zeus opens the day with the Tartarus threat and the golden-chain boast, and nobody argues. What actually changed on Olympus this morning?

The smile at Athena is the tempting one, because it’s real: but watch what it follows: “they were frightened and all of them held their peace.” Zeus can afford to be genial only after the threat lands; the wink is interest paid on believed force, not a retraction of it. And option c mistakes monopoly for neutrality: Zeus doesn’t stop intervention, he nationalizes it: scales at noon, thunderbolts by afternoon. What the chain speech ends isn’t interference. It’s the fiction that heaven’s decisions were ever up for a vote.

Then he commutes to work. He yokes the bronze-hoofed horses with their manes of glittering gold, dresses himself in gold, rides down to Mount Ida above the plain of Troy, hides the horses in a cloud, and takes his seat on the topmost crest: “looking down upon the city of Troy and the ships of the Achaeans.” The ruler who just banned interference hasn’t renounced it; he’s monopolized it, and taken the best seat in the house. Below him the armies collide, and all morning the day is even: shield on shield, spear on spear, “and the earth ran red with blood.” A fair fight, for exactly as long as fairness is allowed to run.

At noon the allowance expires. Zeus holds up his golden scales, sets a fate of death in each pan: one for Troy, one for the Greeks: and lifts the balance by the middle. The Greek pan sinks. Then he thunders from Ida and throws lightning into the Achaean lines, and the army that was holding its own an hour ago comes apart in terror. Now ask the obvious question. This is the god who promised Thetis, back in Book One, that the Greeks would lose until Achilles was missed. He already knows which pan sinks: he loaded it. So what is the weighing for? For what weighing is always for when the verdict precedes it: witnesses. A preference can be argued with. A procedure can’t. Watch any absolute ruler long enough and you’ll see the scales come out: the committee, the referendum, the review: some apparatus that converts a decision into a discovery. The thunder isn’t the decision. The thunder is the press release.

Pause: check yourself

At noon Zeus holds up golden scales, the Greek pan sinks, and he thunders. He has already promised Thetis this outcome. So what are the scales for?

Option a is the oldest reading and the best distractor: elsewhere in Homer, fate does sometimes bind even gods. But this Zeus promised Thetis this exact result in Book One, and he spends the rest of today enforcing it with targeted thunderbolts: a ruler genuinely bound by the balance doesn’t also need artillery to make the verdict come true. Option b’s half-truth is that the ritual has an audience; the deeper function is what procedure always does for absolute power: you can argue with a preference, and nobody argues with a weighing. He tips the scale, then thunders. Physics takes the blame.

The rout is total and specific. Idomeneus runs. Agamemnon runs. Both Ajaxes: both of them: run. The only Greek left facing the enemy is Nestor, and not by choice: Paris has shot one of his horses through the top of the skull, “a very deadly place,” and the animal’s dying struggles have tangled the team. The old man is hacking at the traces with his sword while Hector’s chariot bears down on him. Diomedes sees it and shouts to Odysseus for help: where are you flying to, with your back turned like a coward? Butler gives the reply one clause: Odysseus “would not give ear, but sped onward” to the ships. No excuse offered, none invented; the poem just writes it down and leaves it in the file. So Diomedes goes alone: takes the old man into his own chariot, drives straight at Hector, and kills his charioteer Eniopeus with the first spear.

For about a minute this looks like Book Five again: Diomedes forward, Troy giving way. Then Zeus intervenes personally: a thunderbolt into the ground just in front of Diomedes’s horses, with a flare of burning brimstone. The reins drop from Nestor’s hands, and the old man says the sentence that governs the whole day: turn and run, for “no man, however brave, may thwart the purpose” of Zeus. Today the victory is Hector’s; tomorrow, if it pleases him, it may be ours. There is no strategy against weather.

Diomedes’s answer is the human center of the book. Everything you say is true, old man: but there is a grief that “pierces me to the very heart.” Hector will stand up in front of the Trojans and say: the son of Tydeus fled before me to the ships. “May earth then swallow me” first. Notice what he is not afraid of. Not the lightning, not death: a sentence. A quotation in someone else’s mouth. Nestor’s consolation is genuinely shrewd: say what he likes, no Trojan will believe him: and neither will “the wives of the mighty warriors whom you have laid low.” Your reputation is held by hostile witnesses, and hostile witnesses are the most credible kind. They turn. Hector chases them, shouting the exact speech Diomedes dreaded: the Greeks gave you the best seat and the full cup, and now they will despise you: “Be off, girl and coward that you are.” Three times Diomedes half-wheels his horses to go back and die, and three times Zeus thunders from Ida, and the bravest man in the Greek army needs all three peals to keep driving away from an insult.

Pause: check yourself

Diomedes agrees that no one can fight Zeus, turns his horses: and says the one thought that pierces him is Hector telling Troy he ran. What is the poem measuring here?

Nestor’s consolation really is good: your reputation is held by hostile witnesses, he says, and the widows of the men you’ve killed will vouch for you: which is why b tempts. But look at what the argument fails to do: Diomedes still wheels his chariot around three times, and it takes three separate peals of Zeus’s thunder to keep him pointed toward safety. An argument that worked wouldn’t need artillery support. That’s the measurement: the culture’s shame instinct doesn’t recognize force majeure. Even a certified, god-sent thunderbolt doesn’t void the ledger entry he fled: and c is exactly the accounting the ledger refuses to do.

Meanwhile Hector is having the best day of his life, and the poem lets him talk to his horses to prove it. Xanthus, Podargus, Aethon, Lampus: now pay me back for all the honey-sweet corn Andromache has fed you, for the wine she mixed for you before she served me, “her own husband.” Run down Nestor, whose shield is solid gold; strip Diomedes of the cuirass Hephaestus made him. Take those two trophies and the Greeks sail home tonight. Up on Olympus, Hera hears the vaunt and shakes the mountain with rage on her throne. She tries to recruit Poseidon: have you no pity for the Greeks who load your altars at Helice and Aegae? If all of us who favor them pushed back together, Zeus would be left “sulking alone on Ida.” It’s a coalition pitch: the exact scenario the golden chain was built to preempt: and Poseidon’s refusal shows how well the math has been absorbed: we must not set ourselves against Zeus, “for he is far stronger than we are.” The god of earthquakes has read the memo.

So opposition shrinks to the size of what’s permitted, which turns out to be a thought. Hera puts it into Agamemnon’s mind to rally the army: a serviceable suggestion, exactly as licensed. He climbs onto Odysseus’s black ship, the middle of the line, where a voice carries both ways: to Ajax’s tents at one end and Achilles’s at the other: and delivers shame first, then accounting: you boasted at Lemnos, over full mixing-bowls, that each of you was worth a hundred Trojans, and now you are no match for one man. Then he turns to Zeus weeping and audits his own piety: I never once sailed past an altar of yours without burning fat and thigh-bones on it: and asks for precisely nothing but survival: “suffer us to escape at any rate with our lives.” Nine years of siege, negotiated down to a request to keep breathing. Zeus pities the tears and sends an eagle carrying a fawn, which it drops beside his own altar. The army takes heart. But read the fine print of the omen: it means today will not be an extermination. The plan itself hasn’t moved an inch.

The rally produces the book’s one discovery: Teucer, Ajax’s bastard half-brother, ninth man over the trench and the best archer the Greeks have. His method is pure siblinghood: he crouches behind Ajax’s tower shield, ducks out, kills a man, and ducks back “as a child to its mother.” Eight arrows, eight dead. Agamemnon arrives with praise and a price list: when Troy falls you get the first prize after mine, a tripod or a chariot and pair or a woman for your bed: and manages to fold the phrase “bastard though you were” into the compliment, because in this economy even praise files your paperwork for you. Teucer’s reply is a professional’s: don’t urge me, I haven’t stopped; eight shafts, all buried in the flesh of young men: “but this mad dog I cannot hit.” He keeps aiming at Hector and keeps killing the men beside him instead: Gorgythion, whose helmeted head droops “as a garden poppy in full bloom” bows under spring rain; then the replacement charioteer Archeptolemus: Apollo tips that arrow aside. Hector finally answers the archery in his own idiom: a jagged stone to the collarbone, “a very deadly place,” and the bow drops from a hand that no longer works. Ajax stands over his fallen brother with the shield while two friends carry him, groaning, back to the ships.

With Teucer down, Zeus puts his weight back on the scale, and Hector hunts the Greeks across their own trench like a hound on a boar, killing the hindmost, his eyes glaring like Gorgo’s. On Olympus, Hera tries once more, this time on Athena, and gets a grievance speech in return: I saved his son Heracles, labor after labor, every time he cried to heaven: and this is my thanks; now he hates me and “lets Thetis have her way because she kissed his knees.” That sentence is worth pausing on: even goddesses model influence as a market, and Athena believes she’s been outbid by a better lobbyist. She throws off her embroidered vesture and puts on her father’s own war-shirt: there is a message in the wardrobe: Hera lashes the horses, and the gates of heaven, which the Hours keep, bellow open of their own accord. It is a real sortie. It gets exactly as far as the outer gates.

Zeus sees them from Ida and doesn’t move. He sends Iris with a message, and the message is not a prohibition but an invoice: I will lame your horses, hurl you both from the chariot and break it in pieces, and the wounds my lightning leaves will take ten years to heal. Iris delivers it word for word and appends an editorial of her own: “bold hussy”: free of charge. And Hera, who was shaking Olympus an hour ago, folds in a single sentence: I am not for fighting men’s battles in defiance of Zeus; “let them live or die as luck will have it.” The Hours unyoke the horses. The chariot is leaned against the courtyard wall. The two goddesses take their golden thrones among the company of the gods, “but they were very angry”: which is, of course, permitted. Anger is free. Everything else now has a price.

Pause: check yourself

The gates of heaven open by themselves for Hera and Athena’s war chariot: and one message from Iris turns it around. What actually sets the limit of their opposition?

Option b is the strong one, because Poseidon really did state the theme first: we must not set ourselves against Zeus. But the goddesses armed after hearing that logic: argument didn’t stop them, and the sortie was serious enough that Athena put on her father’s own armor to make it. What stops them is Iris reciting an itemized bill: specific, credible, personal. That’s the book’s little lesson in deterrence, and the answer to option a: family feeling didn’t restrain anyone in Books One through Five, when defiance was cheap. Vague anger survives vague threats. It rarely survives a price list from someone who has demonstrated, this same afternoon, that he pays out.

Zeus comes home to gloat, which strong players rarely resist, and then does something more interesting: he declassifies the war. Why so sullen, he asks the two of them: tired from killing your dear Trojans? And since resistance is over, he reads out the schedule. Tomorrow will be worse. Hector will not stop “till he has roused the son of Peleus”: on the day the armies fight in dire straits at the ships’ sterns, “about the body of Patroclus.” That name has not been spoken since Book One, when he was a quiet figure in Achilles’s tent, and its first reappearance is inside a death notice with a date on it. “Like it or no, this is how it is decreed.” Secrecy is an instrument you need only while someone can still interfere; once Zeus has proven nobody can, the plan costs nothing to publish. But the disclosure works on the reader too. From this line forward you know what Hector’s triumph is for: scaffolding for another man’s return: and you carry that knowledge into the most beautiful night scene in the poem.

Darkness stops the battle: “welcome and thrice prayed for” by the Greeks, resented by Hector, who is certain nightfall is the only thing that saved the fleet. He calls an assembly on the riverbank, in a space clear of corpses, leaning on his eleven-cubit spear with its ring of gold, and issues orders that would pass for sound administration in any army: bring oxen and sheep and wine from the city; feed the horses; gather wood and burn watchfires until dawn, so the Greeks cannot slip away over the sea unpunished; graybeards and boys to man the walls; every woman to keep a fire lit in her house against a surprise attack. Then the plans for morning, and the wish that gives him away: would that I were as sure of being immortal and never growing old “as I am that this day will bring evil to the Argives.” He is certain of exactly one thing, and it is the wrong one. The Trojans shout applause, and camp: for the first time in nine years: outside their own walls, in the open, “high in hope.”

They sacrifice oxen to the immortals, and the wind carries the sweet savour of it up to heaven, and the gods refuse it: “for they bitterly hated Ilius with Priam and Priam’s people.” The offering rises; nobody’s home. And then, having told us that, the poem does the last thing you’d expect: it makes the doomed camp’s hope the most gorgeous thing in eight books. The watchfires burn on the plain like stars around a bright moon on a windless night, when every peak and glade and jutting headland stands out sharp, “and the stars can all of them be told, and the heart of the shepherd is glad.” A thousand fires between the ships and the river, fifty men in the glow of each, and the horses champing their oats and white barley beside the chariots, waiting for dawn. Homer knows what dawn is bringing: he had Zeus announce it a page ago. He gives them the stars anyway.

Last one

The book ends on the Trojan watchfires: stars on a windless night, a glad shepherd, horses champing by the chariots. Why does Homer make the enemy’s hope this beautiful?

Option c gets the geography right: the Greeks can see every one of those fires from their ships: but the simile’s register is wrong for dread: the shepherd’s heart is glad, and gladness belongs to the Trojan side of the night. Option a is the trap for modern readers: we do know the schedule, because Zeus published it. But Homer could have scored this night as hubris, and instead he spends his best simile making Troy’s hope worth what hope is worth. The fires get to be stars for one night. Knowing about tomorrow doesn’t make the beauty ironic. It makes it generous.

TLDR

Zeus bans divine interference, weighs the day for Troy, and drives the Greeks back toward the ships. Hera and Athena are checked, Hector camps on the plain, and the Trojan fires make the Greek danger visible all night.

Who’s who

Zeus

King of the gods

He opens the book by ending the game: no god interferes, on pain of Tartarus, and he could haul the lot of them up on a golden chain. Then he sits on Ida and runs the day by scale, bolt, and timetable.

Hector

Commander of Troy

The best day of his war, and he knows whose gift it is. He chases the Greeks to their trench, drops Teucer with a jagged stone, and camps his army in the open to finish the job at dawn.

Diomedes

Son of Tydeus

The only man who drives back into the rout: to pull Nestor out. Zeus drops a thunderbolt in front of his horses, and turning around costs him more than dying would have.

Nestor

Old king of Pylos

Pinned behind a dying horse until Diomedes takes him aboard. He states the day’s one rule: no man, however brave, may thwart the purpose of Zeus.

Hera

Queen of the gods

She shakes Olympus with rage, fails to recruit Poseidon, and arms for a sortie anyway. One itemized threat from Zeus and she folds: her only win today is a whisper in Agamemnon’s mind.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

She answers the ban with lawyer’s language: no fighting, just “serviceable suggestions”: then puts on her father’s own armor to defy him. Iris meets her at the gates with the price list.

Iris

Messenger of the gods

Zeus’s courier, delivering the threat that stops the sortie: lamed horses, a shattered chariot, wounds ten years in the healing. She adds a “bold hussy” of her own on the way out.

Poseidon

God of the sea

Hera asks him to help her push the Trojans back. His refusal is the whole divine situation in one line: we must not set ourselves against Zeus, for he is far stronger than we are.

Agamemnon

Commander-in-chief of the Greek army

With the army pinned at the ships he prays weeping, itemizing every altar he ever burned fat on, and asks only to escape alive. Zeus sends an eagle: pity, not a change of plan.

Teucer

Archer, Ajax’s half-brother

The book’s discovery: he ducks out from behind his brother’s great shield, kills eight men with eight arrows, and cannot hit Hector: who finally answers with a stone.

Ajax

Bulwark of the Greeks

His tower shield is Teucer’s whole firing position: the archer runs back to it “as a child to its mother.” When his brother falls, he stands over him with the shield.

Odysseus

King of Ithaca

Diomedes shouts for help saving Nestor; he “would not give ear” and keeps running for the ships. The poem offers no excuse for him, and Diomedes never gets an answer.

Paris

Prince of Troy

One arrow this book, and it lands where it hurts: through the skull of Nestor’s trace-horse. The tangle it causes nearly hands Hector the old man’s life.

Achilles

Offstage, scheduled

Still absent, now on the calendar: Zeus decrees that Hector wins only until the son of Peleus rises. The whole book is stage-setting for one man’s return.

Patroclus

Achilles’s companion

A single name in Zeus’s prophecy, and the hinge of the entire plan: the war turns on the day the armies fight over his body. First mentioned since Book One: and he isn’t asked.

Andromache

Hector’s wife

Present in one tender, strange detail: Hector urges his horses on by reminding them she fed them honey-sweet corn and mixed their wine before she served his. Last book’s farewell hangs over this one.
