The Iliad, retold

Book 15

Book Fifteen, after Samuel Butler’s translation: Zeus wakes, recites the ending in advance, and a god kicks down in a moment what an army built in a day.

Book Fifteen opens with the sound of a system snapping back into place. For two books the Iliad has been a heist: Hera seduced her husband to sleep on Mount Ida while Poseidon, disguised, rallied the Greeks, and Ajax put Hector on the ground with a stone. Now Zeus wakes. He takes in the whole picture in one glance: Trojans routed, Poseidon in the Greek ranks, Hector “gasping for breath, wandering in mind and vomiting blood”: and the whole insurgency, assembled over days of scheming, folds in about an hour. Nobody fights him. Nobody even argues long. If you want to know what enforcement looks like when the threat is credible, this is the book.

His first move is not violence but a story. He turns on Hera and reminds her of the time he hung her from heaven: two anvils fastened to her feet, her wrists bound in a golden chain no god could break, swinging in mid-air among the clouds. The other gods raged and could do nothing: any of them who came near, he “gripped and hurled from the heavenly threshold till he came fainting down to earth.” Notice what kind of threat this is. He doesn’t say what he’ll do to her; he says what he has already done, once, in front of everyone, to the most senior goddess there is. Institutions run on memory like this. A punishment carried out once, publicly, before witnesses who happen to be immortal, never has to be carried out again. Zeus’s power in this scene isn’t the lightning. It’s the archive.

Hera’s response is a masterpiece of technical truth. She swears by heaven above and earth below, by the waters of Styx: “the most solemn oath that a blessed god can take”: by her husband’s own head and by their bridal bed, that Poseidon is acting “of his own mere motion.” And he is. She never sent him anywhere. She just made sure the only person who could stop him was unconscious. The oath is airtight and the innocence is fake, and Zeus knows it, and he smiles. He doesn’t need her sincerity; he needs her compliance, and the oath: precisely because she worked so hard to make it technically survivable: tells him he has it. Then he puts her to work: go fetch Iris and Apollo. The first act of the restored government is to make the ringleader deliver its orders.

Pause: check yourself

Zeus wakes, makes one threat, retells one old story, and the whole insurgency: the seduction, Poseidon’s intervention, two books of scheming: folds within the hour. What makes the collapse so fast?

The oath matters less than it looks: Zeus smiles at it because he can hear the lawyering: technically true, materially false: and doesn’t care. He needs compliance, not sincerity. Hera’s speech on Olympus (option c) does spread the surrender, but notice the reason she gives: he sits apart and cares for nobody, “for he knows that he is much stronger.” The load-bearing thing is the story Zeus tells first: anvils, golden chain, gods hurled from the threshold. A punishment executed once, in public, before witnesses who are immortal never needs executing again. Deterrence is cheap when the demonstration already lives in everyone’s memory. That’s why his opening move is not violence but a reminder.

Watch her on Olympus, because Homer does. The gods crowd around Hera with welcome cups; she takes only Themis’s, and delivers the message with “laughter on her lips but her brow furrowed with care”: fools that we are, to keep flying at Zeus. He sits aloof and cares for nobody, because he knows he is stronger than all of us together. Make the best of whatever he sends you. And then: because Hera never does anything with only one motive: she twists the knife: you, Ares, might as well start now. Your son Ascalaphus is dead.

Ares slaps his thighs and calls for his armor: let me avenge my son at the ships even if it ends with me “struck by Zeus’s lightning and lying in blood and dust among the corpses.” He orders his horses yoked; their names are Panic and Rout. It takes Athena to stop him, and she does it physically before she does it verbally: tears the helmet off his head, the shield off his shoulders, wrenches the spear out of his hand: and then: madman, you have ears that hear not. If you go, Zeus won’t stay at Troy; he’ll come here and grip us up one after another, “guilty or not guilty.” Then she offers the coldest consolation in the poem: better men than your son have died already or will die hereafter, and “one cannot protect every one’s whole family.” It works. That’s the measure of what has changed since the nap ended: an hour ago the gods were unionizing, and now grief itself gets talked out of the room on deterrence grounds.

But the extraordinary thing in this book isn’t the crackdown. It’s what Zeus says next, almost in passing, to explain why Apollo needs collecting. He recites the rest of the Iliad. Hector will revive and drive the Achaeans in confusion onto the ships of Achilles. Achilles will send Patroclus into the battle. Patroclus will kill many: “among them my own noble son Sarpedon”: and Hector will kill Patroclus in front of Ilius. Achilles will kill Hector to avenge him. And from then on the Greeks will push the Trojans back until Troy falls through the counsels of Athena. Nothing else, and nothing sooner: no god may help the Greeks until Achilles has his honor, because I bowed my head to Thetis, and I keep my promises. It is the entire remaining plot of the poem: nine books, plus the fall of Troy that lies beyond its last line: delivered as a schedule. He flags his own son’s death in a subordinate clause and keeps going.

Think about what this does to you, the reader. Ten books ago Patroclus was a quiet presence by a tent; a few pages from now he’ll walk out of a sickroom on an errand of pity; and you now know, with the certainty of a signed order, where the errand ends. From this point on you know more than every character in the poem. Suspense is gone, and Homer clearly wanted it gone, because he traded it for something heavier: watching people be free inside an ending that is already fixed. Every choice Patroclus makes from here will be genuinely his, and will also be exactly what the schedule says. The Iliad doesn’t think those cancel out. That’s the wager of the whole poem.

Pause: check yourself

Zeus recites the rest of the war in one breath: Hector to the ships, Patroclus dead, Hector dead, Troy taken by Athena’s device: and Homer lets you overhear all of it. What does the poem buy by giving away its own ending?

The economy reading (b) half-works: Homer’s audience did know the myth: but it can’t explain why the poem stages the reveal as a scene, with Zeus flagging his own son’s death in a subordinate clause. And the speech does display authority (a), though even Zeus frames part of it as obligation: he bowed his head to Thetis. What the reveal actually changes is you. Patroclus doesn’t know. For the rest of the poem, every kind thing he does: and his next scene is an act of pure kindness: happens under a sentence you have read and he hasn’t. The poem trades the cheap tension of what for the heavier one of watching.

Iris takes the stand-down order to Poseidon, threat included: Zeus is older and much stronger, and you should consider well whether you can hold your own against him. And Poseidon answers with the best constitutional argument in Greek literature. We are three brothers, he says: Zeus, Hades, and I: and when we divided the world we did it by lot, like heirs. I drew the grey sea for evermore. Hades drew the darkness below. Zeus drew the air and sky and clouds. “But earth and great Olympus are the common property of all.” So I will not walk to his orders on ground that is no more his than mine. Let him keep his bragging talk for his own sons and daughters, “who must perforce obey him.” Every word of this is correct. Poseidon isn’t a rebellious vassal; he’s a co-heir being ordered around on common land, and he can produce the deed.

What happens next is the part worth studying. Iris: a messenger with no power of her own at all: asks softly: am I really to carry back an answer that hard, “or will you reconsider? Sensible people are open to argument, and you know that the Erinyes always range themselves on the side of the older person.” She has offered him a door, and Poseidon walks through it with his dignity intact and his position fully preserved. It is well, he says, when a messenger shows so much discretion. I will give way: “in spite of my displeasure”: but write this down: if Zeus spares Troy against the will of myself, Athena, Hera, Hermes, and Hephaestus, “let him understand that he will incur our implacable resentment.” Then he goes down into the sea, and the Greeks sorely miss him. He has obeyed the power and refused the principle. The retreat is total and concedes nothing; the field is lost and the grievance is filed. Every subordinate who has ever executed a decision they formally objected to knows exactly what he just did.

Pause: check yourself

Poseidon answers the stand-down order with a legal brief: three brothers, three lots, the earth held in common: and then leaves the field anyway. What has he actually done?

It isn’t a bluff-call (b): he goes, and Zeus visibly wins the exchange: the Achaeans sorely missed him. But “decoration” (c) gets the speech exactly backwards. Look at what he makes Iris carry home: a formal reservation with a penalty clause attached: if Zeus spares Troy against the will of the named gods, “implacable resentment.” He obeys the power and disputes the authority, and he makes the record say so. That is the oldest move subordinates have for surviving defeat with their standing intact: comply under protest. The field is lost; the principle: and the grievance: are preserved in full.

Meanwhile Apollo drops from Ida “like a falcon, bane of doves,” and finds Hector sitting up: just conscious, the sweat drying, recognizing faces again. Their exchange is oddly gentle. Which of the gods are you, Hector asks in a weak voice; don’t you know Ajax hit me in the chest with a stone? I was sure I’d go down to the house of Hades today. Take heart, says Apollo, and pours strength into him: and Homer reaches for a simile he has used exactly once before: a stalled, full-fed horse that snaps its rope and gallops gloriously to the river, head high, mane streaming over his shoulders. In Book Six that horse was Paris, bursting glossy from the citadel while his city bled for him. Here it’s Hector, back from the edge of death on borrowed divinity. The simile is a warning label both times: this shine is not the man’s own. Thoas, the sharpest of the Greek captains, reads it instantly: “Is it not Hector come to life again?” The hand of Zeus must be with him: and organizes a fighting retreat on the spot.

Then Apollo takes point, aegis in hand, and Homer runs a control experiment. As long as the god holds the aegis quietly, the fight is even and men fall on both sides at the normal rate. When he shakes it in the Greeks’ faces and shouts, they simply forget how to be brave: “they forgot their former prowess”: and the panic is total. And at the fortifications that cost the Greeks so much: the wall thrown up in a day back in Book Seven, the deep trench, the stakes: Apollo doesn’t fight. He kicks the banks of the trench in to make a bridge as broad as a spear-throw, and he flattens the wall “as easily as a child who, playing on the sea-shore, has built a house of sand and then kicks it down again.” Homer is so struck by it that he breaks into second person, the rarest register he has: “even so did you, O Apollo, shed toil and trouble upon the Argives.” The simile is the whole theology of the poem in one image. It isn’t that the god is strong. It’s that the ratio between mortal effort and divine effort is the ratio between engineering and play: and that the god, like the child, kicks the thing down for the same reason he could have built it: because it costs nothing.

Pause: check yourself

The wall cost the Greek army a day’s desperate labor, plus a trench and stakes. Apollo flattens it “as easily as a child… kicks down a house of sand.” Why that simile, and not a storm or an earthquake?

A storm or an earthquake would make the act impressive: a collision of great forces. The child makes it trivial, and trivial is the insult. Scale (b) is genuinely in the image, but the simile’s engine isn’t size; it’s cost. Homer picks the one destroyer for whom building and unbuilding are the same activity: play. The wall took a day of collective, frightened engineering; to Apollo it is an afternoon’s sand. And the innocence reading (a) mistakes the effect for comfort: Homer is so far from softening it that he breaks into second person, almost an accusation: “even so did you, O Apollo.”

One quiet scene sits in the middle of the rout. Patroclus has spent the last four books in Eurypylus’s tent, keeping a wounded man company and spreading herbs on his wound to ease the pain. When he sees Trojans coming through the breach he slaps his two thighs with the flat of his hands: the exact gesture Ares made at the news of his dead son: and says goodbye: I know you need me, but a servant will look after you now; I must run to Achilles and try to persuade him to fight. “A man does well to listen to the advice of a friend.” It is the kindest sentence in the book and the most terrible, because you have read the schedule and he hasn’t. The plot of the Iliad turns on a man leaving a sickroom out of compassion, at the exact minute a memo said he would.

At the ships the fighting turns nautical and strange: Trojans thrusting from chariots, Greeks up on their decks swinging the long jointed boarding-pikes stored for sea-fights. Twice the book shows you Zeus’s micromanagement up close. Teucer, who has been dropping Trojans all afternoon, draws on Hector with the shot that would have ended the fighting at the ships then and there: and Zeus snaps his bowstring mid-draw. Teucer stares at the string he “strung this selfsame morning” and knows exactly what happened; so does Hector, who tells his men it is easy to see when Zeus is helping people and when he is bringing them down. Then he gives them a line with a long afterlife: “he dies with honour who dies fighting for his country.” On the other side old Nestor prays as the ring shrinks: remember the thigh-bones we burned you: and Zeus answers with a thunderclap that the Trojans, hearing it, take as being for them. Homer doesn’t say whose it was. In this book prayer travels up, orders come down, and the two lines don’t connect.

And through all of it there is Ajax. No god carries his spear; nobody revives him; the entire divine apparatus of the book is arranged against his side. What he has is a sea-pike twelve cubits long: call it eighteen feet, jointed with rings: and he goes striding from deck to deck with it like the trick rider Homer compares him to, a man leaping between four galloping horses on a public road while the town marvels. His speeches aren’t about glory; they’re arithmetic. There is no wall behind this one, he tells the Greeks; no strong city within reach to send fresh forces; we stand on the plain of the armed Trojans “with the sea behind us, and far from our own country,” so our salvation is in the might of our hands. And once, mid-battle, he states the poem’s most practical sociology: fight so as to win respect from one another, for “men who respect each other’s good opinion are less likely to be killed than those who do not.” Shame, in this army, is a survival technology. He is driven off the raised stern; he steps back to the seven-foot oar-bench and holds that instead, spearing every man who comes up with fire: twelve of them by the book’s end: while Hector grips the stern of the ship that once carried Protesilaus to Troy and screams for flame. The book leaves them there: the man with the whole sky behind him, and the man with nothing behind him but the sea: and it does not let you doubt which one Homer respects more.

Last one

No god helps Ajax all book: the entire divine machinery is working for the other side. Homer gives him a giant pike and a lot of shouting instead of an aristeia. What is he for?

Option c describes the trajectory truthfully: he is pushed off the stern to the oar-bench: but it reads retreat as failure, when yielding one deck and holding the next is precisely the job. And glory (a) is what Homer strips out on purpose: Ajax’s speeches contain no prizes and no honor-arithmetic, only supply facts: no wall behind this one, no city in reach, the sea at our backs: plus one piece of survival sociology: men who respect each other’s good opinion are less likely to be killed. Hector shines with a god’s borrowed strength; Ajax stands with nobody’s. The poem knows which is rarer.

TLDR

Zeus wakes, restores his plan, and orders Poseidon off the field. Apollo revives Hector and breaks Greek resistance until Hector reaches the ships and seizes a prow for burning.

Who’s who

Zeus

King of the gods

He wakes on Ida, sees the wreckage of Hera’s trick, and restores order with one story and one schedule: the anvils, and the rest of the war dictated in advance down to Patroclus’s death and the fall of Troy.

Hera

Queen of the gods

Caught. She swears by Styx, by Zeus’s own head, and by the bridal bed that Poseidon acted on his own: technically true, since all she did was put the only enforcer to sleep. Then she carries her husband’s orders like a model wife.

Apollo

God of the bow

Zeus’s enforcer this book: he revives Hector with a touch, takes up the tasselled aegis, and kicks down the Greek wall the way a child kicks down a house of sand: for the same reason, and at the same cost.

Iris

Messenger of the gods

Carries Zeus’s stand-down order to Poseidon, threat included: then, unprompted, offers him the door: sensible people are open to argument, and the Erinyes side with the elder. The war’s best diplomat has no power at all.

Poseidon

God of the sea

Ordered off the field, he cites the constitution: three brothers drew lots for sea, underworld, and sky, and the earth is common property. Then he yields anyway: under protest, with his grievance filed in full.

Ares

God of war

His son Ascalaphus is dead, and he calls for his horses: Panic and Rout: to go avenge him, even if it ends under Zeus’s lightning. It takes Athena physically disarming him to keep him in his seat.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

The only cool head on Olympus: she strips Ares of helmet, shield, and spear before his grief can bring Zeus down on all of them. Her consolation is ice: one cannot protect everyone’s whole family.

Hector

Commander of Troy

Found vomiting blood after Ajax’s stone, he is back at the head of the army within the hour, shining like a stalled horse loosed at last. The shine is Apollo’s, not his: and Homer notes his death is already being hurried on.

Ajax

Bulwark of the Greeks

No god helps him all book. He strides from deck to deck with an eighteen-foot boarding pike, preaching arithmetic: no wall behind this one, the sea at our backs: and kills twelve men who come at the ships with fire.

Patroclus

Achilles’ closest friend

Still in Eurypylus’s tent, spreading herbs on a wound, when the wall breaks. He leaves to beg Achilles to fight: an errand of pure compassion, already entered in Zeus’s schedule with his death at the end of it.

Teucer

Archer, Ajax’s half-brother

He draws on Hector with the shot that would have ended the fighting, and Zeus snaps the bowstring he strung that very morning. He puts the bow away, picks up a spear and shield, and stands in beside his brother.

Nestor

Old king of Pylos

Prays loudest as the ships are hemmed in: remember the thigh-bones we burned you: and gets a thunderclap in answer. The Trojans hear it and take it as theirs. Homer lets both readings stand.

Eurypylus

Wounded Greek captain

The man whose wound Patroclus has been tending since the rout began. When Patroclus leaves his tent for Achilles’, the plot of the whole poem walks out with him.

Achilles

Offstage, scheduled

Still by his ships, and now on a timetable: Zeus names the day he sends Patroclus out, the day he loses him, and the day he kills Hector for it. His anger has become an item in someone else’s plan.
