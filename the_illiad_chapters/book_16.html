The Iliad, retold

Book 16

Book Sixteen, after Samuel Butler’s translation: Achilles lends everything but himself, Zeus watches his son die on principle, and Patroclus forgets the one order that mattered.

Book Sixteen opens with the poem’s greatest fighter watching his best friend cry. Patroclus comes back from the tents of the wounded with tears “welling from his eyes, as from some spring whose crystal stream falls over the ledges of a high precipice,” and Achilles teases him for it: you look like a little girl running at her mother’s skirts, tugging at her dress, begging to be carried. It’s affectionate, and it’s the last easy moment they will ever have. Patroclus answers with the harshest thing anyone says to Achilles in the whole poem: every Argive champion is lying wounded at the ships, and you are still nursing your grievance. Peleus was not your father, nor Thetis your mother: “the grey sea bore you and the sheer cliffs begot you, so cruel and remorseless are you.” Then the ask: if some oracle is holding you back, at least send me. Let me wear your armor, so the Trojans mistake me for you and quit the field. And Homer, who almost never steps out of the frame, steps out of the frame: “He knew not what he was asking, nor that he was suing for his own destruction.”

Watch what Achilles does with the request, because it’s the hinge of the entire poem. First he re-litigates. No oracle, no secret word from Zeus: I am cut to the heart that a man of my own rank robbed me simply because he had the power to, took my prize “as though I were some common vagrant.” It’s Book One recited from memory, word-perfect after all this time, which tells you how often he runs the tape. But then: let bygones be bygones; no man may keep his anger for ever. He can’t hold both positions: the grievance says sit, the burning fleet says fight: so he does what people do when two things they can’t stop being collide. He splits the difference. Take my armor. Lead my Myrmidons. Save the ships. I stay here.

Then the conditions, and they deserve slow reading. Drive the Trojans from the ships: and come back. Even if Zeus puts triumph within your reach, do not fight on without me, “or you will rob me of glory that should be mine.” Do not, for lust of battle, lead the army on to Ilius, “lest one of the ever-living gods from Olympus attack you: for Phoebus Apollo loves them well.” Three explicit warnings, and notice the ordering: my glory, then your safety, then my glory again. The recall clause protects his honor-price before it protects his friend. And then a wish that shows where nine years of this have taken him: would that every Trojan and every Argive might die, “but that we two might be alone left to tear aside the mantle that veils the brow of Troy.” Just the two of them, and no army left whose opinion could ever wound him again.

Pause: check yourself

Achilles won’t go himself, but sends Patroclus out in his armor with strict orders: drive them from the ships, then come straight back. What kind of decision is the poem showing us?

The trial reading quotes Achilles’ real words, and the tactical reading describes real effects: but read the fine print of the recall order: come back “or you will rob me of glory that should be mine.” The conditions guard his honor before they guard Patroclus. That’s the tell that this is not strategy but compromise: in matters of identity there are no half-measures that hold, and Achilles has tried one: the armor goes out, the self stays home, and the man wearing the gap between them is Patroclus. Book One’s grievance is still setting policy; it has just found a way to bill someone else.

The deadline arrives mid-conversation. Out at the ships Ajax has been holding the line past what a body can do: helmet ringing under continuous hits, left shoulder dead from the weight of the shield, “the sweat rained from every pore”: until Hector steps in close and shears the head off his ash spear with a sword-stroke. Ajax reads the sign like the professional he is: Zeus wills this: and gives ground, and the first ship goes up in flame. Achilles sees the glare and smacks his thighs: up, now, or there will be no way home. So Patroclus arms, piece by piece: greaves, cuirass, silver-studded sword, shield, the helmet with its horse-hair crest: every piece of Achilles but one. He does not take the great Pelian ash spear, cut by Chiron for his father on a mountain top, because no other Achaean can wield it. Homer places that detail exactly where you can’t miss it: the costume transfers. The man doesn’t.

Automedon yokes the immortal horses, Xanthus and Balius, foaled by a harpy to the west wind: and beside them, in the side-traces, the mortal horse Pedasus, war-loot from a sacked city, “who, mortal steed though he was, could take his place along with those that were immortal.” Keep an eye on Pedasus. Meanwhile Achilles turns out his Myrmidons, who have been penned up and furious for weeks. The poem gives them two similes: wolves with their jaws red from a kill, and then: as they deploy: wasps from a roadside nest that “silly children love to tease,” so that any passerby gets the fury the children earned. Someone else provoked this nest; the Trojans are about to be stung for it. They pack so close that helmet touches helmet: “shield pressed on shield, helm on helm, and man on man.”

And then Achilles does something he does nowhere else in the poem. He goes into his tent and opens the chest Thetis packed for him: shirts, cloaks, thick rugs, a mother’s idea of war: and takes out a cup no other man may drink from, reserved for one god alone. He scours it with sulphur, rinses it, washes his hands, stands in the middle of his yard, and prays to Zeus of Dodona with full formality. Two requests: send victory with my comrade: and “grant that he may return unharmed, with his armour and his comrades.” Homer answers in the very next sentence, with the flattest, most terrible bookkeeping in epic: “Part of it he did indeed vouchsafe him: but not the whole. He granted that Patroclus should thrust back war and battle from the ships, but refused to let him come safely out of the fight.” Achilles puts the cup back in the chest and comes out to watch. He thinks he has filed one request. Heaven processed two.

Pause: check yourself

Achilles prays with full ritual: the reserved cup, the washed hands: for victory and for Patroclus’ safe return. Zeus grants exactly one. What does the half-grant reveal?

The Book Six parallel fails on the facts: Theano’s prayer was refused outright, while this one is heard and half-granted: the ledger isn’t closed, it’s itemized. And the debt to Thetis explains Zeus’s motive, not the scene’s meaning. What the half-grant exposes is a hidden assumption inside Achilles’ compromise: that “win” and “come back” travel together. They never did. Zeus processes the prayer the way reality processes every half-measure: literally, clause by clause. Patroclus will accomplish everything he was sent to do. The sending was the mistake.

The disguise works better than anyone dared hope. The Trojans see the gleam of the armor, conclude that Achilles has put aside his anger and been reconciled, and “every one… looked round about to see whither he might fly for safety.” An army’s morale breaks on a rumor about one man’s mood. Patroclus throws his first spear into the thickest press, kills the Paeonian captain Pyraechmes, drives the panicked Trojans off the ship and puts out the fire, “leaving the half-burnt ship to lie where it was.” Mark that ship: singed, saved, the mission technically accomplished within the hour. Everything that happens after this is extra. Then the rout becomes a rampage. Trojan chariots jam and snap their poles in the trench their own side dug; the immortal horses take it at a bound. He spears Thestor through the jaw and hoists him over his own chariot rail “as one who sits at the end of some jutting rock and draws a strong fish out of the sea”: a man landed like a catch, gaping. The lists of the dead pile up in nines. And somewhere in here the recall order: free the ships, then come back: quietly expires, unrepealed and unremembered.

Then Sarpedon stops running. Watching his Lycians break, he names it: “Shame on you, where are you flying to?”: and jumps down from his chariot to meet the man in Achilles’ armor himself. The two go for each other like vultures screaming on a high rock. And the poem cuts away, mid-charge, to Olympus: because the real fight over Sarpedon happens up there. Zeus turns to Hera and says the thing no one in this poem is supposed to say: I love this man, and he is about to die. “I am in two minds whether to catch him up out of the fight and set him down safe and sound in the fertile land of Lycia, or to let him now fall.” The king of the gods, publicly considering the override.

Note carefully what Hera does not say. She does not say you can’t. She says: “Do as you will, but we shall not all of us be of your mind.” The man’s doom has long been fated: and there are many sons of gods fighting around Troy. Save yours, and every god will want theirs saved, and you will have taught them that the rule is negotiable for anyone who loves hard enough. Then she offers the substitute: let him fall, and send Death and sweet Sleep to carry him home to Lycia, where his kin can bury him “with mound and pillar, in due honour to the dead.” Zeus assents: and sheds a rain of blood on the earth for the son he could save and won’t. This is the poem’s ultimate stress test of its own rules, and they hold. The most powerful being in the universe, with the ability, the desire, and the stated option, is stopped by an argument about precedent: an exception made once for love is not an exception, it’s the end of the rule: followed by an eternity of gods queueing up with dead sons and fresh grudges. Even omnipotence, it turns out, is a governed office. What absolute power buys you, in the end, is a better funeral.

Pause: check yourself

Zeus says out loud that he is minded to pluck Sarpedon off the field alive. Hera stops him with one short speech. Why does her argument beat the king of the gods?

Fate is the distractor Homer plants himself: Hera uses the word: but her next sentence gives the game away: “Do as you will, but we shall not all of us be of your mind.” She concedes the power completely; what she prices is the politics. If capability were the constraint there would be nothing to argue about, and Zeus wouldn’t weep. What stops him is that a rule bent once in love is dead as a rule, and he would spend forever adjudicating the exceptions. So the son dies, and the compensation is honors: Sleep and Death, the washed body, mound and pillar. The grudge reading mistakes Hera’s tone: she isn’t collecting a debt here; she’s defending the constitution, which is far colder and far stronger.

Down on the plain, the duel is short and lopsided in a way nobody predicted. Sarpedon’s first spear misses Patroclus and kills Pedasus: the mortal horse in the immortal team goes down screaming, the divine pair plunge and tangle, and Automedon, without dropping the reins, draws his sword and cuts the dead one out of the traces so the living can run on. If you want this book’s whole plot in a single image, it’s there in the harness: the mortal yoked in among immortals is the one who dies, and the team is cut free and driven on. Sarpedon misses again; Patroclus doesn’t: the spear lands “just where the midriff surrounds the ever-beating heart.” He falls the way great things fall in this poem, “like some oak or silver poplar or tall pine to which woodmen have laid their axes… to make timber for ship-building.” And his dying words are not about his wife, his kingdom, or his father the god. They are a work order to his cousin Glaucus: rally the captains, fight for my body: “My name will haunt you henceforth and for ever if the Achaeans rob me of my armour now that I have fallen at their ships.” He dies mid-logistics, arranging his reputation. In this world even the corpse is a status asset.

Glaucus hears the charge land on him and can’t even hold a sword: Teucer’s arrow is still in his arm from the assault on the wall. So he prays to Apollo, and inside the prayer is a barb you’re not supposed to miss: Zeus “defended not his son; do you, therefore, O king, heal me.” The god your king couldn’t count on: maybe you’ll do better. Apollo heals him on the spot, and Glaucus goes to work: rallies the Lycian captains, then finds Hector and files the allies’ grievance: we die far from home for your sake and you do nothing. The armies converge on the corpse. Zeus pours darkness over the scrum, and the fighting buries its own cause: “A man had need of good eyesight now to know Sarpedon, so covered was he from head to foot with spears and blood and dust,” while men swarm the body like flies round brimming milk-pails. When Meriones and Aeneas start trading taunts over a missed throw, Patroclus cuts them off with what could be the book’s epigraph: “blows for battle, and words for council; fight, therefore, and say nothing.” Remember he said that. And through all of it Zeus never once looks away from the fight: because, the poem says, he is deciding how best to kill Patroclus: now, by Hector’s hand over this body, or later, after he has driven Troy harder and cost them more. He picks later. Grief for one son does not interrupt the scheduling of the next man’s death.

Then the consolation package is executed to the letter. At Zeus’s order, Apollo lifts Sarpedon out of range of the weapons, washes him in the river, anoints him with ambrosia, wraps him in immortal raiment, and hands him to the twin couriers, Sleep and Death, who set him down in the rich land of Lycia for the mound and the pillar. It is tender, and it is exactly what Hera specified, clause for clause. The gentlest passage in the book is the fulfillment of a settlement’s terms.

Meanwhile the recall order dies its quiet death. Patroclus pursues the Trojans and Lycians “in the pride and foolishness of his heart. Had he but obeyed the bidding of the son of Peleus, he would have escaped death.” Notice the mechanism, because it isn’t recklessness, exactly. Every surge was locally reasonable: press the rout, avenge a comrade, claim the armor: and every surge succeeded, and each success carried him one wall further from the ships he was sent to save. No single step disobeyed; the disobedience is the sum. He ends up at the angle of the wall of Troy itself, and the poem counts it out like a referee: “Thrice did Patroclus charge… and thrice did Apollo beat him back, striking his shield with his own immortal hands.” On the fourth charge the god uses his voice: “Draw back… it is not your lot to sack the city of the Trojan chieftains, nor yet will it be that of Achilles, who is a far better man than you are.” It is the book’s fourth warning, and the first delivered by someone in a position to know. Patroclus gives ground: some.

Pause: check yourself

The ships are safe, the fire is out, the Trojans are running: the mission, fully accomplished. Patroclus keeps going, all the way to the wall of Troy. Why doesn’t he stop?

The god-drove-him reading is textually real: Homer double-books every cause: but the same sentence charges “the pride and foolishness of his heart,” and Zeus only amplifies what he finds. The vengeance reading explains one surge, not the drift of an afternoon. What actually kills the recall order is that nothing ever triggers it: it was written for the man who left the tents, and by mid-book that man has spent hours being mistaken for Achilles and winning. Success is doing the killing here: each victory makes the order protecting him look like paperwork from a war that no longer exists. He never refuses the order. It just stops applying to anyone he still recognizes as himself.

Hector, meanwhile, is sitting in his chariot inside the Scaean gates, genuinely unsure: drive back out, or call the army inside the walls? He never gets to decide. Apollo puts on the shape of his uncle Asius and needles him: why have you left off fighting? drive straight at Patroclus: so the final duel is arranged by a god at both ends: one man shoved forward, the other already marked. Hector drives out, and Patroclus meets the charge with a jagged stone that hits Cebriones, Hector’s driver, square in the forehead: “his eyes fell to the ground at his feet” and he drops from the car “as though he were diving.” And Patroclus: the man who opened this book weeping for other people’s wounds, who told Meriones to fight and say nothing: stands over the body doing crowd-work: what a graceful diver; he’d have fetched up oysters for a whole crew in rough water; “it seems, then, that there are divers also among the Trojans.” The joke is the diagnosis. Sixteen books’ worth of other men’s cruelty is now coming out of the borrowed helmet, and he is talking like a man who cannot lose: which, in this poem, is the last thing men do before they lose. He and Hector fight over Cebriones like two lions over a dead stag, one gripping the head, the other a foot, until at sundown the Achaeans drag the body away.

Then the poem does something it has done for no one else: it kills its most likable man slowly, in three stages, and makes you watch each one. Patroclus charges three times and kills nine men each time; on the fourth: “like a god,” the phrase this book uses as an alarm bell: Apollo is waiting, wrapped in mist so no one sees him, and strikes his back and broad shoulders with the flat of one hand. That’s all. An open hand. His eyes spin. And then the god undresses him. The helmet is knocked off and rolls rattling under the horses’ feet, its plumes fouled with blood and dust: and Homer stops time to note that this had never happened before, “for it had served to protect the head and comely forehead of the godlike hero Achilles”: and Zeus, in the same breath, hands it to Hector to wear, adding that Hector’s own end is near. The spear shatters in his hands; the shield-strap drops; Apollo “undid the fastenings of his corslet.” Piece by piece, the god repossesses exactly what Achilles lent. The disguise is stripped off on the field, and what’s left standing dazed in the crush is only Patroclus: the man the costume obligated, and never protected.

Stage two is a man named Euphorbus, whom the poem introduces with a full résumé: best spearman of his time, finest horseman, twenty men dismounted in his first outing: and then shows us in action: he drives his spear into the back of a stunned, unarmed man, yanks it out, and runs into the crowd, unwilling to face Patroclus “unarmed though he now was.” Stage three is Hector, who has watched the wounded man giving ground, comes through the ranks, and drives a spear through his belly. Then he makes his speech over the body. You thought you would sack our city and carry off our women; the vultures shall have you here; poor wretch: Achilles, for all his bravery, availed you nothing. He even scripts the scene he needs to have won: I can just hear him charging you: come not back to the ships “till you have rent the bloodstained shirt of murderous Hector about his body.” It is what winners do: back-fill a story in which the kill was a duel, and the duel was theirs.

The dying man files the correction. “Vaunt as you will,” says Patroclus, and then delivers the coldest arithmetic in the poem: Zeus and Apollo beat me: it is they who stripped the armor from my shoulders. Among mortals, Euphorbus. “You are yourself third only in the killing of me.” Had twenty like you come against me, my spear would have had them all. And then, with the clear sight this poem grants only to the dying, the prophecy: you too have but a little season; death and the day of your doom stand close beside you already, “and they will lay you low by the hand of Achilles.” His soul goes down to Hades “mourning its sad fate and bidding farewell to the youth and vigor of its manhood”: and Hector answers a corpse. Why foretell my doom? Who knows: maybe Achilles is the one my spear takes first. He plants a foot on the body, pulls his spear free, and goes hunting Automedon and the horses: but the immortal team the gods gave Peleus carries the charioteer out of reach, the one piece of Achilles’ equipment that can never be caught. So the book closes on its last exchange of goods: the dead man holds the truth, and the living man holds the armor and a deadline he has decided not to believe in. Everyone who dies in this book sees the future. Everyone who kills refuses to.

Last one

Apollo strikes first, Euphorbus second, and Hector: third: lands the finishing blow and makes the victory speech. Why does Homer spread the kill across three?

The sparing reading is half-true: “had twenty such men as you attacked me” does preserve Patroclus’ standing: and the pattern reading is genuinely prophetic, since an archer and a god will one day share the killing of Achilles. But watch where the scene spends its energy: Hector gets a long vaunt, complete with invented dialogue for Achilles, and the poem has already shown us everything the vaunt leaves out: the mist, the open hand, the spear in the back. The three-stage kill exists so that when Hector says “I killed him,” the audience can count. The dying correction: “you are yourself third only”: carries the authority the poem gives the dead, and the prophecy attached to it names Hector’s killer. He shrugs it off and keeps the armor. He is now wearing his own death notice and calling it a prize.

TLDR

Patroclus borrows Achilles' armor, saves the ships, kills Sarpedon, and ignores Achilles' limit by driving on toward Troy. Apollo strips him of force, Euphorbus wounds him, and Hector kills him.

Who’s who

Patroclus

Achilles’ friend and squire

He comes to Achilles weeping like a spring falling over a cliff, borrows the armor to frighten Troy, and follows the plan perfectly until he starts winning. A god, an unknown, and Hector kill him: in that order.

Achilles

Still at the ships

He can’t relent and can’t watch the fleet burn, so he splits the difference: his armor, his men, his horses: everything but himself. Zeus grants his prayer for Patroclus by exactly half.

Sarpedon

King of Lycia, son of Zeus

He shames his running Lycians, meets the man in Achilles’ armor in the open, and falls like a tree cut for ship timber. His last words are a work order: save my armor, or my name will haunt you.

Hector

Commander of Troy

He wavers at the Scaean gates until a god talks him back in, strikes the third and final blow, claims the whole kill: and laughs off the dying prophecy that names his own killer.

Apollo

God of the silver bow

The first killer. He beats Patroclus off the wall of Troy three times, then strikes him from behind in a mist: helmet off, spear shattered, cuirass undone: and leaves him bare for the mortals.

Zeus

King of the gods

He says out loud that he could carry his son home alive, loses the argument to Hera’s one unanswerable point, and sheds a rain of blood on the earth for the death he signs off on.

Hera

Queen of the gods

She never tells Zeus he can’t save Sarpedon: only what it would cost: every god has children in this war, and a rule broken once out of love is broken for everyone.

Glaucus

Lycian captain

Sarpedon’s cousin, too wounded to hold a sword when the dying charge lands on him. He prays to Apollo, is healed on the spot, and spends the rest of the book holding Troy to its debt.

Euphorbus

Dardanian spearman

Best spearman of his generation, the poem says: and he proves it by putting a spear in the back of a stunned, disarmed man and running into the crowd. Second of the three.

Automedon

Achilles’ charioteer

He drives the immortal horses for Patroclus, cuts the dead trace-horse loose mid-battle without dropping the reins, and at the end those horses carry him: not his master: out of Hector’s reach.

Cebriones

Hector’s driver

A bastard son of Priam, killed holding the reins by Patroclus’ jagged stone. The joke about the graceful diver, made standing over him, is the surest sign of how far the plan has drifted.

Ajax

Defender of the ships

He holds the last ship past the limit of a body: sweat pouring, shoulder numb: until Hector’s sword cuts the head off his spear and he reads the sign: Zeus wills the fire.

Meriones

Cretan fighter

He trades taunts with Aeneas over a missed throw until Patroclus shuts it down with the book’s epigraph: blows for battle, words for council: fight, and say nothing.

Hypnos

Sleep, twin of Death

The consolation clause in Hera’s bargain: when Sarpedon falls, Sleep and Death carry the washed, anointed body home to Lycia for mound and pillar.

Thetis

Achilles’ goddess mother

Present only in what she packed: the chest of shirts and rugs, the cup no man but her son may drink from: and the precedent Hera invokes, of divine parents whose sons die anyway.
