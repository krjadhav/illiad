The Iliad, retold

Book 17

Book Seventeen, after Samuel Butler’s translation — two armies price a dead man above the living, Hector dresses in the armor of the man who will kill him, and immortal horses learn to weep.

Patroclus is dead, and Book Seventeen is about what he is worth. Not his life — that account is closed. His body. For one entire book, two armies fight from morning to dark over an object with no military value at all: it can’t hold ground, can’t fight, can’t even feel what’s being done to it. Both sides understand this perfectly, and both spend living men on it anyway — named men, mourned one by one as they drop. If you want to know what the Trojan War is actually about, this is the book, because for a day the war stops pretending to be about territory and fights openly over the only thing it was ever about: meaning.

Menelaus gets there first. He stands over the body “as a cow stands lowing over her first calf” — a startling simile for a hard man, and exactly right, because what he’s guarding can no longer guard itself. Then Euphorbus arrives with a claim: I was the first Trojan to drive a spear into Patroclus; step back and let me have my full glory. He isn’t lying — he did hit him first, after a god had done the real work — and the demand shows you how the economy runs: credit for a kill is a property right, and he’s willing to die enforcing it. Menelaus answers like a man twenty years older: “boasting is an ill thing”; your brother made the same speech, and “his own feet never bore him back to gladden his wife and parents”; and then the shrug that closes it — “even a fool may be wise after the event.” Euphorbus throws anyway, fails, and Menelaus leans his whole weight behind a spear through the base of his throat.

Then Homer does what Homer does: he stops to grieve the man he just killed. The boy’s hair, “like that of the Graces,” braided in bands of silver and gold, is “bedrabbled with blood,” and the poem hands him the gentlest simile in the book — a fine young olive tree a man raises in a clear space where water is abundant, full of promise, putting out white blossoms in every breeze, until a hurricane arrives from nowhere and levels it with the ground. That is the obituary of a boaster who picked the wrong fight, and the poem still mourns him: even a death he asked for costs the world something. Homer keeps no villains. He keeps saplings, and weather.

Apollo — working today in borrowed faces, the first of several — points Hector at the scene, and Menelaus, alone over the body with a wall of Trojans coming on, holds the most honest staff meeting in the Iliad, with himself. “Alas! what shall I do?” If I abandon the armor and the body of the man “who has fallen fighting on my behalf,” any Greek who sees me will “cry shame upon me.” If I stay, they will swarm one man. But when a man fights “in despite of heaven” against someone a god is befriending, he will soon rue it — so let no Greek think ill of a retreat. And if I could find Ajax, we would come back and “fight Hector and heaven too.” “This, of many evils, would be the least.” Every fighter in the poem runs this arithmetic. Menelaus is the only one Homer lets us overhear — shame weighed against survival on an open ledger — and it does not make him smaller. It makes everyone else’s silence look like performance.

Pause — check yourself

Alone over the body, Menelaus talks himself through it: stay and be swarmed, retreat and risk the cry of shame, or go find Ajax and come back. What makes this soliloquy unlike every hero-moment before it?

The cowardice reading fails on the outcome: he doesn’t abandon the body, he re-resources the problem — retreats, fetches Ajax, and comes straight back, which is exactly what the plan said. Piety is in the math, but as one input among several, and that’s the point: heaven gets a row in the ledger, not a veto. Every fighter runs this calculation; the code requires them to run it silently and announce only the brave conclusion. Menelaus — mid-tier, unglamorous, with the least image to protect — is the only one Homer lets us overhear, and the honesty is the heroism.

He backs off like a lion pushed from a stockyard, “sulkily,” finds Ajax on the left of the line, and says the sentence the rest of the book obeys: come with me to dead Patroclus, “if so be that we may take the body to Achilles — as for his armour, Hector already has it.” They are barely in time. Hector has stripped the corpse and is dragging it off, meaning to cut off the head and fling the trunk “before the dogs of Troy.” Then Ajax arrives with his shield “like wall,” and everything changes shape. Hector falls back into the crowd and sends the armor to the city, “a great trophy for himself”; Ajax plants himself over the body like a lion over its cubs, brows drawn down until they cover his eyes. He will do this all day. Other men in this poem surge and blaze. He anchors.

What happens next is the most interesting Trojan scene in the book: Glaucus, senior Lycian now that Sarpedon is dead, looks at Hector and calls him a fraud to his face. You make “a brave show,” but in a fight you are “sadly wanting”; a “runaway” has no claim to a reputation like yours. Then the specific charge: Sarpedon was your guest-friend and your comrade, he bled for your city for years, and when he fell you left him “to be the spoil and prey of the Danaans.” Why should any Lycian die for a man of less note? “If the Lycians will listen to me, they will go home, and leave Troy to its fate.” Notice what the accusation is made of. Not sentiment — contract law. And his proposed remedy is the book’s thesis stated as strategy: drag Patroclus into Troy and the Argives will trade for him — Sarpedon’s armor back, “and we should get his body to boot.” The corpse in the dust between the armies is not a corpse to anyone on this field. It is the hardest currency in the war.

Hector’s answer is telling: he never rebuts the charge about Sarpedon at all. He denies the cowardice — “Jove’s will is stronger than ours,” the standard alibi — and then does something no rebuttal could match: he leaves the battle, runs down the men carrying Achilles’ armor to Troy, and changes into it. Homer gives the armor its genealogy like a curse: the gods gave it to Peleus; Peleus grew old and gave it to his son; “but the son did not grow old in his father’s armour.” Neither did its next wearer. Now Hector fits it to his own body — dressing himself in the identity of the one man on earth guaranteed to kill him. And Zeus watches him do it, wags his head, and mutters the poem’s coldest tenderness: “A! poor wretch, you arm in the armour of a hero… and you reck nothing of the doom that is already close upon you.” Then the sentence, structured like a payment: I do indeed endow you with great might now — “but as against this you shall not return from battle to lay the armour of the son of Peleus before Andromache.” Strength up front, and the price is home. Zeus even names her. He knows exactly what he is foreclosing — the wall, the boy, the farewell of Book Six — and he grants the power anyway: pity and verdict in one motion of the head.

Pause — check yourself

Zeus watches Hector strap on Achilles’ armor, shakes his head, murmurs “poor wretch” — and then fills him with strength. Why both, in one breath?

The trap reading gets the mechanics right — the strength does carry Hector toward the man who will kill him — but it fails on the staging: Zeus is muttering to himself, alone. Nobody baits a man in private; there is no audience to deceive, and nothing conditional in what he says — the doom is “already close upon you,” fixed before the gift. That’s what makes it a settlement. Zeus even names what’s being foreclosed — Hector will never lay this armor “before Andromache” — the whole farewell of Book Six unwound in a clause. The armor has now doomed both men who borrowed it, and the god watching the third man dress can pity him, and pay him, but not save him.

Armored as Achilles, blazing so that he “seemed to all of them like the great son of Peleus himself,” Hector gathers the allies and makes an offer that would embarrass a general who believed his own speeches: whoever drags Patroclus into Trojan hands, “I will give him one half of the spoils while I keep the other. He will thus share like honour with myself.” Honor, published with a price sheet. They charge — “fools that they were,” says Homer, who has read ahead — and Ajax gives Menelaus his own unsentimental read: I am less concerned for the body, which “will shortly become meat for the dogs and vultures of Troy,” than for “the safety of my own head and yours.” They shout for help; help comes — Oileus’s fast son, Idomeneus, Meriones, then more than the poet can name — and the two sides settle into the day’s real work. The Greeks tell each other: better the earth open and swallow us here than let Troy carry him off. The Trojans answer in kind: though we fall to a man beside this body, let none shrink. Two armies, each announcing that it will pay any number of living men for one dead one.

Homer’s simile for the fight is deliberately unheroic: a man hands his crew an ox-hide drenched in fat, and they stand in a ring and stretch it, tugging until the moisture leaves and the fat soaks in. That is the day — the body hauled “hither and thither within the compass of but a little space,” Trojans pulling toward Ilius, Greeks toward the ships, the earth running red on both sides of it. Stand back and ask the cold question: why? A body holds no pass and guards no ships. But the Greeks are fighting for the funeral — for the rites that finish a death properly instead of letting it end as “meat and morsel for Trojan hounds” — and for the ability to face Achilles with something other than nothing. The Trojans are fighting for a trophy that would prove heaven is theirs, and for Glaucus’s trade — leverage in a market where corpses buy corpses. Rites, ransom, trophy: not one motive on either side is material, and every man present prices it above his own life. Ground is cheap in this war. Meaning is the expensive thing, and a dead friend is made of it.

Pause — check yourself

The body can’t fight, can’t hold ground, can’t feel what’s done to it — and hundreds of living men are spent on it before dark. What are the armies actually buying?

Leverage is the strong distractor because Glaucus proposes exactly that trade — Patroclus for Sarpedon’s armor and body. But leverage only explains the Trojans; the Greeks aren’t holding their own dead hostage. They’re fighting for the funeral, for a death finished properly instead of ending as “meat and morsel for Trojan hounds,” for the right to face Achilles with something other than shame. Only the meaning reading covers both armies at once: every motive on the field today is symbolic, and every man prices it above his own life. Which is the war in miniature — the ground was always cheap. The whole thing runs on the expensive stuff.

And then the poem walks away from the scrum and finds the day’s grief standing where no one is fighting. The horses of Achilles — immortal, a gift from the gods to Peleus — have pulled out of the battle, and they are weeping. Automedon, their driver now that their driver is dead, lashes them, coaxes them, scolds them; nothing. They will not go back to the ships and they will not go into battle. They stand with the chariot “as a pillar set over the tomb of some dead man or woman,” heads bowed to the ground, hot tears falling, their manes spilling from under the yoke-straps and trailing in the dust. The poem has shown us grief performed all along — breast-beating, formal laments, dirges on cue. This is the other kind: motionless, useless, and it has spread to animals that cannot die.

Zeus sees them, and for the second time in the book pities what he watches. What he says over them is the closest the Iliad ever comes to stating its thesis in plain words: “Poor things, why did we give you to King Peleus who is a mortal, while you are yourselves ageless and immortal? Was it that you might share the sorrows that befall mankind? for of all creatures that live and move upon the earth there is none so pitiable as he is.” Read it twice. The king of the gods, mid-war — his war, his design — looks down and says: there is nothing in the world more wretched than man. And his regret is specific: we should never have given them to a mortal, because everything given to mortals becomes a way of losing. The horses, the armor, the strength he just handed Hector — every gift that crosses the line from immortal to mortal turns into a vector of grief on arrival. Zeus cannot fix this. He does what a god can: promises that Hector will never drive these horses, and breathes enough strength into them to carry Automedon out alive.

Pause — check yourself

Immortal horses stand like a gravestone and cry, and Zeus asks why the gods ever gave them to a mortal — for of all creatures on earth “there is none so pitiable” as man. What is the poem doing in this pause?

The indictment reading tempts because it’s adjacent to something true: divine gifts do become vectors of grief in this poem — the horses, the armor, the strength Hector was just handed. But Zeus’s line isn’t about divine fault, and he proposes no reform; the regret is that immortal things were made to “share the sorrows that befall mankind” — that contact with mortals means contact with loss. That isn’t a confession, it’s a definition: to be human is to be the grieving kind of thing. The poem has been showing this since Book One. Here, for one still moment beside two weeping animals, it stops showing and says it.

The horses shake the dust from their manes and fly back in, and Automedon discovers what Patroclus was worth in purely technical terms: one man cannot hold the reins and use a spear at once. A friend, Alcimedon, climbs up to drive, and he jumps down to fight. Hector spots “weak hands” on the immortal team and recruits Aeneas to help capture it — the two best men in Troy, hunting a chariot — and the raid costs them Aretus, dropped like an ox under the axe, before the two Ajaxes wade in and break it up. Automedon strips his kill and says the honest thing that vaunting usually exists to hide: “I have done little to assuage my sorrow… for the man I have killed is not so good as he was.” Grief, in this economy, has an exchange rate, and he knows he is still deep in deficit.

Meanwhile the gods work the field like handlers, one to a corner. Athena comes down — Zeus “had changed his mind,” Homer notes, as casually as that — takes the shape of old Phoenix, and puts strength into Menelaus, making him “bold as a fly, which, though driven off, will yet come again and bite if it can, so dearly does it love man’s blood.” It is the least flattering blessing in epic and one of the truest: persistence, not brilliance, is his whole gift. He kills Podes, Hector’s boon companion at the table. So Apollo, in another borrowed face, needles Hector: you have quailed before Menelaus, “who has ever been rated poorly as a soldier,” and he has just carried a corpse off single-handed and killed your true comrade. Notice the gods’ instrument of choice, on both sides: not thunder. Shame. They motivate heroes exactly the way heroes motivate each other, because it is the only lever that always works. Then Zeus does use thunder — wraps Ida in cloud, shakes the aegis, and hands the rout to Troy.

Through all of this, the men at the center have been fighting in the dark. Zeus has hidden the fight over the body in a fog so thick that sun and moon might as well be gone, while a spear-cast away the rest of both armies skirmish at leisure “in full daylight with brilliant sunshine all round them.” Darkness is reserved for the important dead. Inside it, Ajax finally says what everyone can see — “even a fool may see that father Jove is helping the Trojans”; every spear they throw lands, brave man or coward, and ours all fall harmless. And then he prays, and the prayer is the most human sentence in the book because of what it does not ask for. Not victory. Not rescue. “O father Jove, lift this cloud from over the sons of the Achaeans; make heaven serene, and let us see; if you will that we perish, let us fall at any rate by daylight.” Kill us in the light, if kill us you must. And “Father Jove heard him and had compassion upon his tears,” and cleared the sky — granting the seeing and not the saving, which is exactly what was requested. It is the war’s whole theology in one transaction: the gods will rarely spare you, but they can be moved to witness you. For these men, being seen is the need that outlasts the need to live.

Last one

Fog so thick he cannot tell friend from friend, and Ajax prays — not for rescue, not for victory: “if you will that we perish, let us fall at any rate by daylight.” What is he actually asking for?

The fair-fight reading fails on the prayer’s own premise: Ajax has already conceded the outcome — “even a fool may see that father Jove is helping the Trojans” — and he opens with “if you will that we perish.” He isn’t negotiating the result; he’s negotiating the conditions. In a culture where the self is what others see of you, dying in the dark isn’t just death — it’s erasure: no witness, no story, no “he fell well” carried home. So he asks to be visible, and Zeus, who would not save them, is moved to tears and grants exactly that: the seeing, not the saving. Witness is the last human need, and this is the one prayer in the book answered in full.

With the light back, Ajax turns to logistics: someone must tell the son of Peleus, who cannot yet have heard — a guess that is exactly right. Achilles is at the ships assuming his friend is alive and on his way back, because his goddess mother told him the future all his life, and told him everything except this. Menelaus goes looking, ranging the host “as keenly as an eagle,” and finds Antilochus, Nestor’s young son, on the far left of the battle. The delivery is four sentences with no cushioning in them: heaven is heaping calamity on us. Patroclus has fallen. Run to the ships and tell Achilles. As for the armor, Hector has it. Antilochus is “struck with horror”: he stands a long time unable to speak, his eyes filling; then he hands his armor to a friend and runs, weeping as he goes. That is the book’s real cliffhanger — not the tug-of-war but a boy crossing the plain in tears, carrying a sentence that will end the strike, the stalemate, and eventually Troy. The worst news in the Iliad travels at the speed of one grieving runner.

Behind him, the men who stayed do the heavy, unglamorous thing. Menelaus and Meriones get the body up in their arms “with a great effort,” and Homer compares them to mules dragging a ship-timber down a rough mountain track, panting and sweating — which is what carrying your dead actually is — while the two Ajaxes hold the rear, “one in heart as in name,” like a wooded ridge that turns a river in flood. The Trojans come on like hounds after a wounded boar and scatter, pale, whenever the boar turns; Aeneas and Hector press hardest, all the way to the trench. And there the book simply stops — “of fighting there was no end” — armor scattered, retreat in progress, the runner still running. No duel today, no winner, no blaze of glory for anyone. Just this: the man the poem has always rated in the middle — the wronged husband, the softer brother, “rated poorly as a soldier” — killed the first man who speared Patroclus, held the body alone, did the honest math, fetched Ajax, chose the messenger, and carried his friend out on his own arms. Menelaus never gets a book of glory. He gets this one instead, which is better: the day the poem shows what a merely decent man is worth, on the day the thing being fought over is meaning itself.

TLDR

The armies fight over Patroclus' body while Hector wears Achilles' armor. Menelaus sends Antilochus to warn Achilles, and the Ajaxes hold the Trojans off long enough for the Greeks to carry the corpse away.

Who’s who

Menelaus

King of Sparta

The day belongs to the poem’s mid-tier man: he kills Euphorbus, does his cost-benefit math out loud, fetches Ajax, picks the messenger, and helps carry the body home. No blaze of glory — just everything that needed doing.

Hector

Commander of Troy

He strips Achilles’ armor from Patroclus and puts it on — the third man in bronze that has stopped bringing its wearers home. Zeus watches him dress, pities him, and pays him in strength.

Ajax

Son of Telamon

The anchor. He plants his shield over the body like a wall, orders the line to stand together and win no glory, and prays for the one thing left worth asking: to die in the light, where it counts.

Patroclus

The dead man at the center

He never moves and never speaks, and the whole book orbits him. Both armies price his body above their own lives — a funeral to the Greeks, a trophy and a trade to Troy.

Euphorbus

Son of Panthous

He speared Patroclus first and wants the credit, even against Menelaus. He dies for the claim, and Homer mourns him with the book’s gentlest simile: a young olive tree, flowering, leveled by one gust.

Glaucus

Lycian captain

Sarpedon’s countryman calls Hector a coward to his face: you left your guest-friend’s body to the enemy, so why should Lycians die for yours? Even loyalty, it turns out, keeps a ledger.

Aeneas

Prince of Troy

Shamed back into the fight by a god in an old servant’s shape, he rallies the line and joins Hector in the hunt for the immortal horses. The horses decline to be caught.

Automedon

Charioteer of Achilles

Left alone with a team no mortal but Achilles can drive, he hands the reins to a friend and jumps down to fight. His vaunt over his kill is the book in one line: it does little to assuage his sorrow.

Zeus

King of the gods

He pities Hector and dooms him in the same breath, pities the weeping horses, and says the poem’s thesis out loud: of everything that lives and moves on earth, nothing is more wretched than man.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

Sent down when Zeus changes his mind, she borrows old Phoenix’s voice and makes Menelaus bold as a fly — the creature that keeps coming back no matter how often it’s driven off.

Apollo

God of archery

He spends the day in borrowed faces — goading Hector twice, shaming Aeneas once — always arriving at the exact moment a man’s resolve wobbles. Shame is his instrument, and it never misses.

Antilochus

Nestor’s young son

Chosen to carry the news no one wants to hold. He goes speechless, fills with tears, hands his armor to a friend, and runs weeping across the plain toward Achilles.

Achilles

Still unaware

At the ships, expecting his friend back alive any moment. His goddess mother told him the future all his life — and never this part. The news is on its way.
