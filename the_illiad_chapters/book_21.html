The Iliad, retold

Book 21

Book Twenty-One, after Samuel Butler’s translation: Achilles chokes a river with corpses, fire fights water, and the gods turn the end of the world into a family brawl.

Book Twenty-One is what happens when Achilles has no human limit left. He drives the Trojans into the river, catches twelve alive for Patroclus’ funeral fire, and then meets Lycaon, a man he once captured and sold. Lycaon does everything a supplicant should do: clasps the knees, tells the story, offers the old ransom logic. Achilles kills him anyway.

The speech over Lycaon is the book in miniature. Do not talk to me of ransom, Achilles says. Better men than you have died; I will die too; until then, every Trojan I meet goes down. This is not rage looking for compensation anymore. It is rage that has accepted death and therefore made itself impossible to bargain with.

Pause: check yourself

Lycaon supplicates Achilles with the old ransom protocol. Why does it fail?

The prior ransom matters because it proves the old system used to function. Achilles now rejects the system out loud. If death is coming for everyone, and Patroclus is already dead, no payment can matter enough to interrupt killing.

The river Scamander finally objects. Not because he has become gentle, but because his channel is clogged with corpses and his water cannot reach the sea. He raises Asteropaeus, a fighter descended from a river, against Achilles. Asteropaeus can throw with both hands and wounds him, barely. Achilles kills him too, then boasts that no river-lineage can save a man from Zeus’ grandson.

Pause: check yourself

Scamander rises against Achilles because the river is full of bodies. What kind of limit is this?

The river does not deliver a sermon about pity. It complains that its stream is blocked. That makes the limit colder and stronger: even if no human can stop Achilles, matter has capacity. Corpses have volume.

Then the landscape fights him. Scamander surges, rolls bodies out of his current, batters Achilles with waves, and nearly drowns the best warrior in the poem like a trapped animal in a flood. Poseidon and Athena steady him just enough to keep fate on schedule. Hera calls in Hephaestus, and fire goes to war with water: elms, willows, lotus, fish, eels, all burning until the river begs for terms.

Pause: check yourself

Hera sends Hephaestus against Scamander. What does the fire-and-water fight reveal?

Hierarchy is present, and Hephaestus is the god of craft, but the scene's pressure is escalation. A human killing spree has become a river crisis, then an elemental battle. The war keeps converting each limit into a larger violence.

Once the river yields, the gods start fighting one another like a family argument that has found weapons. Athena drops Ares with a stone and then knocks Aphrodite down when she tries to help him. Poseidon challenges Apollo over an old labor dispute at Troy; Apollo declines. Artemis mocks him for it, and Hera boxes her ears until she runs crying to Zeus. The gods reduce cosmic war to slapstick without making it harmless for anyone below.

Pause: check yourself

After the river nearly drowns Achilles, the gods start knocking each other down. Why does Homer let this become almost comic?

Distance is part of the joke, but contrast is the point. Achilles has just turned a river into a mass grave. When the gods copy the violence, it becomes squabbling because nothing permanent is at stake for them. Mortality is what gives the human scenes their weight.

At the wall, Priam orders the gates opened so the Trojans can get inside. Apollo saves the army by impersonating a Trojan and pulling Achilles away across the plain. It is not victory. It is evacuation under cover of a divine decoy. The book ends with Troy breathing because a god chose to waste one man’s time.

Last one

Apollo distracts Achilles while the Trojans rush through the gates. What has Troy won?

The rescue is tactical and temporary. Apollo does not defeat Achilles; he spends Achilles' attention. In Book Twenty-One, that is the only scarce resource Troy can still exploit.

TLDR

Achilles slaughters so many Trojans that the river Scamander rises against him. Hera sends Hephaestus' fire to subdue the river, the gods brawl absurdly, and Apollo distracts Achilles long enough for Troy to shut its gates.

Who’s who

Achilles

The unstoppable force

He kills past supplication, past ransom, and almost past geography. His own death no longer restrains him; it licenses him.

Lycaon

The failed suppliant

Once captured and sold by Achilles, he tries the old ransom system again and discovers that Patroclus' death has broken it.

Scamander

The river god

He rises not from pity but from overload: his channel is packed with corpses. The world itself refuses Achilles' throughput.

Asteropaeus

River-born fighter

A two-spear warrior descended from a river. He wounds Achilles lightly, then dies proving that even river blood cannot stop him.

Hephaestus

Fire against water

Summoned by Hera, he burns the river's trees, fish, and eels until Scamander begs him to stop.

Hera

Greek partisan

She escalates the river fight by calling in fire, then later boxes Artemis into tears during the gods' undignified brawl.

Apollo

The decoy

He refuses Poseidon's challenge, but saves the Trojan army practically: he distracts Achilles long enough for the gates to close.

Priam

King at the gates

Seeing the rout, he orders Troy's gates opened so the survivors can pour in before Achilles reaches them.

Poseidon

Schedule keeper

He steadies Achilles when the river nearly drowns him, not out of love, but because Achilles still has fated work to do.

Athena

Olympian enforcer

She supports Achilles and humiliates Ares and Aphrodite when the divine quarrel breaks into open fighting.

Ares

War knocked down

He charges Athena and is dropped by a stone, turning the god of war into another body on the field.

Artemis

Mocker mocked

She taunts Apollo for refusing Poseidon, then gets beaten by Hera and runs weeping back to Zeus.

Patroclus

The dead cause

His funeral still demands blood in Achilles' mind. Every failed plea in this book passes through that absence.

Aphrodite

Rescuer punished

She tries to lead Ares away after Athena drops him, and Athena knocks her down too.
