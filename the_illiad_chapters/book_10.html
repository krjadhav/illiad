The Iliad, retold

Book 10

Book Ten, after Samuel Butler’s translation: two sleepless armies have the same idea on the same night, and the whole difference is who they send.

Book Ten opens with a sound. The army sleeps; the two men responsible for it can’t. Agamemnon stands in the dark counting the Trojan watchfires and listening to what drifts across the plain: “the sound of pipes and flutes and of the hum of men.” That is the noise of an enemy that’s winning: relaxed enough to have music. He tears his hair out by handfuls before Jove and goes looking for Nestor, because Nestor is what he has left. The embassy to Achilles failed a few hours ago. There is no plan. There is only the hope that two worried men walking the camp at night might produce one.

The waking chain is a small portrait of command under stress. Menelaus turns out to be awake already, panther skin over his shoulders, and comes to his brother on his own: guilt is a good alarm clock; the war is for his sake. Agamemnon sends him off with instructions that tell you exactly how frightened he is: wake every man by his lineage and his father’s name, “give each all titular observance, and stand not too much upon your own dignity.” Flattery, deployed as emergency infrastructure. At midnight, losing, the king of kings discovers that titles flow uphill from the men who grant them. Nestor, woken, still finds breath to criticize Menelaus for sleeping: he isn’t: and Agamemnon, for once in this poem, defends his brother. Then the round: Odysseus woken with a word, Diomedes with Nestor’s heel in his ribs. He’s asleep on an ox hide among men using their shields as pillows, and he comes up growling the book’s best compliment: “Old man, your heart is of iron; you rest not one moment from your labours.”

They hold the council outside the wall, on the one patch of ground “clear of corpses”: clear because it’s the exact spot where Hector stopped killing when night fell. Nestor names the situation: “life and death are balanced as it were on the edge of a razor”: and then does something no one has done in nine books of war: he posts a wage. Whoever will creep out and grab a straggler, or overhear what the Trojans intend, gets a black ewe with her lamb from every chief, and a standing invitation to every feast and clan-gathering. He mentions fame too: “high as heaven in the mouths of all men”: but he says it, out loud, next to a price list. Nine years of men charging phalanxes for free, and suddenly this one job comes with compensation attached. It’s worth stopping on why.

Pause: check yourself

Nestor attaches a price list to the scouting job: a black ewe with her lamb from every chief, a seat at every feast. Nine books in, nobody has needed to be paid to fight. Why does this mission come with wages?

Hazard pay is the natural guess, and it isn’t wrong about the risk: but battle is also lethal, and nobody ever needed a ewe to charge a phalanx. What’s different tonight isn’t the danger; it’s the audience. The whole engine of this war runs on being seen: Helen watching from the wall, Hector unable to face the Trojans as a shirker. Strip away the witnesses and the engine has no fuel, so Nestor improvises a substitute currency: livestock and dinner invitations, fame’s cash equivalent. Notice he still promises fame “high as heaven”: but he has to say it, because in the dark it no longer goes without saying.

Diomedes volunteers on one condition: not alone. His reasoning is the most practical account of partnership in the poem: when two go together, one sees the opening the other misses; a man alone “is less full of resource, and his wit is weaker.” At that, everyone offers: both Ajaxes, Meriones, Nestor’s son, Menelaus, Odysseus. And Agamemnon says something with a false bottom: choose on merit, not birth: don’t “take the worst out of respect for his lineage, because he is of more royal blood.” Homer decodes it flatly: he said this because he feared for Menelaus. An order to spare his brother, dressed as a meritocracy memo, phrased so nobody has to be insulted by it. Diomedes picks Odysseus, obviously: the man Athena loves, with whom “we should pass safely through fire itself.” Odysseus’s reply is to cancel the compliments: say neither good nor ill of me, these men know me; the stars have moved, two-thirds of the night is spent. The best operator in the army treats praise as a scheduling delay.

The arming scene is an inventory of borrowed things. Diomedes gets a spare sword and a plain bull’s-hide skullcap “without either peak or crest.” No plume: the plume exists to be seen, and tonight the whole point is not to be. Night war strips off exactly the parts of the costume that daylight war is fought for. Then Homer spends a full paragraph on the helmet Meriones lends Odysseus: leather, felt-lined, studded with boar’s teeth: and traces its provenance through four hands: stolen by Autolycus out of a house in Eleon, given to Amphidamas, given again as a guest-gift to Molus, passed down to Meriones. Notice what Homer just did: he gave a hat a biography. Hold that thought; the men this raid kills won’t get one. Athena sends a heron on their right as they set out: too dark to see, but they hear its cry, and a heard omen is enough to pray on. Both men pray, both promise her offerings, and then they go, “like two lions prowling by night amid the armour and blood-stained bodies of them that had fallen.”

Meanwhile: Homer loves a mirror: Hector is holding the same meeting on the other side of the plain, for the same reason, at the same hour. But watch how differently the two sides source a volunteer. Nestor asked for boldness first and mentioned rewards second. Hector opens with the fee: “He shall be well paid if he will”: a chariot and the two best horses in the Greek camp, plus “infinite honour to boot,” honor as the throw-in item. What steps forward is Dolon: son of a rich herald, “ill-favoured, but a good runner,” an only son among five sisters. Homer hands you his whole file in a line and lets you do the math: money, no beauty, no brothers, and exactly one asset. Speed. This is a man who has never been anyone in the daylight economy of looks and lineage, presented with the first market where his one asset trades.

And Dolon doesn’t accept the offer: he renegotiates it. Hold up your sceptre, he says, and swear that the horses will be those horses: the team that carries Achilles. He has priced his own life, and the number he picks is the most famous horses alive, property of a man who isn’t even in the war. Hector swears it by Zeus without blinking: pledging away something he would have to win the entire war to touch. Butler gives the transaction its epitaph in one sentence: “The oath he swore was bootless, but it made Dolon more keen on going.” A worthless promise, and a real motivation. Both men leave the meeting satisfied, and neither one is holding anything.

Pause: check yourself

Dolon won’t move until Hector swears on his sceptre to hand over Achilles’ horses. Hector swears at once, and Homer calls the oath “bootless.” What has actually been transacted here?

Option b takes Dolon at his own valuation, which is precisely the trap he’s in. Look at what he demands: not gold: his family has gold: but the one prize in the war that cannot be transferred; Odysseus will laugh later that no mortal but Achilles can even drive that team. Greed didn’t overcome his fear; it negotiated with it, and fear accepted a price that could never be paid. Hector’s side is just as hollow: he swears by Zeus to deliver property he’d have to win the war to collect. Homer’s word is exact: bootless. Two men in perfect good faith, trading things neither of them has.

So the good runner sets out in a grey wolf skin and a ferret-skin cap, javelin in hand: and Odysseus hears him coming. The plan takes five seconds: let him pass, then cut him off from home. They lie down among the corpses: two live men indistinguishable, in the dark, from the dead: and let him trot by. When Dolon hears footsteps behind him, he stops, because he assumes it’s friends: Hector calling him back. By the time he sees enemies it’s a chase, “as a couple of well-trained hounds press forward after a doe or hare that runs screaming in front of them.” And even here, even now, Homer keeps the scorekeeping running: Athena puts fresh speed into Diomedes “for fear some other of the Achaeans might have the glory of being first to hit him.” Pitch dark, secret mission, one fleeing scout: and the credit ledger is still open. Diomedes throws to miss, on purpose; the spear over the shoulder is a sentence with one word in it. Dolon stands stock still. His teeth chatter. He turns pale, and he starts to cry.

What follows is the cleanest interrogation in ancient literature. Dolon leads with money, because money is his native language: take me alive, we have “great store of gold, bronze, and wrought iron,” my father will pay anything. Odysseus answers with the two most efficient words a captor has ever used: “Fear not.” Read them carefully: there is no promise inside. Not we’ll spare you; just “let no thought of death be in your mind.” A sedative, not a contract. And Dolon talks. Not under torture: fluently, eagerly, in complete paragraphs. Where’s Hector? He gives up the location of the entire Trojan command council. How are the watches set? He explains which contingents are asleep and why: the allies keep no guard; “their wives and children are not here.” And then he volunteers the upgrade nobody asked for: if you actually want into the camp, the Thracians are newly arrived, at the far edge, unguarded; their king is Rhesus; his horses are “whiter than snow and fleeter than any wind that blows,” his armor golden, fit for gods. He even proposes a settlement structure: take me to the ships, or tie me up here until you’ve verified my information. He’s still negotiating. He thinks he’s adding value.

Diomedes explains the decision the way you’d explain a business rule, and it’s worth quoting because there isn’t a gram of anger in it: if we ransom you or let you go, “you will come some second time to the ships of the Achaeans either as a spy or as an open enemy, but if I kill you and make an end of you, you will give no more trouble.” Dolon reaches up to touch his chin: the beard-clasp, the formal gesture of supplication, the move that has stopped sword-arms all through this poem: and the sword comes down before the gesture arrives: “his head fell rolling in the dust while he was yet speaking.” Mid-word. Homer has killed a great many men in nine books, and no one else dies in the middle of a sentence.

Pause: check yourself

Dolon answers every question truthfully, then volunteers more: the layout, the sleeping allies, Rhesus and his horses. Diomedes kills him anyway, mid-plea. What did Dolon get wrong about the deal he thought he was making?

Option b pattern-matches to Adrestus begging Menelaus, and the scenes rhyme on purpose: but listen to Diomedes’ actual reason. It isn’t vengeance or policy; it’s forward-looking risk math: released, “you will come some second time”; dead, “you will give no more trouble.” Option c is true as far as it goes: “fear not” promised nothing: but the deeper error was Dolon’s own sequencing: he paid first. He transferred everything he had of value before securing anything in return, and then offered to be tied up as collateral for goods already delivered. Information is the one currency that leaves your hands the moment you show it.

They strip the ferret-skin cap, the wolf pelt, the bow and spear, and Odysseus hangs them in a tamarisk tree as an offering to Athena, “the goddess of plunder”: then marks the spot with pulled-up reeds and tamarisk boughs so they can find it again on the way back. Tidy men, filing their receipts mid-raid. Then the Thracians. They’re sleeping “tired out with their day’s toil,” armor laid out beside them in three orderly rows, each man’s horses standing at his side, Rhesus in the middle. The division of labor takes one sentence: Odysseus assigns: you kill the men, I’ll manage the horses. And Diomedes goes to work: Homer’s simile is a lion on a flock that has no shepherd: smiting “right and left” until twelve men are dead. The detail the poem slows down for is Odysseus, following behind, dragging each fresh corpse aside by the feet. Not out of respect. So the stolen horses won’t shy at stepping on bodies, “for they were not yet used to them.” The raid’s one moment of tenderness is for the animals.

Twelve, and then the king makes thirteen: killed “as he was breathing hard,” with an evil dream hovering over his head that night. The dream, Homer says, was the grandson of Oeneus: the man standing over him. Rhesus dreams his own death in the same instant he dies of it, which is the closest he gets to being awake for it. And here is the thing to notice, because Homer is careful about it in a way that’s easy to miss: not one of the twelve gets a name. In every daylight battle the poem stops mid-carnage to tell you a dead man’s father, his river, his town, the wife left behind: Axylus who kept open house by the roadside, and it availed him nothing. Those one-line obituaries are the poem’s moral bookkeeping: every corpse costs something, and the poem pays. Here the entry reads: a “hideous groaning,” earth red with blood, bodies dragged clear by the ankles. Nothing else. Ask why, and be suspicious of the easy answers.

Pause: check yourself

Twelve Thracians die without waking, and Homer: who normally halts mid-battle to give a dead man his father, his town, his unfinished life: names none of them. Why withhold the obituaries here?

The judgment reading tempts because Dolon really did flag the Thracians as the unguarded ones: but the sleeping allies had their reason (“their wives and children are not here”), and Homer doesn’t moralize it. Think instead about what the biographies have been for all along: they mark death as a two-sided event: a life meeting an enemy, witnessed by the poem if by no one else. Tonight there is no meeting. Diomedes smites right and left; Odysseus drags the bodies clear so the horses won’t spook. The tenderness goes to the animals and the bookkeeping goes to nobody, because this isn’t war as the poem defines war: it’s work. By withholding the ritual, Homer makes you feel the difference.

Diomedes stands among the bodies and hesitates: not over what he’s done, but over whether to do more. Drag out the chariot? Carry off the golden armor? “Or whether again, he should not kill some more Thracians.” That’s the sentence the night has produced: killing as one option on a menu of errands. Athena appears with the professional’s answer: get back to the ships, now, before some other god wakes the Trojans: and she’s right on time, because Apollo “kept no blind look-out” and has seen her working. His countermove is to wake a kinsman of Rhesus, who starts up to find empty horse-lines and men “gasping in their death-agony,” and the camp dissolves into uproar behind the raiders. On this night the gods contribute exactly one thing each, and it’s the same thing: timing. Hers says when to stop. His says it’s already too late.

The ride home is a victory lap. Nestor hears hoofbeats and hopes out loud; the two ride in; there are handshakes and congratulations all round, and the old man marvels at horses like sunbeams. Odysseus files his report in three sentences: the king, the twelve, and “a thirteenth man: a scout”: and Butler notes that “he laughed as he spoke.” Then the closing sequence, in order: the horses are tied at Diomedes’s manger, where his own team stands eating sweet corn; Dolon’s bloody kit is hung on a ship’s stern for Athena; the two men wade into the sea and wash the sweat from their necks and thighs; then a warm bath; then olive oil; then they sit down to a meal and pour wine to the goddess. That’s the ending. Thirteen men are cooling on the far side of the plain, a head lies in the dust by a tamarisk tree, and the book closes on grooming, breakfast, and a drink. No one in the scene says anything about the night. The poem doesn’t either. Whether that silence is the poem’s or the men’s is the last question Book Ten asks you.

Last one

Thirteen men dead in their sleep, a severed head by a tamarisk tree: and the book ends with a swim, a hot bath, olive oil, breakfast, and a cup of wine for Athena. What is that ending doing?

The purification reading has real support: Hector wouldn’t pray with unwashed hands, and these men do pour wine to Athena: but look at what Butler actually says they wash off: “the sweat from their bodies, and from their necks and thighs.” Sweat. Not blood, not guilt: the residue of exercise. That word choice is the whole ending: the night raid leaves on a man exactly what rowing practice would. Nobody mentions Dolon pleading or the twelve who never woke; Odysseus laughed giving the summary. The horror isn’t that they celebrate: it’s that there is nothing, in the men or in the verse, that needs consoling. Routine is how atrocity gets digested, and Book Ten ends by showing you the digestion, mid-swallow, without comment.

TLDR

Both worried about the Greek setbacks, Agamemnon and Menelaos meet at night and agree to work on sending spies to the Trojan camp. Agamemnon goes to Nestor’s shelter, while Menelaos collects the other chiefs. Nestor addresses the collected leadership, and asks for volunteers to infiltrate the Trojan camp. Diomedes volunteers and chooses Odysseus to go with him. Odysseus’ armor includes a helmet of boar-tusks (Mycenaean). After prayers to Athena, Odysseus and Diomedes set out. Meanwhile, Hector has done the same thing on the Trojan side, choosing Dolon as his spy. Odysseus and Diomedes see Dolon coming, so they ambush him and chase him down. Odysseus interrogates Dolon, who answers all the questions without demur. Dolon describes the Trojan order of encampment, including the splendid chariot of the Thracian King Rhesos. Dolon pleads for his life, but Diomedes kills him anyway. Diomedes and Odysseus find the Thracians asleep, so they slaughter twelve of them and escape with the chariot and team of Rhesos, back to the Greek camp. On the way they pause to pick up the arms stripped from Dolon’s corpse for a dedication to Athena.

Who’s who

Odysseus

King of Ithaca

He runs the night: spots Dolon coming, conducts the interrogation on a comfort that promises nothing, manages the horses while Diomedes manages the killing, and laughs on the ride home.

Diomedes

Son of Tydeus

First to volunteer, asking only for a partner: two men together see more. He kills Dolon mid-sentence, then twelve sleeping Thracians and their king, and would keep going if Athena didn’t call time.

Dolon

Hector’s scout, briefly

Rich, ill-favoured, fast: an only son among five sisters. He prices his life at Achilles’ horses, tells his captors everything and then some, and is beheaded while still talking.

Rhesus

King of the Thracians

He arrived too late to fight and dies before he can wake, thirteenth of thirteen, an evil dream hovering over his head. His horses: whiter than snow, fleeter than wind: are why anyone knew his name.

Hector

Commander of Troy

He has the same idea as the Greeks on the same night, but opens his council with the fee. He swears on his sceptre to give Dolon horses he doesn’t own; Homer calls the oath bootless.

Nestor

Old king of Pylos

He proposes the mission and posts its wages: a black ewe with her lamb from every chief. It’s his heel in the ribs that wakes Diomedes: “old man, your heart is of iron.”

Agamemnon

Commander-in-chief of the Greek army

Sleepless, tearing his hair by handfuls while the Trojan camp enjoys its pipes and flutes. The rout is his to answer for, so the midnight council is his idea.

Menelaus

King of Sparta

Awake before his brother, panther skin over his shoulders, volunteering for the raid like everyone else. Agamemnon steers the choice away from him without ever saying his name.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

She sends a heron the raiders hear but cannot see, lends Diomedes speed so no one else claims the credit, and ends the raid at the exact right moment. Both men pray to her; both come home.

Apollo

God of the silver bow

He keeps no blind look-out: seeing Athena at work, he wakes a kinsman of Rhesus: after the horses are gone. His one move of the night is the alarm, and it comes too late.

Meriones

Cretan captain

Quartermaster of the raid: he lends Odysseus a bow, a quiver, and the boar’s-tusk helmet whose stolen-and-gifted pedigree is the fullest biography anyone gets in this book.

Achilles

Offstage, everywhere

Still out of the war and still its unit of account: the fee Dolon demands for his life is Achilles’ team: horses, Odysseus laughs, that no mortal but Achilles can even drive.

Zeus

King of the gods

Blamed for everything, consulted for nothing: Agamemnon decides Zeus has changed sides and “inclines towards Hector’s sacrifices,” and Hector swears Dolon’s worthless oath in his name.
