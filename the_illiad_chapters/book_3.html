The Iliad, retold

Book 3

Book Three, after Samuel Butler’s translation: a truce, a duel for Helen, and a goddess who refuses to lose her stake.

The armies that spent all of Book Two getting into motion finally meet, and Homer opens with one telling detail. The Trojans advance screaming, like cranes wheeling over a marsh. The Greeks come on in silence, “minded to stand by one another.” You already know something about how this war has gone for nine years.

Out of the Trojan line struts Paris: Butler calls him Alexandrus, same man, decoder below: in a panther skin, waving two spears, challenging any Greek to single combat. He is the reason for the entire war, and he’s dressed for a portrait. Menelaus sees him and is glad “as a hungry lion that lights on a carcass”: because Menelaus is the husband, the one man on the plain with a personal claim. And Paris takes one look at the man he wronged and dives back into the ranks like someone who’s just stepped on a snake.

Hector tears into him, and the rebuke has nine years of embarrassment behind it: evil-hearted Paris, fair to see, woman-mad, false of tongue: better you’d never been born. Won’t the Greeks laugh: Troy’s champion is beautiful and useless? You had the nerve to sail across the sea and steal a fighting man’s wife: and not the nerve to face him. If the Trojans weren’t so soft, he adds, they’d have stoned you long ago.

Paris’s reply is the most self-aware moment in the poem. Your scorn is just, he says: your heart is like a shipwright’s axe. But don’t taunt me with the gifts of Aphrodite: the gods’ gifts are precious, they give them where they please, and no one can have them for the asking. Translation: I didn’t earn my face, so I decline to be ashamed of it. And then he surprises everyone with an actual offer: sit both armies down, and I’ll fight Menelaus alone, for Helen and all her wealth. Winner takes her home. Everyone else swears peace and sails away.

Pause: check yourself

Hector calls Paris a pretty coward. Paris answers: “don’t taunt me with Aphrodite’s gifts: the gods give them where they please, and none can have them for the asking.” What’s he actually claiming?

It’s a genuinely coherent position: nobody earns their face, their charm, or their draw in life’s lottery, so mocking a man for his gifts is as empty as praising him for them. Notice, though, who finds this philosophy comfortable: the man holding the winning ticket. People who got lucky discover the irrelevance of merit much faster than people who didn’t.

Hector is delighted: this is the best idea his brother has ever had: and strides into no-man’s land holding his spear sideways to push his own ranks back, while Greek arrows are still coming at him, until Agamemnon shouts for a ceasefire. The terms are announced. Menelaus accepts, and adds one condition that tells you everything about diplomatic trust at Troy: fetch old Priam to swear the oath in person: “for his sons are high-handed and ill to trust,” said politely, in public, about the king’s own children. Young men’s minds are air, he says; an old man looks before and after. Both armies are overjoyed. Nine years of war, and it could all be over by dinner. They climb off their chariots, strip their armor, and sit down in two great rows with a strip of ground between them.

While the lambs are being fetched, the poem does something extraordinary. Iris goes to bring Helen to the wall: wearing, as usual, a borrowed face, her sister-in-law’s: and we get our first look at the woman the war is about. She’s at her loom, weaving a great purple web, and embroidered into it are the battles of the Trojans and Achaeans, fought for her sake. She is weaving the Iliad. The poem catches its own subject making a version of it, seeing the war the only way she’s allowed to: as needlework, from a room. Iris tells her the fight is now over her directly, and something old moves: Helen’s heart “yearned after her former husband, her city, and her parents,” and she goes to the wall in tears.

On the tower sit Troy’s elders: too old to fight, “fluent orators,” chirping, Homer says, like cicadas in a tree. They watch Helen approach and murmur the most quoted lines in the book: small wonder Trojans and Greeks endure so much, so long, for a woman so terribly like the immortal goddesses to look upon. And then, in the same breath: still: let them take her and go, before she breeds sorrow for us and our children. Both halves are honest. That’s what makes it the truest thing anyone says about her.

Priam calls her over gently: “my child”: sit here, see your first husband and your kin. “I lay no blame upon you: it is the gods, not you, who are to blame for this war.” Then, with the duel being staged below, the old king asks her to name the enemy. Who is that big, royal-looking one? Agamemnon, she says: a good king and a brave soldier, and once my brother-in-law, “if that world ever existed.” And the shorter one, built like a ram, pacing the ranks? Odysseus, master of stratagems. Here old Antenor leans in with a story: when Odysseus and Menelaus came on embassy about Helen years ago, I hosted them. Menelaus spoke fast and clean, few words, right to the point. Odysseus stood staring at the ground, holding the sceptre stiff as a post: you’d have called him a sulking fool: until the voice came out of that chest “like winter snow before the wind,” and after that no man alive thought about how he looked.

Then Helen scans the army for the two faces she most wants to find, and can’t: Castor the horse-breaker and Pollux the boxer: her brothers. Maybe they never sailed, she says. Or maybe they came and won’t show themselves for shame: the shame and disgrace I have brought on them. Homer closes the scene with one line, flat as a gravestone: she did not know that both already lay under the earth, at home in Lacedaemon. It’s the gentlest, cruelest sentence in the epic. She reads her own disgrace into what is actually grief.

Pause: check yourself

Helen assumes her brothers are hiding out of shame. Homer tells us: but not her: that they’re dead. Why hand the reader knowledge the character can’t have?

Helen has spent nine years being told she’s the cause of everything, and it’s reshaped her whole model of the world: absence must mean shame, and the shame must be hers. Homer lets us watch her be wrong in the saddest possible direction. That’s what internalized blame does: it explains everything, which is exactly how you know it’s no longer tracking reality.

The oath scene, when it comes, is heavy machinery. Priam drives out; wine is mixed; Agamemnon cuts wool from the lambs’ heads and prays the contract into the record, clause by clause: if Paris kills Menelaus, Helen stays and we sail home; if Menelaus kills Paris, Troy returns her, her wealth, and a fine to be remembered by generations unborn: and if Troy won’t pay it, I stay and fight until it does. He cuts the lambs’ throats. Both armies together pray the same prayer over the wine: whoever breaks these oaths, may their brains be poured out on the ground like this wine: theirs, and their children’s. Then Homer, in the coldest aside yet: thus they prayed: but not yet would Zeus grant it.

Priam won’t stay to watch. “I dare not see my son fight Menelaus: only the gods know which of them will fall.” He loads the dead lambs into his chariot and drives home. Everyone else on that plain is treating the duel as the afternoon’s entertainment; the father can’t. Meanwhile Hector and Odysseus measure out the ground and shake lots in a bronze helmet for first throw. Paris wins the toss. It’s the only thing he’ll win today.

Pause: check yourself

Priam tells Helen “I lay no blame on you: the gods are to blame,” and everyone at Troy talks this way. What does blaming the gods actually buy them?

It’s kindness and evasion in one move: and the poem has it both ways, because the gods really are pulling strings. But notice what the formula prevents: if the gods did it, no one has to ask why Paris wasn’t stoned years ago, why Helen wasn’t returned, why the elders’ “let her go” never became policy. “Larger forces” is how communities keep loving people whose choices are costing them everything.

The duel itself is short and almost ends the epic. Paris arms himself piece by piece: borrowing his brother Lycaon’s cuirass, because the man who started the war doesn’t own one: and throws first: his spear bounces off Menelaus’s shield. Menelaus throws with a prayer that’s really a policy argument: grant me revenge, so that men in ages to come shrink from wronging their host. His spear punches through shield and cuirass and rips Paris’s shirt at the flank: a flinch saves his life. The sword-stroke that follows shatters on the helmet, and Menelaus, mid-duel, files a complaint with heaven: “Father Zeus, of all gods you are the most despiteful”: then gives up on equipment entirely, grabs Paris by the horsehair plume, and starts dragging him bodily toward the Greek lines, the chin-strap strangling him as they go. The duel is decided. The war is over. A leather strap is completing the paperwork. Then Aphrodite snaps the strap. Menelaus is left holding an empty helmet, wheels around for the kill: and she wraps her man in mist and sets him down, of all the places in the world, in his own perfumed bedroom.

Then she goes to collect Helen from the wall, disguised as an old wool-dresser Helen loved back in Sparta: come home, he’s waiting on the bed, radiant: you’d think he was going to a dance, not coming from a duel. And Helen, recognizing the goddess’s neck and eyes under the disguise, delivers the bravest speech any mortal gives in these first books: why do you beguile me? Where will you hand me next: some man in Phrygia? Go sit beside him yourself. Be his wife. Be his slave. I am done garnishing his bed; every woman in Troy would be right to despise me. For one paragraph, the most owned person in the poem refuses. Then Aphrodite drops the costume: provoke me, bold woman, and I will hate you as extravagantly as I have loved you: I’ll make both sides burn for your death. Helen wraps her mantle around her and follows in silence.

Pause: check yourself

Aphrodite saves Paris’s life, then threatens Helen’s when she balks. What does the pairing tell you about divine favor in this poem?

Paris and Helen are both Aphrodite’s assets, and she protects her assets: that’s what the rescue was, not tenderness. The moment Helen tries to leave the portfolio, love converts to threat at full value, instantly. And she isn’t unusual: Athena’s deal with Achilles in Book One was a payment schedule. Being loved by a power: divine or otherwise: means being useful to it, on terms you don’t set.

The bedroom scene that follows is barbed in every line. Helen greets her husband with open contempt: I wish you’d died out there, under the hands of the brave man who was mine first. You used to boast you were the better fighter: go challenge him again. Actually, don’t; he’ll kill you. And Paris, untouched as ever, shrugs it off: Menelaus won today with Athena’s help; another day the gods who stand by me will settle it. Come to bed: I’ve never wanted you more, not even the first time. And she goes with him. Homer doesn’t tell us what she feels crossing that room. He doesn’t need to; he’s spent the whole book showing us.

Outside, Menelaus is still stalking the space between the armies, hunting for a man who is no longer on the battlefield. Nobody shields Paris: Homer notes that if any Trojan had seen him, they’d have pointed, “for they all hated him as they did death itself.” His own side. Agamemnon makes it official: victory to Menelaus; return Helen and her wealth; pay the fine. The Greeks roar. And by every rule sworn to that morning: fair ground, clear winner, the gods’ names on the contract: the Trojan War ends here. Add up who wants it to: the Trojans hate Paris, the Greeks want to go home, the elders on the wall already voted to give her back, and Helen herself said “take me” in everything but words. Every human being involved is ready for peace. Book Four opens with the gods deciding otherwise.

Last one

The duel was fair, the winner was clear, and both armies swore to the terms under the gods’ names. Why doesn’t the war end?

The stated cause: Helen: was just settled, fairly, in front of everyone. It changes nothing, because by year nine the war is also Hera’s grudge, Athena’s grudge, Zeus’s promise to Thetis, and every faction’s sunk honor. Wars, institutions, and feuds all share this property: they outlive their reasons, because the thing that keeps them running is never just the thing that started them. A technicality (Paris merely vanished) will do as a pretext: pretexts are cheap once the incentives want war.

TLDR

Paris offers single combat with Menelaus, and the war briefly looks solvable. Menelaus wins the duel in practice, but Aphrodite removes Paris from danger and the promised settlement collapses before it can become peace.

Who’s who

Paris

Prince of Troy, Helen’s second husband

The prince who started the war by stealing Helen, and the poem’s study in unearned gifts: beautiful, charming, and brave for about a minute at a time. Butler calls him Alexandrus.

Menelaus

King of Sparta, the wronged husband

Helen’s first husband, and the one man at Troy with a personal claim. Give him ten fair minutes and a working sword and he ends the war: he gets nine and a half.

Helen

The woman the war is for

Stolen queen, weaver of the war’s first tapestry, and its sharpest critic: including of herself. In Book Three she stands up to a goddess for exactly one speech.

Hector

Commander of Troy

Troy’s best soldier and Paris’s older brother, doing the eternal older-brother job: shaming him into responsibility, then organizing the mess that follows.

Priam

King of Troy

The old king of Troy. He blames the gods rather than Helen, swears the truce with Agamemnon, and drives home before the duel: a father can’t watch.

Aphrodite

Goddess of love

Goddess of love, and Paris’s patron. She snaps a chin-strap to save his life, deposits him in his bedroom, and reminds Helen that a goddess’s love is a leash.

Agamemnon

Commander-in-chief of the Greek army

Commander of the Greeks. He presides over the oaths, and when Paris vanishes from the field he declares the obvious: Menelaus won: pay up.

Odysseus

King of Ithaca

He measures the dueling ground and casts the lots. In Antenor’s story he’s the ambassador who looked like a fool right up until he opened his mouth. Butler calls him Ulysses.

Iris

Messenger of the gods

Messenger of the gods. She fetches Helen to the wall wearing her sister-in-law’s face: in time to watch two husbands fight over her.

Antenor

Trojan elder

A Trojan elder who once hosted Odysseus and Menelaus on their embassy for Helen. His scouting report on how each man spoke is the poem’s first character study.

Zeus

King of the gods

King of the gods. Both armies swear the truce in his name and pray for peace. Homer notes, in six cold words, that he does not grant it.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

Paris credits her for Menelaus’s good day. Her real work comes in Book Four, when the gods decide the truce has to die.
