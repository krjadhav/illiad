The Iliad, retold

Book 12

Book Twelve, after Samuel Butler’s translation — a wall built without permission, an omen read two ways, and the price of the best seat at the table.

The Trojans have reached the Greek wall — the rampart and staked trench the Achaeans threw up to protect their beached ships — and its timbers are ringing under the blows. Patroclus, meanwhile, is in a tent dressing the wound of Eurypylus, which is exactly where the poem wants him: parked. And just as the assault peaks, Homer does something no storyteller in a hurry would ever do. He stops the battle to tell you the wall is already gone.

Not gone in the story — gone in the world. The narrator climbs out of the war entirely: after Hector was dead, after the city was sacked in the tenth year, after the Greeks had sailed home, Poseidon and Apollo turned the mouths of eight rivers against the wall and let them run for nine days, Zeus raining the whole time “that he might wash it sooner into the sea.” Poseidon himself, trident in hand, surveyed the work, threw the beam-and-stone foundations into the deep, smoothed a great beach of sand over the place where the wall had been, and put the rivers back in their courses. Homer even gives the reason, in the tone of an inspector closing a file: the Greeks had built to guard their ships and their spoils, “but they had not offered hecatombs to the gods. It had been built without the consent of the immortals, and therefore it did not last.”

Sit with how strange that is. In the middle of the most urgent action the poem has staged, Homer hands you the demolition report — dated years in the future, executed by gods, filed against a permit violation. He even ties the wall’s lifespan to the plot: it stood firm so long as Hector lived and Achilles nursed his anger — a fortification with a narrative expiry date. And the digression does something bigger than theology. It moves the camera to where the narrator actually stands: centuries out, on a smooth beach, where the wall, the ships, and the whole size of that generation are things you have to be told about. Tonight’s battle will be fought, to the death, over a thing that is already sand.

Pause — check yourself

Homer halts the assault mid-swing to reveal that the wall is already gone — washed into the sea by eight rivers, years after the war. Why break the story to say so?

The missing-ruins reading is ancient and genuinely clever — Aristotle joked that the poet who invented the wall also flooded it away, tidying up after his own fiction — which is why option a tempts. But Homer states his reason, and it’s theological bookkeeping: “built without the consent of the immortals, and therefore it did not last.” No hecatombs, no permit; no permit, no permanence — even infrastructure sits on the gods’ ledger. And the digression raises the stakes rather than lowering them: the poem makes you watch men die tonight over a thing it has just told you is sand — which is the Iliad’s view of everything men build, walls and reputations alike. The only structure that outlasts the rivers is the telling.

Then the water recedes and we’re back in the present tense, where the wall still matters totally. Hector is working the front like a lion turning on the dogs — and Homer, mid-simile, drops one of his quiet invoices: “his courage is all undaunted, but his high spirit will be the death of him.” Not a prophecy; a billing notice, slipped into a subordinate clause. Hector wants his chariots across the trench. The horses refuse — they stand neighing on the brink, because the Greeks dug overhanging banks and planted sharp stakes, and a horse can read that argument even at a gallop. The engineering works perfectly on animals. Men are about to be a different problem.

Enter Polydamas, the man this book exists to introduce. His role in the Trojan command is to say the unwelcome thing, and his first counsel is pure staff work: it’s madness to force horses across a staked trench into a narrow killing ground; leave the chariots with the squires, cross on foot, in a body, behind Hector. And Hector is pleased — he does something better than agree. He springs to the ground in full armor, first, so that every man who sees him dismounts too, and the army re-forms into five companies on foot. The advice is good, and the scene shows you a command that can hear advice. But notice the condition under which it got heard: it pointed exactly where Hector already wanted to go.

Pause — check yourself

Polydamas calls it madness to drive horses across a staked trench; Hector is pleased and leaps down from his chariot on the spot. What is this scene setting up?

The hierarchy reading is the tempting one, because the scene really does show a functioning command: sound advice, instantly adopted, led from the front. But Homer is running a controlled experiment, and this is the control. Same advisor, same quality of reasoning, twice in one book — and the variable isn’t the advice, it’s the direction it points. Counsel that aims Hector at the ships is heard as wisdom; counsel that aims him home will be heard as treason, at spear-point. (And option c undersells Hector: he understands logistics fine — he’s the first man on the ground.) Whether you can take advice is only ever tested by advice you don’t like.

Homer immediately runs the other arm of the experiment. Asius son of Hyrtacus will not leave his horses — he drives his chariot at a gate the Greeks have left open for their own retreating men, sure the defense is finished. In the gateway he finds two Lapithae, Polypoetes and Leonteus, standing “like two high oak trees upon the mountains,” then fighting like wild boars, while stones come off the wall as thick as snow. Asius smacks his thighs and files a complaint upward: “Father Zeus, of a truth you too are altogether given to lying” — he had felt sure the Greeks couldn’t stand, and here they are defending their wall the way wasps defend their nests, and bees their little ones. Homer has already told us, in passing, that Asius dies for this, on the spear of Idomeneus. But keep his complaint. A man has just discovered that what he took for a promise from Zeus was only his own confidence wearing a god’s name. Someone far more important is about to make the same mistake.

Because the main force under Hector and Polydamas is still standing at the trench, staring at a sign nobody can un-see: an eagle crossing the host on the left with a monstrous blood-red snake in its talons — alive, twisting backward until it strikes the eagle in the neck, so that the bird drops it into the middle of the army and goes down the wind with a cry. The Trojans shudder. Polydamas steps up to Hector again, and listen to how he has to begin: at councils you are always given to rebuke me, even when I speak wisely — “you would have them support you always.” The cost of dissent gets stated before the dissent does. Then the reading, which is careful and correct: we are the eagle. We may break through the gates and the wall; the Greeks may give way; but we will not come home in good order, and we will leave many men behind us. The eagle did not get to keep the snake. Any expert seer, he says, would read it exactly so.

Hector looks at him fiercely, and out comes the most quoted sentence of defiance in ancient literature. Your reading displeases me; heaven must have robbed you of your reason. You’d have me forget the counsels of Zeus — the promises he made me, “and he bowed his head in confirmation” — and be ruled instead by birds. What do I care whether they fly toward dawn or dark, on my right hand or my left? “There is one omen, and one only — that a man should fight for his country.” And then, in the same breath as the deathless line, the rest of the speech: why are you so afraid? You won’t die at the ships anyway — you’re not steadfast enough to be in danger. But if you hang back from the fight, or talk one other man out of it, “you shall fall forthwith before my spear.”

Read cold, the famous line looks like rationalism — a man sweeping the whole apparatus of bird-signs off the table in favor of plain duty. But look at what he actually appeals to: not reason against religion, but one divine message against another. Zeus nodded to him personally; that beats a bird a colleague interpreted. He isn’t above omens. He has a favorite. And the tell is the threat at the end — an argument that is winning doesn’t need to promise to kill the other side of the debate. What makes the moment terrible rather than merely stubborn is that Zeus immediately backs him: a wind off Ida drives the dust into the Greeks’ faces, and the Trojans surge at the wall, tearing down breastworks and heaving up the buttresses. When your judgment starts failing at the exact moment heaven starts subsidizing you, there is no signal left that can reach you. The promise is real. It covers glory today. Hector is spending it as if it covered everything after.

Pause — check yourself

“There is one omen, and one only — that a man should fight for his country.” The line has been quoted for millennia as proud secular defiance. What is it in context?

The secular reading requires deleting the sentence before the famous one: Hector’s stated ground is “the counsels of Jove, and the promises he made me — and he bowed his head in confirmation.” A man arguing sign-against-sign is not above signs; he has a favorite. Option b is the strong distractor because it’s Hector’s own argument, and it isn’t crazy — Zeus does outrank birds. But the promise covers glory today, not safety after, and Polydamas’ reading — we breach the wall and don’t come home in good order — turns out exactly right. Watch the tells: a winning argument doesn’t end with “you shall fall forthwith before my spear,” and the cruelest touch is that Zeus immediately sends a tailwind. The line deserves its fame — fighting for your country is a real loyalty, plainly stated. The poem just hangs it at the precise moment conviction stops being courage and becomes deafness.

On the wall, the defense is being managed rather than merely fought. The two Ajaxes walk the battlements — Ajax son of Telamon and his smaller namesake — handing out fair words to some and sharp ones to any man they catch slacking, with a line every manager since has rediscovered: Argives, good, bad, and indifferent — “there was never fight yet in which all were of equal prowess” — there is now work for all of you. Stones fall in both directions, thick as one of Zeus’s snowfalls that bury the headlands and the tilled fields and stop only at the breaking sea. The wall holds. So Zeus commits his reserve: he turns his own son against the Greeks — Sarpedon, king of Lycia, sent at the wall like a famished lion at a fenced sheepfold, who knows the shepherds and the dogs are waiting and comes on anyway.

What happens next is the clearest statement of the aristocratic bargain anyone ever wrote down. Sarpedon turns to Glaucus with a question: why do we two, in Lycia, get the seats of honor? Why the choicest cuts, the cups kept brimming, the great estate on the banks of the Xanthus — orchard and wheatland — and why do men look at us as though we were gods? Therefore: it becomes us to stand at the head of the Lycians and bear the brunt of the fight, so that any man in the ranks can say, our princes eat the fat of the land and drink the best of the wine — but they are fine fellows; “they fight well and are ever at the front in battle.” No one before or since has read the invoice out this plainly. The land and the full cups are not tribute to their excellence. They are wages, and the job is dying first.

Then the kicker, which lifts the speech from sociology into philosophy. My good friend, Sarpedon says, if by staying out of this fight we could escape old age and death forever after, I would neither press forward myself nor bid you do so. But death “in ten thousand shapes hangs ever over our heads, and no man can elude him” — therefore let us go forward, “and either win glory for ourselves, or yield it to another.” He is not pretending the front rank is safe, and he is not pretending glory outweighs life. He is saying the premium is due whether or not you buy the policy — death collects from cowards on the same schedule — so the only thing actually for sale is what your death purchases. Glaucus heeded his saying, Homer reports, and the pair led the Lycians up the wall. The deal closes. It always closes. That is what makes it a bargain and not a trick.

Pause — check yourself

Sarpedon asks Glaucus why Lycia serves them the choicest cuts, keeps their cups brimming, and grants them the estate on the Xanthus — and answers: therefore we fight in front. What kind of speech is this?

The timing makes the pep-talk reading tempting — it is a battlefield speech, and it works; Glaucus follows him up the wall. But no motivator includes Sarpedon’s concession: if we could skip this fight and be ageless and immortal, “I should neither press forward myself nor bid you do so.” A pep talk hides the exits; Sarpedon points at them and explains why they’re fake — death in ten thousand shapes collects on the same schedule whether you buy glory or not. That concession also sinks option c: the perks aren’t proof the princes are better men, they’re wages for a job, and the job is standing where the spears land first. It is the frankest statement of aristocracy’s terms on record — privilege that prices its own obligations is the only kind that can look the ranks in the eye.

The Lycian assault lands on the stretch of wall held by Menestheus, who looks down, sees Sarpedon and Glaucus “bringing destruction with them,” and can’t make himself heard over the din of battered shields and hammered gates — so he sends a runner: bring the Ajaxes, both if possible, or at the very least Ajax son of Telamon, and Teucer the famous bowman with him. They come. Ajax announces himself by lifting a jagged stone off the battlement — a stone a man in the bloom of youth could hardly hold “as men now are,” Homer says, measuring us against them and finding us small — and crushing Epicles’ four-crested helmet, and the head inside it. Then Teucer puts an arrow into Glaucus’s bare shoulder as he climbs, and Glaucus does an intensely human thing: he slips back down covertly, so no Greek will see that he’s hit and taunt him. Wounded, mid-assault, and still managing the enemy’s narrative on the way down.

Sarpedon is stung with grief when he sees Glaucus go, and does not stop. He spears Alcmaon, then grips the battlement with both hands and pulls — and the whole panel comes away, “a breach through which many might pass.” The engineered thing gives up before the man does. Teucer hits him — but on the shield-strap, because Zeus will not have his son fall by the ships’ sterns; Ajax hustles him back off the rampart, and Sarpedon, retiring only a little, turns and shames his Lycians forward: for all my prowess, I cannot break through to the ships single-handed — “the more there are of us the better.” The fight jams there, poised on the battlements, and Homer reaches for the two most domestic similes he owns: the armies contest the wall like two farmers with measuring-rods quarreling over a boundary strip in a shared field, and the battle hangs level like the scales of an honest working woman weighing wool, balancing them carefully to win a pitiful wage for her children. A war for all the ships in the world, measured in yarn.

Then Zeus pays out the glory he promised. Hector springs at the wall shouting “up, Trojans, break the wall of the Argives, and fling fire upon their ships,” and lays hold of the stone standing outside the gates — thick at one end, pointed at the other, a stone two of the best men in a town could hardly lever onto a wagon “as men now are.” He lifts it alone, the way a shepherd carries a fleece in one hand, because Zeus makes it light for him; strides in close so the blow will gain force; and drives it into the middle of the double doors with his whole weight behind it. Both hinges break. The bars give. The doors fly open, one this way and one that, and Hector leaps through the gap “with a face as dark as that of flying night,” bronze flashing, a spear in each hand, eyes like fire. None but a god could have withstood him, Homer says, and no god tries. The Trojans pour over the wall and through the gates, and the Greeks run for their ships in “uproar and confusion.” The wall that Poseidon will one day wash into the sea has already failed at the only job it ever had. And somewhere in the crowd coming through the gate is Polydamas — the man who was right, following the man who was backed.

Last one

The gate falls to one man and a stone that two of the best men “as men now are” could hardly lever onto a wagon. Why does the book close on that image?

Both distractors are true, and neither is the point. Zeus does lighten the stone — but Homer gives the stride, the leaned-in weight, the burst hinges, and the face “as dark as flying night” to Hector alone; the god subsidizes the act without performing it. And the rhyme in option c is real: gate and wall, man and rivers, tonight and afterward. But the reason this image ends the book is the confrontation it stages. The wall is everything collective in the poem — surveyed, staked, buttressed, barred, built by an army and condemned by a committee of gods — and what actually breaks it is one mortal body at maximum output. “As men now are” is the flash-forward’s voice returning: by the narrator’s day the wall is sand and men of that size are gone, and the only thing still standing is the sentence describing them. Polydamas, meanwhile, was right twice and heard once — the eagle is through the gate, still carrying the snake.

TLDR

The Trojans press against the Greek wall and ditch. Polydamas reads a bad omen and counsels caution, Hector rejects him, Sarpedon attacks the gate, and Hector smashes the wall open with a stone.

Who’s who

Hector

Commander of Troy

He takes good advice at the trench, rejects better advice at the omen, and ends the book lifting a stone two men couldn’t budge — leaping through the smashed gate with a face, Homer says, as dark as flying night.

Polydamas

Hector’s counselor

The man whose job is saying the unwelcome thing. Right about the trench, right about the omen, and heard exactly once — he names the cost of dissent before he pays it.

Sarpedon

King of Lycia, son of Zeus

He asks Glaucus why Lycia gives them the best seats and the brimming cups, and answers: therefore we fight in front. Then he tears the battlement out of the wall with his bare hands.

Glaucus

Lycian captain

Sarpedon’s second, back from the armor swap of Book Six. Teucer’s arrow finds his bare shoulder and he slips down covertly — wounded, and still managing what the enemy might say.

Ajax

Bulwark of the Greeks

Son of Telamon. He walks the wall handing out fair words and sharp ones, kills Epicles with a stone few men could lift, and bulls Sarpedon back from the breach.

Teucer

Archer, Ajax’s half-brother

The best bow in the Greek camp. One arrow stops Glaucus on the wall; another hits Sarpedon’s shield-strap — and only a father’s intervention keeps it from being famous.

Asius

The man who wouldn’t dismount

Every other captain leaves his chariot at the trench; he drives his at an open gate, meets two Lapithae fighting like wild boars, and calls Zeus a liar. The poem has already scheduled his death.

Zeus

King of the gods

He promised Hector glory and pays it out all book: the dust-wind, the lightened stone, the son saved at the shield-strap. What the promise doesn’t cover, Hector has not asked.

Poseidon

God of the sea

He appears only in the future tense: trident in hand, turning eight rivers against the Greek wall for nine days until the beach is smooth sand and the wall is a story.

Apollo

God of the silver bow

Poseidon’s partner in the demolition to come — he gathers the mouths of the rivers from Ida and aims them at the wall the Greeks built without asking.

Achilles

Offstage, decisive

The wall stands, Homer says, only so long as he keeps nursing his anger. His absence is the reason the Trojans are at the gates at all.

Patroclus

In the tent, for now

The book opens with him dressing Eurypylus’s wound, out of the fight. The poem is keeping him parked exactly where his story will need him.
