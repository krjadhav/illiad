The Iliad, retold

Book 2

Book Two, after Samuel Butler’s translation — a lying dream, a stampede for the ships, and the longest list in literature.

Everyone at Troy is asleep except Zeus. He’s awake because he owes a debt: he promised Thetis, at the end of Book One, that the Greeks would lose until they missed Achilles. Which leaves him an engineering problem — how do you make an army start losing without lifting a finger? The solution he lands on tells you everything about how power works in this poem: he sends a lie. Homer calls it the Lying Dream, without embarrassment, and Zeus gives it its script directly: go tell Agamemnon the gods are finally unanimous and Troy falls today.

The dream is well engineered. It doesn’t arrive as a shining god — that would invite questions. It arrives wearing the face of Nestor, the counselor Agamemnon trusts above everyone. The message authenticates itself by the face that delivers it. Four thousand years on, phishing has not improved on this design.

Pause — check yourself

The lying dream doesn’t appear as a god. It appears as Nestor. What’s the exploit?

Agamemnon can’t verify the message, so he verifies the messenger — and the messenger is forged. Trust is a shortcut, and every shortcut is an attack surface. Con men, forged letters, and phishing emails have run on this exact principle ever since.

Agamemnon wakes up convinced. Notice what he’s convinced of: exactly the thing he most wants to be true. He recounts the dream to his council of elders, and the real Nestor endorses it with the most revealing argument in the chapter: if any other man had reported this dream, we’d call it a lie — but the man who saw it is our foremost man, so arm the troops. Read that twice. Rank substitutes for evidence, stated as explicit policy, by the wisest person in the room.

Then Agamemnon adds a clever flourish of his own — the kind of idea that only occurs to a leader who’s been assured he can’t lose. Before committing to battle, he’ll test morale. He will tell the army to give up and sail home; his officers, pre-briefed, will hold the line; presumably the men roar their refusal and march out doubly committed. He’s so certain of the ending that he scripts it.

The army hears “sail home” and runs for the ships. Not walks — runs. Homer gives it two similes, waves under a storm wind and a wheat field flattened by a gust, and then the details: dust rising, launch channels being cleared, props already coming out from under the hulls. Minutes from departure. Nine years of “honored to serve” evaporate at the first official permission to say otherwise. That’s what compliance looks like when you’ve been mistaking it for loyalty: every man privately done with the war, each assuming he was the only one, until the boss himself said the forbidden sentence and the private consensus went public all at once.

Pause — check yourself

Agamemnon says “let’s go home” expecting a roar of defiance. He gets a stampede. What did he misread?

The dream said arm the troops; the test was Agamemnon’s own invention, built on the assumption that obedient men must be willing men. But preferences people are afraid to voice don’t disappear — they wait. The moment the man at the top says the forbidden thing himself, everyone discovers everyone else agrees, and the dam goes in seconds. Every collapsing regime, and more than a few companies, finds this out the same way.

It takes goddesses to stop it. Hera — who has more invested in this war than anyone fighting it — sees the ships about to launch and sends Athena down with the argument that will run through the whole day: shall we really let Troy keep Helen, after so many Greeks died far from home for her sake? Athena doesn’t address the army; armies can’t hear. She picks one man. She finds Odysseus standing still beside his ship, hands at his sides, sick about the whole thing — and tells him to go turn it around, man by man.

Odysseus drops his cloak and goes to borrow the one object that matters: Agamemnon’s sceptre — forged by Hephaestus, handed down god to king to king until it reached the house of Atreus. Legitimacy, in this world, is a physical object, and Odysseus makes sure he’s holding it. Then he works the beach, and Homer reports his method without blinking. To every king and chief: courteous words — surely you understood the king was only testing us; stand to your post. To every commoner still shouting: the sceptre across his back — sit down and listen to your betters; we cannot all be kings here. Two messages, two protocols, sorted by rank. The poem doesn’t apologize for this any more than it apologized for the prize-women of Book One. It just shows you the machine running.

Then comes Thersites. Homer spends a loving paragraph establishing that he is the ugliest man who came to Troy — bandy-legged, lame, shoulders hunched over his chest, a pointed head with a few hairs on it — and that he’s a commoner with an uncontrollable mouth. While everyone else sits, he keeps yelling at Agamemnon: your tents are stuffed with bronze and women we hand you first from every town we sack; what more do you want? And Achilles, a far better man than you, you robbed to his face. Check that speech against Book One: it is Achilles’s own argument, point for point. When Achilles made it, a goddess came down to negotiate with him. When Thersites makes it, Odysseus whips him with the sceptre until he bleeds and sits down crying — and the army, the same army that was sprinting for the ships an hour ago, bursts out laughing and agrees it’s the best thing Odysseus has ever done.

Pause — check yourself

Thersites makes, almost point for point, the argument Achilles made in Book One. Achilles got a goddess sent to negotiate; Thersites gets whipped and laughed at. Why?

The content is identical; only the speaker’s status changed. From the best warrior alive, the complaint was a crisis that reached Olympus. From an ugly nobody, it’s insolence to be beaten out in public. Homer even stacks the deck — making Thersites grotesque so the crowd’s laughter feels natural — and then lets you notice, uncomfortably, that nobody ever answered the argument. They answered the man.

With the crowd warmed up, Odysseus gives the real speech, and it’s masterly precisely because it contains no new information. Going home empty-handed after nine years, he says, is shame we’d carry forever. And remember Aulis — the day we sailed: the serpent that ate eight sparrow chicks and then their mother, nine in all, and Calchas reading the omen for us — nine years of war, and the city in the tenth. We are in year nine. Leaving now means quitting the night before the prophecy pays out. It’s sunk costs converted into an investment on the verge of maturity, and the army that was launching ships this morning roars until the hulls ring.

Nestor follows with the organizational fix: reform the army by tribes and clans, so men fight beside people who know them — and so that when a unit hangs back, you can see exactly which one. Accountability through visibility. Agamemnon, delighted, wishes aloud for ten Nestors — and then says the most interesting sentence in the book, almost in passing: this feud with Achilles is wrecking us, and “I was the first to offend.” Alone, unprompted, cost-free — an actual admission of fault. It changes nothing today. File it away; the whole epic is about what it will eventually cost to say that sentence when it counts.

Pause — check yourself

At dawn the army was running for the ships. Two speeches later it’s roaring for war. What actually turned it around?

Odysseus and Nestor add zero facts to what every soldier knew at sunrise. What they change is the frame: nine sunk years stop being a reason to leave and become an investment one year from paying out, quitting becomes shame instead of relief, and the new tribe-and-clan structure means every man now fights in front of people who know his name. Crowds don’t run on information. They run on incentives and stories about themselves.

They sacrifice a bull to seal it. Agamemnon prays over it: let the sun not set before I’ve burned Priam’s palace and torn my sword through Hector’s shirt. Homer, deadpan: Zeus accepted the sacrifice — and kept piling on the toil. The god takes the meat and declines the request. The transaction model of piety, honestly priced. One warm detail survives the ceremony: Menelaus turns up to his brother’s sacrifice uninvited, because he knows how much Agamemnon has on his hands. Homer notices small loyalties as carefully as large betrayals.

Then the muster, and Homer empties his entire stock of similes to convey the scale — a forest fire seen from far off, flocks of geese and cranes screaming over a fen, flies swarming a milk pail in spring — and then the poem does the strangest thing in it. It stops. For three hundred lines, the story halts while Homer names every contingent in the army: the Catalogue of Ships. Fifty ships of Boeotians, and the two dozen towns they came from. Forty of Phocians. Twelve from Salamis under Ajax. Nireus of Syme, the handsomest man at Troy after Achilles, with exactly three ships — “a man of no substance.” Even the small get counted.

Modern readers skip the catalogue. I think that’s a mistake — not because the names matter now, but because of what the list is for. Ask what this war actually pays. Not land; they’re leaving when it’s over. Loot, some, and we’ve seen who gets first pick. For most of these men it pays exactly one thing: your name and your town, in the permanent record, spoken forever. The catalogue is the paycheck. And buried in the middle sits the entry that makes the whole day sting: fifty ships from Phthia, the Myrmidons of Achilles — not fighting, because their leader is still by the sea, still furious over Briseis. Their horses stand around champing celery; the men throw discs on the shore. Ajax gets the poem’s most backhanded superlative: best of them all — while Achilles sits out.

Last one

Homer halts the story for three hundred lines to name every captain, home town, and ship count. Why is the Catalogue there?

There are no pensions at Troy. For most of these men there’s no Helen and no grudge — the war pays in memory, and a line in the permanent song is what a town’s fifty ships buy. Homer prices it honestly: Nireus, three ships, “a man of no substance,” still gets his name spoken. Forever, as it turned out — you just read it.

Meanwhile Zeus sends Iris, fast as wind, to Troy — where old Priam is holding assembly at the gates as if it were peacetime. She arrives wearing the face of a Trojan sentry (gods almost never introduce themselves in this poem; masks are standard procedure) and delivers the news: I’ve seen many battles, but never a host like the one crossing the plain right now — thick as leaves, thick as sand. Hector doesn’t debate it. He recognizes the goddess, breaks up the assembly mid-sentence, throws open the gates, and has the whole city under arms by the mound men call Batieia. The Trojans get their own catalogue — shorter, and in more languages: Dardanians under Aeneas, Lycians under Sarpedon and Glaucus, allies from half of Asia, some captains getting one line and a quiet note that they will not be going home.

So Book Two ends with both armies finally in motion — the thing nine years of siege failed to produce, arranged in a single day. Count what it took: one divine lie, one botched loyalty test, one stampede, one beaten commoner, two speeches, and a list. And notice who got what he wanted: nobody on that plain. Only Zeus, who needed the armies to collide so the Greeks could start losing. The men march out believing today is the day they take Troy. The reader knows they’re marching so that Agamemnon can be taught the value of the man he insulted — a lesson he almost gave himself this morning, for free, in one sentence he wasn’t ready to act on.

Who’s who

Zeus

King of the gods

King of the gods. He owes Thetis a favor from Book One, and he pays it the cheapest way available: not with thunderbolts, but with a lie delivered while everyone sleeps.

Agamemnon

Commander-in-chief of the Greek army

Commander of the Greek forces. Handed a false promise of victory, he improvises a loyalty test on top of it — and finds out what his army actually wants.

Odysseus

King of Ithaca

The man who stops the stampede. He borrows Agamemnon’s sceptre, flatters the kings, beats the commoners, and talks the whole army back into the war. Butler calls him Ulysses.

Nestor

Old king of Pylos

The oldest and most trusted of the Greek counselors — which is exactly why the lying dream borrows his face. Trust is the vulnerability.

Thersites

The commoner who talks back

The ugliest man at Troy, and the only commoner with the nerve to say what everyone thinks. He makes Achilles’ argument at Achilles’ volume — with none of Achilles’ rank. It goes badly.

Hera

Queen of the gods

Queen of the gods, and the war’s most committed backer. When the Greeks are minutes from sailing home, she’s the one who notices.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

Sent down by Hera when the Greeks bolt for the ships. She doesn’t address the army — she picks one man, Odysseus, because turning a crowd around only takes the right one.

Iris

Messenger of the gods

Zeus’s messenger, fast as wind. She carries news of the advancing Greek host to Troy wearing a Trojan sentry’s face — the gods almost never appear as themselves.

Hector

Commander of Troy

Priam’s son and Troy’s best soldier. One warning, and he has the assembly dissolved, the gates open, and the whole city under arms. The Iliad’s other great man starts moving here.

Priam

King of Troy

The old king of Troy, holding assembly at the gates as if it were peacetime, while the greatest army ever assembled crosses his plain.

Achilles

The Greeks’ best fighter, on strike

Still by his ships, still furious about Briseis, still out of the war. His fifty ships get an entry in the catalogue anyway — with a note that no one is leading them.

Calchas

Prophet of the Greek army

The seer whose nine-sparrows omen at Aulis — nine years of war, victory in the tenth — becomes Odysseus’ best argument for staying.

Menelaus

King of Sparta

Agamemnon’s brother and the reason everyone is here. He shows up to the sacrifice uninvited, because he knows how busy his brother is. Homer notices small loyalties too.

Helen

The cause of the war

The woman the war is nominally about. In Book Two she exists only in rhetoric — “shall we leave Troy the glory of keeping Helen?” — which is how causes usually work by year nine.

Thetis

Sea goddess, Achilles’ mother

Achilles’ sea-goddess mother. Book Two is Zeus paying the debt he nodded to her in Book One — with the interest charged to everyone else.

Briseis

Achilles’ prize, taken

The woman Agamemnon seized in Book One. She appears here only as the reason fifty ships’ worth of soldiers throw discs on the beach instead of fighting.

Hephaestus

Smith of the gods

The smith of the gods. His contribution to Book Two was made generations ago: the sceptre Agamemnon leans on. Legitimacy, like most infrastructure, was built by someone long dead.
