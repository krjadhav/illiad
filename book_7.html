The Iliad, retold

Book 7

Book Seven, after Samuel Butler’s translation — two gods stage a duel, Hector and Ajax part like friends, Paris refuses to return Helen, and the Greeks build a wall that makes a god jealous.

Book Seven is a single day, and by the end of it the war has not moved an inch. The duel is a draw. The peace offer is refused. The dead are burned, the wall goes up, and both armies throw a feast. What the day produces isn’t progress — it’s machinery. Watch how much of it gets invented in twenty-four hours: a challenge protocol, a lottery, a truce, a burial schedule, a fortification — and watch who each piece of machinery actually serves. This is the book where the war stops being an event and becomes an institution.

It opens with Hector and Paris coming back through the gates together, and Homer’s simile for their arrival tells you the state of the army: they are as welcome as a breeze to rowers who have “laboured at their oars till they are faint with toil.” The rank and file are spent, and the sight of two rested princes reads to them as rescue. Each prince promptly kills a man — even Paris, when he actually shows up, fights — and the Trojans begin making such havoc of the Greeks that Athena comes darting down from Olympus to stop it.

Apollo intercepts her at the oak tree, and the negotiation between these two — who are on opposite sides of everything — takes about four lines. He wants Troy to win; she wants Troy erased; and they agree instantly on the one thing that matters to either of them today, which is stopping the general battle. Let’s have Hector challenge some Greek to single combat, he proposes. So be it, she says. Then the two of them perch on Zeus’s high oak in the likeness of vultures, “proud of their men,” to watch. The management of this war cannot agree on its outcome, but it converts an afternoon of anonymous slaughter into a show without a single objection — and its first act as producers is to take box seats. Helenus, Priam’s son and the best of the bird-readers, overhears the divine plan and pitches it to his brother with the insider information still attached: challenge their best man — I have heard the gods, and “the hour of your doom is not yet come.” Hector is glad. Of course he’s glad; he’s just been told the one thing no fighter ever gets to know. He grasps his spear by the middle, and both armies sit down like the sea darkening under a west wind.

Now listen to the terms of the challenge, because they are mostly not about fighting. If I lose, Hector says, take my armor, but send my body home for the fire. If your man loses, I’ll hang his armor in Apollo’s temple — but I’ll return the body, and you can raise him a mound by the wide waters of the Hellespont. And then the clause he actually cares about: some day a sailor passing that mound will say, this is the monument of one who was slain long ago by mighty Hector — “thus will one say, and my fame shall not be lost.” He is drafting the inscription on his opponent’s tomb before the fight, and the inscription is about himself. In this economy even the loser’s grave is advertising space, and the winner claims it in advance.

What follows is one of the most honest silences in the poem. The Greek princes sit there “ashamed to decline the challenge, yet fearing to accept it” — the honor system caught between its two engines, which for once cancel exactly. The man the silence squeezes hardest is Menelaus, because the war is his; he gets up, calls them all women, and starts strapping on armor to fight a man everyone present knows would kill him. Butler says so flatly: your life would have ended there, had the princes not sprung on you and checked you. It’s Agamemnon who catches his brother by the right hand and says the thing rank exists to make sayable: you are mad; Hector is far stronger than you; even the best of us — the one sulking at the ships — shrank from meeting him in battle. Menelaus lets himself be persuaded, and his squires strip the armor off him “gladly” — Homer’s small word for how relieved everyone is when an honorable exit finally opens. Shame drove a man toward certain death; hierarchy handed him a way back that cost no face. Both systems worked exactly as designed. Only one of them is allowed to say true things out loud.

Then Nestor does what he always does: tells a story about his own youth — how he alone dared the giant Ereuthalion when every other man “quaked and quailed,” and killed him — and lands it as a taunt: you, the foremost of the whole host, have no stomach for Hector. Nine men leap up at once, Agamemnon first, then Diomedes, then seven more down the roster to Odysseus. And now the army has the opposite problem, so Nestor invents the machine for it: cast lots. Each man marks his token and drops it into Agamemnon’s helmet, and the army prays over the shake — not thy will be done but a shortlist: let it fall to Ajax, or Diomedes, or the king of rich Mycene himself. Out jumps “the very lot which they wanted.” Ajax sees his mark and is glad.

Pause — check yourself

Hector’s challenge meets a long silence — princes “ashamed to decline, yet fearing to accept.” Nestor’s taunt gets nine men on their feet, and then the army draws lots from a helmet. Why lots, when everyone knows who the strongest candidates are?

The piety reading has the surface facts — they do pray over the shake — but listen to the prayer: not “choose rightly” but a shortlist, Ajax or Diomedes or Agamemnon. That’s a crowd telling the raffle which ticket to pull. The fairness option pattern-matches the feast’s equal shares — but that same feast ends with Agamemnon carving Ajax the long slice of the loin, because this culture never stops ranking; it hides the ranking only where ranking would be explosive. Which is what the lots are for. The shame already produced the courage; the lottery is how a group of status rivals makes a selection none of them could survive making out loud.

Ajax arms and comes on like the war god himself, smiling grimly, huge spear in hand, behind a shield of seven oxhides and an eighth layer of bronze — and Homer stops mid-menace to credit the tradesman who built it, Tychius, “by far the best worker in leather.” The Trojans tremble in every limb, and “the heart even of Hector beat quickly” — but he “could not now retreat,” the poem notes, “for he had been the challenger.” The theater he built binds its author first. The fight itself: spears punch through shields, Hector takes a gash in the neck and fights on, and then it escalates to rocks — Hector makes the bronze ring with a boulder, and Ajax answers with “a millstone of a rock” that caves Hector’s shield inward and flattens him beneath it. Apollo stands him back up at once — the producer protecting his lead. They are drawing swords when the heralds arrive, Talthybius from the Greek side and Idaeus from Troy, and part them with staves: you are both dear to Zeus, and night is falling, and “the behests of night may not be well gainsaid.” The duel to the death is called on account of darkness. Even single combat, it turns out, has business hours.

What happens next is the real heart of the book. Ajax, ahead on points, insists on protocol: let Hector say it first — he issued the challenge. The winner will not accept an exit that could read as his own idea. And Hector rises to it with a speech worth reading twice. You are strong and skilled beyond any Achaean, he tells Ajax; let us stop for today and fight again “till heaven decide between us”; and let us exchange presents, so that men will say — and here he scripts the audience’s line, exactly as he scripted the sailor’s — “they fought with might and main, but were reconciled and parted in friendship.” He hands over a silver-studded sword; Ajax hands back a purple girdle. Then each walks to his own side, and both sides celebrate a win: the Trojans receive Hector “as one that had been saved beyond their hopes,” and the Greeks bring Ajax to Agamemnon “elated with victory.” A fight structured so both fighters could win it — that’s not a failure of the duel; that’s the product. The men of the ranks, who resume dying anonymously tomorrow, are offered no such structure.

Pause — check yourself

Fought to a standstill at nightfall, Hector proposes gifts, “that it may be said… ‘they fought with might and main, but were reconciled and parted in friendship.’” What are the sword and the girdle actually for?

The Book Six reading is the strong distractor because the scene rhymes with Glaucus and Diomedes on purpose. But xenia bound those two before they were born — an inherited obligation with a whole network behind it — and it took them out of each other’s wars for good. Hector and Ajax have no prior tie and no future exemption; they say in so many words they’ll fight again “till heaven decide between us.” What they exchange isn’t obligation, it’s publicity: Hector scripts the audience’s exact line, the way he scripted the sailor’s over the mound. The respect may well be real — that’s what makes the machine run. The draw produces two heroes and zero casualties at the top, both armies celebrate a victory that night, and the men whose deaths resume in the morning get neither swords nor sentences.

That night the Greek officers eat. Agamemnon sacrifices a five-year-old bull, and “every man had his full and equal share, so that all were satisfied” — and then, in the same breath, Agamemnon gives Ajax the long slices cut down the loin, “as a mark of special honour.” Equal shares, plus one visible exception: the Greek social order served on a plate. If you’ve been reading since Book One you know this army does arithmetic on portions the way accountants do on ledgers — the whole poem started over a division of spoils. Tonight the arithmetic holds, because the exception matches the day’s deeds, which is all the men have ever asked of it. Then Nestor, “whose counsel was ever truest,” stands up over the wine and founds two institutions in one speech: tomorrow, a pause to gather and burn the dead, with a single barrow raised over all of them in common — and hard by it, while we’re at it, a high wall with gates for the chariots and a deep trench outside. The funeral and the fortification on the same work order. Grief, scheduled; engineering, attached.

Across the plain Troy holds its own council, and Homer gives its mood in four words: “angry and full of discord.” Antenor stands and says the whole unsayable thing at once: give Argive Helen back, and her wealth with her; we are fighting in violation of our solemn covenants, and we shall not prosper till we do it. Note that this is not a fringe voice shouting from the back — he is a senior counselor saying it to Priam’s face, and the only man who rises to answer him is the one who would have to pay. Paris’s reply is the most revealing negotiation in the poem. First the insult — heaven has robbed you of your reason — and then the offer, stated plainly: I will not give up the woman; but the wealth I brought home with her from Argos I will restore, “and will add yet further of my own.” Everything fungible, plus interest. Not the one item whose return would mean standing in front of Troy and admitting the theft. Gold costs Paris nothing he can feel. Returning Helen costs him face, and face is the one currency he has never once agreed to spend — nine years of other men’s sons have been cheaper. Priam, “peer of gods in council,” does not overrule his son. He packages him: at dawn Idaeus will carry the offer to the ships, and with it a request for a truce to burn the dead. The refusal of peace, gift-wrapped in decency.

Pause — check yourself

Antenor moves in open council to return Helen and her wealth. Paris refuses the woman but offers every bit of the treasure — plus more from his own fortune. What does the shape of that counter-offer tell you?

The sunk-cost reading fails on the scene’s own evidence: Troy is not captured by its losses. The council is “angry and full of discord,” a senior elder proposes surrender to the king’s face, and the only man who objects is the one who would pay in humiliation. The city knows the price of peace and can afford it; what it lacks is an institution able to force one prince. The third option isn’t wrong that the Greeks are past bargaining — Diomedes proves that a page later — but it mistakes the offer’s audience. Paris isn’t probing the enemy; he’s buying off his own city, converting “I keep her” into terms an angry assembly can swallow. The counter-offer maps the whole war: thousands die for an asset whose holder will pay anything except the admission.

Idaeus delivers the message at the stern of Agamemnon’s ship, and slips in an edit you could miss at reading speed: all the treasure Paris carried home to Troy — “would that he had sooner perished” — he will restore. A Trojan herald, mid-message, in front of the enemy, wishing his own prince had died on the voyage. That is what Troy’s civil service thinks of the war it administers. The Greeks hold their peace until Diomedes gives the answer the army wants: take neither the treasure nor Helen herself, “for even a child may see that the doom of the Trojans is at hand.” The ranks roar their applause. Nine years in, they are past bargaining; the asking price is the city. Agamemnon relays the refusal — and then grants the other request without a beat of hesitation: as for the dead, burn them; “when men are once dead there should be no grudging them the rites of fire.” He lifts his sceptre to the gods as witness. Every covenant in this poem has been broken so far except the ones about corpses.

The burial morning is the quietest writing in the book. The sun climbs fresh out of the deep currents of Oceanus, the two armies meet in the middle of the plain, and the men “could hardly recognise their dead” until they had washed the clotted gore from them — weeping as they worked, lifting the bodies onto wagons. Priam has forbidden his people to wail aloud, so the Trojans heap their dead on the pyre “sadly and silently” and go back to the city. The Achaeans heap their dead sadly and silently and go back to their ships. Homer writes the two sentences almost word for word the same, and the repetition is the point: at the pyres you cannot tell the armies apart. Then the appointment ends, because it was always an appointment, and the schedule says fight anew.

Pause — check yourself

For one morning the armies mingle — washing gore from faces to learn which corpse is whose, weeping — then burn the dead and go back to their lines to “fight anew, till heaven decide.” What is the poem doing with the truce?

The last-law reading is true as far as it goes — Agamemnon grants the burning as a matter of course — but if reverence were the message, Homer wouldn’t file it under logistics: Nestor proposes the funeral and the fortification in a single speech, with oxen, mules, and a construction plan, and the mourners’ barrow becomes the wall’s first course by morning. The exit reading mistakes smoothness for hope: nobody hesitates when the appointment ends, and cooperation that frictionless isn’t the war breaking down — it’s the war functioning. That is the morning’s real horror, and it is administrative: mourning has a time slot, the mirrored sentences erase the difference between Trojan and Greek exactly where the war insists there is one, and both sides keep the schedule. An institution that can pause itself is an institution that intends to continue.

In the twilight before dawn, the Greeks build. Barrow, wall, towers, gates sized for chariots, a deep trench planted with stakes — a season of civil engineering compressed into one night shift. And the reaction Homer records is not Trojan alarm. It’s divine envy. The gods sit beside Zeus and marvel at the great work, and Poseidon — who once built the walls of Troy itself for old Laomedon, and was cheated of his wages — files a complaint worth quoting almost whole: what mortal will ever again “take the gods into his counsel”? The Achaeans have walled their ships “without offering hecatombs to the gods,” and “the fame of this wall will reach as far as dawn itself,” and men will forget the one he and Apollo labored over. Read what he is actually saying. Not: the wall threatens us. Not even, really: impiety must be punished. But: mortals have manufactured fame without paying us for it, and no god is anywhere in the story. Zeus’s reply is a masterpiece of executive soothing — you are the earthquake; your fame reaches as far as dawn itself — followed by the fix: when the Greeks sail home, shatter the wall, roll it into the sea, spread the beach with sand again. That is the actual settlement between gods and men in this poem. Mortals are permitted to make great things. They are not permitted to make things that last.

Last one

Poseidon’s complaint about the Greek wall isn’t that it’s dangerous. It’s that it went up “without offering hecatombs,” and its fame “will reach as far as dawn itself.” What is a god afraid of here?

Both wrong options quote Poseidon accurately, which is why they tempt — the precedent worry is his opening line and the old wall is his closing one. The tell is Zeus’s answer, which addresses neither: he orders no punishment and promises the old wall nothing. He does exactly two things — soothes Poseidon’s status (“your fame reaches as far as dawn itself,” handing him back his own superlative) and licenses him to erase the wall once the ships sail. Flatter the ego, cap the competitor: Zeus treats the complaint as market anxiety because that is what it is. Underneath sits the poem’s standing settlement with mortality — humans may make great things, and the gods make sure no great thing of theirs lasts — with the one loophole Helen named in Book Six: the songs outlive the sand.

The book ends on commerce and thunder. Ships come in from Lemnos loaded with wine, and the Achaeans pay for it “some with bronze, some with iron, some with hides, some with whole heifers, and some again with captives” — human beings listed fifth in a price schedule, after the livestock, without a flicker of comment. Both armies feast the whole night. And all night Zeus thunders, “boding them ill,” until men on both sides are pouring wine onto the ground before they dare drink. Which is where the day of institutions ends: a draw certified as two victories, a peace priced and refused, the dead filed under a common mound, a wall so good the gods are jealous — and over all of it, the one authority no protocol reaches, growling in the dark that none of this machinery will save anyone. Then they sleep.

Who’s who

Hector

Commander of Troy

He stakes the duel before he knows his opponent, and when Ajax terrifies him he cannot retreat — the challenger is bound by his own theater. He loses on points and leaves with a co-authored legend and a purple girdle.

Ajax

Bulwark of the Achaeans

Son of Telamon, bred in Salamis, behind a shield of seven oxhides. The lot the whole army prayed for falls to him; he wins the exchange, accepts nightfall, and walks off with Hector’s silver-studded sword.

Apollo

God of the silver bow

He wants Troy winning and the slaughter paused, so he pitches Athena a duel and watches from the oak in the shape of a vulture. When Ajax’s stone flattens Hector, he stands him back up at once.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

She darts down from Olympus to blunt the Trojan surge, agrees to Apollo’s show in a sentence, and takes her box seat beside him in the likeness of a vulture, proud of her men.

Agamemnon

Commander-in-chief of the Greek army

He saves his brother with plain arithmetic, carves Ajax the hero’s portion at the feast, and grants the enemy fire for their dead without a grudge. Rank, for once, doing its job.

Menelaus

King of Sparta

The silence squeezes him hardest, so he arms to fight a duel everyone knows he would lose — until Agamemnon catches his right hand and says so out loud. His squires strip the armor off him gladly.

Nestor

Old king of Pylos

He shames nine volunteers onto their feet with a story about his own youth, then invents the lottery — and, over dinner, the wall. The oldest man in the army keeps founding its institutions.

Paris

Prince of Troy

He opens the book killing a man and closes it vetoing peace: all the treasure back, plus his own on top — but not Helen. Gold costs him nothing he can feel; giving her back would cost him face.

Antenor

Trojan elder

He stands up in council and says it plainly: give Helen back, with her wealth; we broke our oaths and will not prosper. Paris answers that heaven has robbed him of his reason.

Priam

King of Troy

He cannot overrule his son, so he packages him: the refusal goes out at dawn gift-wrapped in a truce for burning the dead — and at the pyre he forbids his people to wail aloud.

Helen

The price not paid

She never speaks in this book. She is discussed — the asset Antenor moves to return, Paris refuses to release, and Diomedes announces the Greeks no longer even want.

Diomedes

Son of Tydeus

One sentence this book, and it ends the negotiation: take neither the treasure nor Helen, for even a child may see that the doom of the Trojans is at hand.

Idaeus

Herald of Troy

He parts the duelists at nightfall, then carries the offer to the ships and edits it in transit — would that Paris had sooner perished — a herald reviewing his own prince mid-message.

Helenus

Trojan prince and augur

He overhears the gods planning the duel and delivers the pitch to Hector with the insider information still attached: the hour of your doom is not yet come.

Odysseus

King of Ithaca

One of the nine who stand when Nestor stops talking. This book he is a marked lot in a helmet, and it stays there.

Zeus

King of the gods

Every oath and lot is sworn toward him, and he answers none of it until night — then thunders till both feasting armies pour their wine on the ground, afraid to drink.

Poseidon

Lord of the earthquake

The Greeks wall their ships without offering a single hecatomb, and he takes it as a hostile product launch: the fame of this wall will reach as far as dawn itself.
