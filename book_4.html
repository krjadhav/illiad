The Iliad, retold

Book 4

Book Four, after Samuel Butler’s translation — the gods trade cities over drinks, one arrow ends the peace, and the dying start getting names.

Book Three ended with the war settled on the ground. Book Four opens where wars are actually decided: Olympus, golden floor, Hebe pouring nectar, the gods looking down at Troy the way you’d look at a screen. Zeus starts the conversation, and Homer tells us his intent outright: he spoke “so as to provoke” Hera. Menelaus, he observes, has two goddesses in his corner — and they sit up here watching, while Aphrodite actually shows up and saves her man. And, by the way: the victory was Menelaus’s, fair and square. So — shall we call it? Helen goes home, and Priam’s city stands.

Athena fumes in silence. Hera can’t: is my labor to go for nothing — the sweat I sweated, my tired horses, mustering armies against Priam and his children? Zeus’s reply starts as genuine bewilderment: what have they ever done to you? Must you get inside the walls and eat Priam raw, his sons and all? And then he folds — but files a receipt: have your way, so this doesn’t become a quarrel between us. But someday I’ll want a city that you love, and when I do, you will not stop me. Because understand what you’re asking: of every city under the sun and stars, none honored me like Troy. My altar there never lacked its feast. Perfect piety — and he’s signing it away.

Hera’s counter-offer is the coldest paragraph in the epic. Take my three favorites, she says — Argos, Sparta, Mycenae. Sack them whenever they displease you. I won’t defend them and I won’t care. These are her own worshipping cities, hers the way Troy is Zeus’s, and she puts them on the table like chips — generations of prayers marked down to the price of one grudge. Deal done, she moves straight to logistics: send Athena down, and have her contrive that the Trojans are the first to break their oaths.

Pause — check yourself

Hera offers Zeus three of her own favorite cities — Argos, Sparta, Mycenae — to sack whenever he pleases, as the price of destroying Troy. What has just been established?

Zeus says it himself: no city under the stars honored him like Troy — his altar never lacked its feast. Perfect piety, traded anyway; and Hera prices her own worshippers without a flinch. The bluff reading fails because Zeus treats the offer as binding — that’s what his “someday I’ll want a city of yours” receipt is for. The theology here is exact and bleak: the gods’ economy runs on their politics, not your devotion.

Notice the actual instruction: not “restart the war” but make the Trojans break the truce first. First is everything. If the fighting merely resumes, it’s the gods’ mess. If Troy shoots first, the Greeks get a righteous cause, the oaths fight on their side, and every man who swore over the wine that morning becomes a stakeholder in Troy’s punishment. This is blame engineering, ordered at the executive level. Athena — “already eager,” Homer notes — drops out of the sky like a meteor trailing fire, and both armies look up and read the same omen both ways: either war again, or peace. A sign that supports any conclusion is not information. It’s a mirror.

She finds her man: Pandarus, a captain with a famous bow. Wearing a comrade’s face, she makes the pitch, and it’s engineered to the target: dare to shoot Menelaus now, and you’ll win glory before all Troy — and gifts, rich ones, from Paris, who’d pay anything to watch his rival climb onto a funeral pyre. Take aim; vow a hecatomb of lambs to Apollo for when you’re home. Homer’s verdict comes in four words: “his fool’s heart was persuaded.”

Then, before the treachery, the poem stops to admire the instrument — a bow built from the horns of a wild ibex Pandarus stalked and shot himself, sixteen palms of horn, worked smooth by a craftsman, tipped with gold. Homer gives the weapon a biography before the crime the way he’ll soon give the dead one after. Pandarus shoots from behind a wall of his comrades’ shields — treachery, with logistics support — and the string sings, and the arrow, Homer says, flies gladly.

Pause — check yourself

Athena doesn’t seize Pandarus’s hands and force the shot. What’s her method?

The disguise and the vow are both really there — that’s what makes them tempting answers — but neither is the engine. Nothing about the pitch is a command; it’s a payoff schedule: fame before all Troy, Paris’s gratitude. Homer’s “fool’s heart was persuaded” puts the agency in Pandarus, where it has to stay: the entire assignment was to make the blame Trojan, and blame only sticks to a man who chose. Gods in this poem rarely compel. They present the temptation you were already shaped to take.

Now watch Athena’s double game, because it’s the whole chapter in one gesture. She talked the arrow into the air; now she deflects it — “as a mother whisks a fly from her sleeping child.” Homer’s tenderest simile, spent on the goddess who arranged the shot. She steers the point into the one spot on Menelaus where belt buckle, cuirass, and mail overlap; it punches through all three and just breaks the skin. Blood runs down his thighs, and Homer reaches for a craftsman’s image: purple dye laid on ivory. She needed a wound, not a corpse — enough blood to kill the peace, not enough to cost the Greeks a king. Precision-guided outrage.

Agamemnon sees the blood and comes apart — in two directions at once, and Homer refuses to sort them. He holds his brother’s hand and groans: I’ve been the death of you, pledging this covenant. Real grief, real guilt. And in the same unbroken speech, the other track: if you die, the army sails home, Helen stays, your bones rot at Troy with the work unfinished — and some braggart Trojan will leap on your tomb and laugh: so much for the vengeance of Agamemnon. May the earth swallow me first. Brother-love and brand-management, fused in one sentence. Menelaus, bleeding, is the calm one: relax — the belt caught it. The surgeon Machaon is fetched, draws the arrow, bending the barbs back, and dresses the wound with drugs from a recipe Chiron gave his father — battlefield medicine already three generations deep.

Pause — check yourself

Holding his wounded brother’s hand, Agamemnon grieves — and in the same speech imagines the army sailing home and a Trojan braggart dancing on Menelaus’s tomb. What is Homer doing?

The cynical reading fails on the physical details — the held hand, the sighs, the comrades moaning with him are real grief, not staging. But the optics-calculation is equally real, and Homer writes both in one breath without refereeing. That’s his standard move with motive: Achilles’ honor and greed, Hera’s justice and spite. People rarely know which of their reasons is load-bearing. (And yes — Agamemnon’s homecoming is a catastrophe, which gives option c its shine. But this speech isn’t built as prophecy; it’s built as panic.)

The Trojans re-arm, and Agamemnon has his best chapter in the poem: the walking tour. He leaves the chariot behind — a commander on foot, so the men see him — and calibrates his approach to each captain like a man who’s been studying his roster for nine years. To Idomeneus: perks — at my table your cup stands full when everyone else drinks by allowance; be the man you’re proud to be. To the two Ajaxes: nothing at all — men like you need no orders; give me ten more and Troy falls today. To Nestor: respect — and Nestor earns it on the spot with the poem’s first piece of formation design: chariots in front, solid infantry behind, and the cowards driven into the middle, “that they might fight whether they would or no.” Don’t exhort the unwilling. Build a formation where unwillingness has no exit.

Then the misfires, which are just as instructive. He needles Odysseus: first man at my feasts, and here you stand waiting for others to start the fighting. Odysseus goes off like a spring — what are you talking about? Watch the father of Telemachus in the front line; you’re talking idly — and Agamemnon instantly smiles, retracts everything, no fault to find, we’re of one mind, may the gods cancel whatever I just said. A commander who knows to the millimeter which of his subordinates he can afford to insult.

Diomedes gets the full treatment: your father Tydeus once beat fifty ambushers nearly single-handed — best man of his generation, or so they say, I never met him — and his son talks better and fights worse. Diomedes says nothing. His lieutenant Sthenelus takes the bait instead — we’re better than our fathers; we actually took Thebes — and Diomedes turns and silences his own defender: quiet, friend. It’s no shame for Agamemnon to spur us on — his is the glory if we take the city, and his the shame if we lose it. Then he vaults off the chariot in a clang of bronze that Homer says would have frightened a brave man. He’s the first person in four books the insult machine simply cannot reach.

Pause — check yourself

Agamemnon goads Diomedes with his father’s legend. Diomedes says nothing — and silences Sthenelus for talking back on his behalf. Why swallow it?

Option a is the Book Two lesson, and it’s the trap: Diomedes isn’t afraid — he explains his reasoning aloud, calmly, on the spot. He has internalized the incentive structure so completely there’s nothing left to resent: Agamemnon bears the glory or the shame, so Agamemnon gets to spur. Compare Achilles, who heard a milder provocation and reached for a sword. Professionalism, in any century, is mostly this — knowing which provocations are part of the machinery.

Then the armies close, and Homer gives the sound of each. The Greeks march in a silence so total he marvels at it — you’d swear that enormous host had no tongues, discipline made audible. The Trojans are a din like thousands of ewes bleating for their lambs, a camp of many languages answering itself. Ares drives one side, Athena the other, and with them the staff officers of every battle since: Panic, Rout, and Strife, whose head reaches heaven while her feet still walk the earth. Shields meet shields, and “the earth ran red.”

And now the poem begins a practice it will keep for twenty books: every death gets a name, and an invoice. Echepolus, first man killed in the Iliad, takes a spear through the helmet. Elephenor is killed in the ribs as he stoops to drag off the body and strip the armor — cut down through the gap his own greed opened. Then Ajax kills Simoeisius, and Homer stops the battle to tell you who that was: his mother bore him on the banks of the river Simois, coming down from Ida where she’d gone with her parents to see the flocks — that’s why he carried the river’s name — and “he did not live to repay his parents for his rearing.” He falls like a poplar felled by a wheelwright, left seasoning by the water to become the rim of someone’s chariot wheel. A life, converted to material. Homer invented him thirty seconds ago, and you grieve him anyway.

The killing chains outward. Antiphus throws at Ajax, misses, and kills Leucus — Odysseus’s friend. Odysseus, furious, strides up the line and kills Democoon — a bastard son of Priam who, the poem pauses to note, used to look after his father’s mares at Abydos. Even the one-line dead get a job. The Trojan front gives ground, and Apollo himself shouts from the citadel the sharpest morale line of the war: press them — their skins are not stone, not iron — and Achilles is not even fighting. The most powerful man on the battlefield is two miles away, sitting by his ships, and both sides plan around him like weather. Near the close, Diores’ ankle is crushed by a jagged stone and Peirous spears him as he falls; Thoas kills Peirous in turn; and the two captains, Epean and Thracian, lie dead beside each other while their men fight over the bodies.

Homer ends the book on a strange, pointed sentence: no man could have made light of that fighting — provided he could walk through it untouched, unwounded, with Athena leading him by the hand and turning the spears aside. Which is, of course, a description of you. The reader is the man with divine protection, strolling the field, safe. The poet spends the whole book making battle magnificent and then, in one line, points at your seat. The truce is dead, the first names are in the ledger, and the machine the gods built this morning will now run for twenty books. Book Five belongs to Diomedes.

Last one

Simoeisius gets a riverbank birth, parents he’ll never repay, and a poplar simile — thirty seconds after Homer invents him, ten seconds before Ajax kills him. Why do the dead get biographies?

The biography exists to be destroyed: you meet the mother by the river precisely so the spear takes something you now hold. The poplar simile completes the accounting — a grown life felled and seasoned into material for someone else’s vehicle. Option c is the respectable classroom reading, but it gets the direction backwards: the details are domestic, not heroic — flocks, parents, an unpaid debt of rearing — and they make the killer no grander. Homer does this hundreds of times, and it stays the poem’s moral engine: war’s cost is only ever visible one priced life at a time.

TLDR

The gods decide Troy must still fall, so Athena tricks Pandarus into breaking the truce with an arrow at Menelaus. The wound is minor, but the oath is broken, and open battle begins again.

Who’s who

Zeus

King of the gods

King of the gods. He knows Menelaus won fair, floats peace, gets overruled at his own table — and trades away the most pious city he has, booking a future grudge as compensation.

Hera

Queen of the gods

Queen of the gods. She offers Zeus three of her own favorite cities to destroy whenever he likes, as the price of Troy. Her war, bought and paid for.

Athena

Goddess of wisdom and war

In Book Four she’s the whole machine: she finds the fool, sells him the shot, then deflects the arrow herself — she needs a wound, not a corpse.

Pandarus

The archer who breaks the truce

An allied captain with a magnificent ibex-horn bow and, in Homer’s words, a fool’s heart. One arrow from him ends the truce — and any Trojan claim to the gods’ good will.

Menelaus

King of Sparta

Winner of yesterday’s duel, and today’s designated victim — shot under truce, saved by a belt buckle and the goddess who aimed the arrow at it.

Agamemnon

Commander-in-chief of the Greek army

Commander of the Greeks. His brother’s blood scares him into his best day of leadership: the walking tour where he calibrates praise, perks, and insult to each captain in turn.

Machaon

The army’s surgeon

Son of Asclepius and the Greeks’ surgeon. He draws the arrow, bends back the barbs, and dresses the wound with drugs from Chiron’s old recipe — medicine already generations deep.

Odysseus

King of Ithaca

Insulted as feast-first, fight-last, he flares — and Agamemnon retracts instantly. Later he kills Democoon in rage for a fallen friend. Butler calls him Ulysses.

Diomedes

Son of Tydeus, the coming man

Goaded with his father’s legend, he says nothing, silences his own defender, and fights — the one captain in four books the insult machine can’t reach. Book Five is his.

Nestor

Old king of Pylos

His formation puts chariots in front, solid infantry behind, and the cowards in the middle, where they must fight whether they will or no. Mechanism design, eight centuries BC.

Apollo

God of archery

He watches from Troy’s citadel and shouts the war’s sharpest morale line: their skins aren’t stone or iron — and Achilles isn’t even fighting.

Aphrodite

Goddess of love

Zeus’s exhibit A in the council: while Hera and Athena sat watching, she was the only god on the field actually saving her man.

Paris

Prince of Troy

The rescued man of Book Three, now a payer of bounties: Athena’s pitch to Pandarus is that Paris would reward Menelaus’s death handsomely. Butler calls him Alexandrus.

Achilles

The Greeks’ best fighter, on strike

Still by his ships, still out of the war — and now a weapon in absentia: Apollo rallies Troy on the news that the best of the Greeks isn’t fighting.

Helen

The settlement that almost happened

Zeus’s peace proposal would have sent her home and left Troy standing. The goddesses voted otherwise.

Priam

King of Troy

King of Troy. Zeus calls his city the most pious under the stars — in the same conversation where he signs it away.
